## Core concepts

### Roles and objectives of central banks

| Role | Detail |
| --- | --- |
| **Sole supplier of currency** | Monopoly on issuing legal tender |
| **Banker to the government and to banks** | Holds accounts, settles payments |
| **Lender of last resort** | Provides liquidity to solvent but illiquid banks in a crisis |
| **Regulator and supervisor** | Of the banking system, in many jurisdictions |
| **Conductor of monetary policy** | The primary function |
| **Manager of foreign exchange and gold reserves** | |

**The primary objective in most advanced economies is PRICE STABILITY** — typically an inflation
target around 2%. Some central banks have a **dual mandate** that adds maximum employment;
others have additional objectives covering financial stability, exchange rate stability, or growth.

> **Why not zero inflation?** A small positive target provides a buffer against **deflation**, which
> is far more damaging and harder to escape (it raises real debt burdens, encourages spending
> deferral, and cannot be countered by cutting nominal rates below zero by much). It also allows
> **real wages to adjust downward** without nominal wage cuts, which workers resist.

### The tools

| Tool | Mechanism |
| --- | --- |
| **Policy rate** | The rate at which the central bank lends to banks (or pays on reserves). **The primary tool** — it anchors the entire short end of the yield curve |
| **Open market operations** | Buying or selling government securities to add or drain reserves. Buying **injects** liquidity and lowers rates |
| **Reserve requirements** | The proportion of deposits banks must hold as reserves. Lowering them frees lending capacity. Rarely used actively in advanced economies now |
| **Quantitative easing (QE)** | Large-scale purchases of longer-dated government and other securities, used when the policy rate is at its **effective lower bound**. Aims to lower **long-term** rates directly |
| **Forward guidance** | Communicating the intended future path of policy, to influence expectations and therefore long rates today |

### The monetary transmission mechanism

**How a change in the policy rate reaches the real economy — five channels:**

```
POLICY RATE CHANGE
      │
      ├─→ 1. INTEREST RATE CHANNEL
      │      Short rates move → market rates and the yield curve shift →
      │      borrowing costs change → investment and consumption respond
      │
      ├─→ 2. ASSET PRICE CHANNEL
      │      Lower discount rates raise equity and property prices →
      │      WEALTH EFFECT → consumption rises
      │
      ├─→ 3. EXCHANGE RATE CHANNEL
      │      Lower rates reduce the currency's attractiveness → currency DEPRECIATES →
      │      exports more competitive, imports dearer → net exports rise, import prices rise
      │
      ├─→ 4. CREDIT / BANK LENDING CHANNEL
      │      Bank funding costs fall and collateral values rise → lending expands
      │
      └─→ 5. EXPECTATIONS CHANNEL
             Signals about future policy and inflation → affects wage and price setting today
```

> **The lags are long and variable.** The full effect of a rate change on inflation typically takes
> **12 to 24 months**. This is why central banks must act on **forecasts** rather than on current
> data — and why they are so often accused of acting too late or too much.

### Effects of expansionary and contractionary policy

| | **Expansionary** (cutting rates) | **Contractionary** (raising rates) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Economic growth | **Rises** (with a lag) | **Falls** |
| Inflation | **Rises** (with a lag) | **Falls** |
| Short-term rates | **Fall** | **Rise** |
| Long-term rates | Usually fall — but may **RISE** if inflation expectations increase | Usually fall at the long end if credibility is strong |
| The exchange rate | **Depreciates** | **Appreciates** |
| Asset prices | **Rise** | Fall |

> **The long-rate exception is worth understanding.** A rate cut by a central bank whose inflation
> credibility is weak can **raise** long-term yields, because investors demand more compensation for
> expected inflation. Conversely, a **credible tightening** can **lower** long yields, because it
> reassures markets that inflation will be controlled. This is why credibility is an asset — it
> means policy works with less action.

### Qualities of an effective central bank

| Quality | Why |
| --- | --- |
| **Independence** | Operational freedom from political pressure. Politicians face short electoral cycles and are tempted to stimulate before elections; monetary policy works over years |
| **Credibility** | If the market believes the target will be met, **expectations anchor** and the policy works with smaller rate changes |
| **Transparency** | Clear communication of objectives, reasoning, and forecasts makes policy predictable and reduces market disruption |

> **Credibility is the central bank's most valuable asset.** With **anchored** inflation
> expectations, a temporary price shock does not feed into wage demands, so it does not become
> persistent inflation — and the central bank need not respond aggressively. Without anchoring, the
> same shock triggers a wage-price spiral requiring a severe recession to break. **Credibility is
> slow to build and fast to lose.**

### Targeting regimes

| Regime | Mechanism | Trade-off |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Inflation targeting** | Set policy to achieve a stated inflation rate (usually ~2%) | **The dominant modern approach.** Transparent, accountable, anchors expectations |
| **Interest rate targeting** | Target a specific rate level | Simple, but the appropriate rate changes with conditions |
| **Exchange rate targeting** | Peg to a stable foreign currency | **Imports the anchor currency's credibility** — but **surrenders independent monetary policy** entirely |
| **Monetary aggregate targeting** | Target money supply growth | Largely abandoned: the relationship between money and inflation proved unstable |

> **The impossible trinity** is the key insight behind exchange rate targeting: a country cannot
> simultaneously have (1) a **fixed exchange rate**, (2) **free capital movement**, and (3) an
> **independent monetary policy**. It must give up one. A country pegging its currency with open
> capital markets has **no independent monetary policy** — it must follow the anchor country's rates.

### Limitations of monetary policy

| Limitation | Detail |
| --- | --- |
| **Long and variable lags** | 12–24 months to affect inflation, so policy must be set on forecasts |
| **The effective lower bound** | Nominal rates cannot fall far below zero, so conventional easing is exhausted in a deep recession |
| **The liquidity trap** | At very low rates, additional liquidity is hoarded rather than lent or spent — monetary policy "pushes on a string" |
| **Banks may not lend** | Central bank liquidity does not become credit if banks are capital-constrained or risk-averse |
| **Demand may not respond** | If households and firms are deleveraging, cheap credit does not induce borrowing |
| **Cannot address supply shocks** | Monetary policy works on demand. It cannot fix an energy shortage or a supply chain disruption — it can only suppress demand to match the reduced supply |
| **Blunt instrument** | It affects the entire economy uniformly; it cannot target a specific sector or region |
| **Distributional effects** | Low rates favour asset owners and borrowers over savers, with consequences for inequality |
| **Financial stability risks** | Prolonged low rates encourage a search for yield, leverage, and asset bubbles |

> **The supply shock limitation is the one that matters most in practice.** Faced with
> supply-driven inflation — an energy shock, a war, a pandemic disruption — a central bank has no
> tool that increases supply. It can only **reduce demand** until it matches the constrained supply,
> which means deliberately causing a slowdown to bring inflation down. This is why supply shocks
> pose such acute policy dilemmas.

### Interaction of monetary and fiscal policy

| Combination | Effect |
| --- | --- |
| **Both expansionary** | **Strong growth stimulus**; output rises sharply; **high inflation risk**; the mix leaves interest rates low and the currency weak |
| **Both contractionary** | **Sharp slowdown**; output falls; inflation falls; rates and the currency depend on relative intensity |
| **Fiscal expansionary, monetary contractionary** | **Output effect ambiguous.** Interest rates **rise** (fiscal borrowing plus tightening), which **crowds out private investment**. The public sector grows relative to the private. **Currency appreciates** |
| **Fiscal contractionary, monetary expansionary** | Output effect ambiguous. Interest rates **fall**, supporting private investment. The private sector grows relative to the public |

> **The crucial exam point: the combination determines the COMPOSITION of output, not just its
> level.** Loose fiscal with tight money produces high interest rates, a strong currency, a larger
> public sector, and crowded-out private investment. Tight fiscal with loose money produces low
> rates, a weak currency, and private-sector-led growth.
>
> Coordination matters: policies pulling in opposite directions can leave the level of output broadly
> unchanged while substantially altering who is doing the spending.

## Exam traps

> **Trap 1 — Assuming a rate cut always lowers long-term rates.** If **inflation credibility is
> weak**, a cut can **raise** long yields as investors demand inflation compensation. A credible
> tightening can **lower** them.

> **Trap 2 — Forgetting the impossible trinity.** A **fixed exchange rate with free capital
> movement** means **no independent monetary policy**.

> **Trap 3 — Thinking monetary policy can fix supply shocks.** It cannot increase supply. It can
> only **suppress demand** to match reduced supply — which means causing a slowdown deliberately.

> **Trap 4 — Missing that the policy mix changes composition, not just level.** Loose fiscal with
> tight money **crowds out private investment** and grows the public sector, even if total output is
> unchanged.

> **Trap 5 — Underestimating credibility.** With anchored expectations, a price shock does not
> become persistent inflation. **Credibility is slow to build and fast to lose.**

> **Trap 6 — Assuming central bank liquidity becomes credit.** Banks may be capital-constrained or
> risk-averse, and borrowers may be deleveraging. This is the **liquidity trap** / "pushing on a
> string".

> **Trap 7 — Ignoring the lags.** 12–24 months means policy is always set on **forecasts**, which is
> why criticism of central banks for acting late is partly unavoidable.

> **Trap 8 — Confusing the exchange rate direction.** **Expansionary policy → DEPRECIATION**
> (lower rates make the currency less attractive to hold).

## Self-check

*Closed book. Commit to an answer before opening the fold.*

**1.** Explain the five channels through which a policy rate cut reaches the real economy.

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**(1) Interest rate channel.** The policy rate anchors short-term market rates, which pull the whole yield curve down. **Borrowing costs fall** for households (mortgages, consumer credit) and firms (loans, bonds), so **investment and consumption rise**. This is the most direct channel.

**(2) Asset price channel.** Lower discount rates **raise the present value** of future cash flows, so equity and property prices rise. Households feeling wealthier **increase consumption** — the **wealth effect**. Firms with higher valuations can also raise capital more cheaply.

**(3) Exchange rate channel.** Lower domestic rates make the currency **less attractive** to hold, so capital flows out and the currency **depreciates**. Exports become more competitive and imports dearer, so **net exports rise** — and import prices rise, adding directly to inflation.

**(4) Credit / bank lending channel.** Banks' funding costs fall, and rising asset prices **increase collateral values**, so borrowers qualify for more credit. Bank lending expands. (This channel is blocked if banks are capital-constrained.)

**(5) Expectations channel.** The cut signals the central bank's assessment and intentions, influencing **wage and price setting today**. Forward guidance works purely through this channel — it changes long rates without any current action.

**The lags:** the full effect on inflation takes **12–24 months** and varies. This is why central banks must act on **forecasts**, and why they are routinely accused of being late.

</details>

**2.** Explain the impossible trinity and its implications for a small open economy.

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**The impossible trinity** (or trilemma): a country cannot simultaneously have all three of

1. A **fixed exchange rate**
2. **Free capital movement**
3. An **independent monetary policy**

It must give up one.

**Why:** suppose a country pegs its currency and allows free capital flows. If it tries to set rates **below** the anchor country's, capital flows out seeking the higher foreign return, selling the domestic currency and putting **downward pressure on the peg**. To defend it, the central bank must sell foreign reserves and raise rates back. The peg has taken the rate decision away from it.

**The three viable combinations:**
- **Fixed rate + free capital flows → no independent monetary policy.** Hong Kong's currency board; a eurozone member.
- **Fixed rate + independent policy → capital controls.** China historically.
- **Free capital flows + independent policy → floating exchange rate.** Most advanced economies.

**Implications for a small open economy:**
- Pegging **imports the anchor country's monetary policy** — which may be entirely wrong for domestic conditions. A commodity exporter pegged to a currency whose central bank is tightening for unrelated reasons will import that tightening into a downturn.
- But pegging also **imports credibility**, which can be decisive for a country with a history of high inflation. It is a trade of flexibility for anchoring.
- **Floating** preserves policy independence but means living with exchange rate volatility, which is costly for a small, trade-dependent economy.
- The peg is only as good as the **reserves and political will** to defend it — speculative attacks on unsustainable pegs are a recurring feature of financial history.

</details>

**3.** Why can't monetary policy address a supply shock, and what does a central bank actually do?

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**Because monetary policy operates on DEMAND, and a supply shock reduces SUPPLY.**

When an energy shortage, a war, a pandemic disruption, or a crop failure reduces the economy's productive capacity, inflation rises **and** output falls simultaneously — **stagflation**. The central bank has no tool that increases the supply of energy, semiconductors, or grain.

**The dilemma it faces:**
- **Tighten** to fight inflation → suppress demand → deepen the output contraction that is already underway
- **Ease** to support output → add demand to an economy that cannot supply more → worsen inflation

There is no combination that addresses both. The dual mandate is genuinely in conflict.

**What central banks actually do:**

**(1) Distinguish first-round from second-round effects.** A one-off rise in the price **level** from a supply shock is not persistent inflation. Central banks generally **look through** the direct effect.

**(2) But respond to second-round effects.** If workers bargain for higher wages to restore real income, and firms raise prices to restore margins, the one-off shock becomes a **wage-price spiral**. Central banks tighten to prevent this.

**(3) Rely on anchored expectations.** With **credible** expectations, workers and firms believe the shock is temporary and do not adjust wage and price setting — so the second round never happens and little tightening is needed. This is why **credibility is the central bank's most valuable asset**.

**(4) When expectations de-anchor, tighten hard.** Bringing inflation down from a wage-price spiral requires deliberately causing a recession — the Volcker experience of the early 1980s. That is the cost of losing credibility.

</details>

**4.** A government runs a large fiscal deficit while the central bank tightens sharply. What happens?

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**The output effect is ambiguous** — fiscal expansion adds demand while monetary tightening subtracts it, and which dominates depends on the relative magnitudes.

**But the effects on composition and prices are clear:**

**Interest rates rise sharply.** Both policies push the same way: the government's borrowing adds to demand for loanable funds while the central bank restricts supply. Rates rise by more than either policy alone would produce.

**Private investment is crowded out.** Higher rates make private projects uneconomic, while the government's borrowing is insensitive to rates. **The public sector grows relative to the private sector**, regardless of what happens to total output.

**The currency appreciates strongly.** High domestic rates attract capital inflows. This **worsens the trade balance** — exports become uncompetitive and imports cheaper — so the current account deteriorates.

**The historical example** is the United States in the early 1980s: large deficits combined with Volcker's disinflation produced very high real rates, a dramatically strong dollar, a severe manufacturing recession, and a large trade deficit.

**The key exam point:** the policy **mix determines the COMPOSITION of output**, not merely its level. The mirror image — tight fiscal with loose monetary — produces low rates, a weak currency, and **private-sector-led growth** with a smaller public sector. Two mixes can produce the same GDP with completely different economies underneath.

</details>
