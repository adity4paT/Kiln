## Core concepts

### The foreign exchange market

**Functions:** facilitating international trade and investment, providing a mechanism for hedging
currency risk, and enabling speculation and arbitrage that keeps prices consistent.

**Participants:** central banks, commercial and investment banks (the interbank market, which is the
core of the market), corporations, institutional investors, hedge funds, retail, and brokers.

**Key facts:** the market is **OTC**, operates **24 hours** across time zones, is the **largest and
most liquid** financial market in the world, and is dominated by a handful of major currency pairs.

### Quote conventions — get this right or nothing else works

```
A quote of P/B means:  the number of units of the PRICE currency (P)
                        required to buy ONE unit of the BASE currency (B)
```

In **USD/EUR = 1.10**: EUR is the **base**, USD is the **price**. One euro costs 1.10 dollars.

> **The curriculum convention is price currency / base currency**, with the **base currency second**.
> This is the reverse of how some market participants write it, so **read the question carefully**
> and identify the base currency before doing anything else. A misidentified base inverts every
> subsequent answer.

**Direct vs indirect quotes:** a **direct** quote expresses the **domestic** currency price of one
unit of foreign currency (from the US perspective, USD/EUR). An **indirect** quote is its reciprocal.

**Bid-ask:** the dealer **buys the base currency at the bid** and **sells it at the ask**. The ask
always exceeds the bid; the spread is the dealer's compensation.

### Computing percentage changes

```
% change in the BASE currency = (New rate − Old rate) / Old rate
```

> **The result applies to the BASE currency, not the price currency**, and the two are **not**
> symmetric. If the base currency appreciates 10%, the price currency does **not** depreciate 10%.
>
> To get the price currency's change, **invert both rates first**, then compute.

**Worked example.** USD/EUR moves from 1.10 to 1.20.

```
EUR (the base) change = (1.20 − 1.10)/1.10 = +9.09%   → the EUR APPRECIATED 9.09%

For the USD: invert both. EUR/USD moves from 1/1.10 = 0.9091 to 1/1.20 = 0.8333.
USD change = (0.8333 − 0.9091)/0.9091 = −8.33%        → the USD DEPRECIATED 8.33%
```

**+9.09% and −8.33% — not symmetric.** This asymmetry is tested directly and reliably.

### Nominal versus real exchange rates

The **nominal** rate is the quoted market rate. The **real** rate adjusts for relative price levels
and measures **relative purchasing power** — what actually matters for competitiveness.

```
Real exchange rate (P/B) = Nominal (P/B) × (CPI_base / CPI_price)
```

> **The practical reading:** a country whose **nominal** rate is stable but whose **inflation is
> higher** than its trading partners' is experiencing a **real appreciation** — its goods are
> becoming more expensive abroad and it is **losing competitiveness**, even though the exchange rate
> has not moved.
>
> This is why the real rate, not the nominal, determines trade flows over time.

### Exchange rate regimes

**Ordered from least to most flexible:**

| Regime | Mechanism |
| --- | --- |
| **Formal dollarisation** | Adopt a foreign currency outright. **No domestic monetary policy at all** |
| **Monetary union** | Share a currency with other members |
| **Currency board** | Domestic currency fully backed by reserves of the anchor currency at a fixed rate |
| **Fixed peg** | Pegged to a currency or basket, defended by intervention |
| **Target zone / crawling peg** | Pegged within a band, or adjusted on a schedule |
| **Managed float** | No announced target, but the central bank intervenes |
| **Independent float** | Market-determined, minimal intervention |

> **The impossible trinity governs the choice** (LM4): a country cannot have a **fixed exchange
> rate**, **free capital movement**, and **independent monetary policy** simultaneously. Moving
> toward the fixed end of the spectrum means surrendering monetary independence, unless capital
> controls are imposed.

**Why a country might choose a fixed regime:** to **import credibility** from the anchor currency
after a history of high inflation, to reduce exchange rate risk for a trade-dependent economy, and
to lower transaction costs.

**Why a country might float:** to retain **monetary policy independence** and to keep the exchange
rate as an **automatic adjustment mechanism** for external shocks.

### Exchange rate effects on trade and capital flows

**On trade:**

| | **Depreciation** | **Appreciation** |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Exports | More competitive → **rise** | Less competitive → fall |
| Imports | More expensive → **fall** | Cheaper → rise |
| Trade balance | **Improves** — eventually | Deteriorates |
| Inflation | **Rises** (import prices) | Falls |

> **The J-curve.** A depreciation **worsens** the trade balance before improving it. In the short
> run, contract prices and volumes are fixed, so the same volume of imports simply costs more in
> domestic currency — the balance **deteriorates**. Only as volumes adjust over months or quarters
> does the balance improve, tracing a **J** shape.
>
> The condition for eventual improvement is the **Marshall–Lerner condition**: the sum of the
> absolute values of the export and import demand **elasticities must exceed 1**. If demand is
> sufficiently inelastic, a depreciation never improves the balance.

**On capital flows:** higher domestic interest rates attract capital inflows, which put **upward**
pressure on the currency (LM4's exchange rate channel). Expectations of appreciation also attract
flows, which can be self-reinforcing.

### The balance of payments

```
Current account + Capital account + Financial account = 0
```

(Under the modern classification, the **capital account** is small — capital transfers and
non-produced assets — and the **financial account** records investment flows.)

| Account | Records |
| --- | --- |
| **Current account** | Trade in goods and services, primary income (investment income), secondary income (transfers) |
| **Financial account** | Direct investment, portfolio investment, reserve assets |

> **The identity is an accounting requirement, not an economic claim.** A country running a
> **current account deficit** must, by construction, be running a **financial account surplus** — it
> is **importing capital** to pay for importing goods. A country cannot consume more than it
> produces without someone abroad financing the difference.
>
> The analytical question is never whether the accounts balance (they must) but **how the deficit is
> financed**. Foreign **direct** investment is stable and productive; short-term **portfolio** flows
> are reversible and can leave abruptly — which is what turns a current account deficit into a
> currency crisis (Fixed Income LM15).

### Capital restrictions

**Objectives:**

| Objective | Detail |
| --- | --- |
| **Reduce volatility** | Limit destabilising hot money flows |
| **Maintain a fixed exchange rate** | The impossible trinity's third option — controls allow a peg **and** monetary independence |
| **Protect domestic industry** | Restrict foreign ownership in strategic sectors |
| **Prevent capital flight** | Particularly during a crisis |
| **Retain domestic savings** | Direct savings toward domestic investment |
| **Preserve monetary policy autonomy** | |

**Types:** limits on foreign ownership, restrictions on repatriating profits or capital, taxes on
inflows or outflows, minimum holding periods, and outright prohibitions.

**The costs:** reduced access to foreign capital and a higher cost of capital, reduced market
efficiency and liquidity, evasion and circumvention, deterred foreign direct investment, and damage
to investor confidence that can outlast the controls themselves.

> **The balanced view:** capital controls are widely regarded as second-best, but the case against
> them has softened. There is now broader acceptance — including at the IMF — that **temporary**
> controls on **short-term inflows** can be legitimate macroprudential tools for a small open
> economy facing destabilising hot money, particularly when the alternative is an unsustainable
> credit boom. **Controls on outflows imposed during a crisis** remain far more damaging, because
> they destroy the confidence on which future access depends.

## Exam traps

> **Trap 1 — Misidentifying the base currency.** In **P/B**, the **base is the second** currency.
> Getting this wrong inverts every subsequent answer. Write it down before calculating.

> **Trap 2 — Assuming percentage changes are symmetric.** They are **not**. If the base appreciates
> 9.09%, the price currency depreciates **8.33%**, not 9.09%. Invert both rates and recompute.

> **Trap 3 — Confusing nominal and real rates.** A **stable nominal rate with higher domestic
> inflation is a REAL APPRECIATION** — the country is **losing competitiveness** despite no
> exchange rate movement.

> **Trap 4 — Forgetting the J-curve.** A depreciation **worsens** the trade balance before improving
> it, because contract prices and volumes are fixed in the short run.

> **Trap 5 — Missing the Marshall–Lerner condition.** Improvement requires the sum of the absolute
> elasticities to **exceed 1**. With sufficiently inelastic demand, depreciation never helps.

> **Trap 6 — Treating the balance of payments identity as an economic result.** It is an
> **accounting requirement**. The analytical question is **how the deficit is financed**.

> **Trap 7 — Assuming all capital restrictions are equivalent.** **Temporary controls on short-term
> inflows** are now viewed as a legitimate macroprudential tool. **Controls on outflows during a
> crisis** are far more damaging to future access.

## Self-check

*Closed book. Commit to an answer before opening the fold.*

**1.** The USD/GBP rate moves from 1.25 to 1.32. Compute the percentage change for both currencies.

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**Identify the base first:** in **USD/GBP**, **GBP is the base** and USD is the price. The quote says one pound costs 1.25 dollars, rising to 1.32.

**GBP (the base currency):**
(1.32 − 1.25)/1.25 = 0.07/1.25 = **+5.60%** — the pound **appreciated** 5.60%

**USD (the price currency)** — invert both rates first:
GBP/USD moves from 1/1.25 = 0.8000 to 1/1.32 = 0.7576
(0.7576 − 0.8000)/0.8000 = −0.0424/0.8000 = **−5.30%** — the dollar **depreciated** 5.30%

**Note the asymmetry: +5.60% and −5.30%, not equal and opposite.** The denominators differ because the starting points differ. This asymmetry is tested reliably, and computing one change and flipping the sign is the standard error.

</details>

**2.** A country's nominal exchange rate against its main trading partner has been stable for three years, but its inflation has averaged 6% while its partner's averaged 2%. What has happened to its competitiveness?

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**It has lost competitiveness substantially — a real appreciation of roughly 12%.**

```
Real exchange rate = Nominal × (CPI_foreign / CPI_domestic)
```

With the **nominal rate unchanged**, the real rate is driven entirely by the inflation differential. Over three years:
- Domestic prices rose by 1.06³ = **1.191** (19.1%)
- Foreign prices rose by 1.02³ = **1.061** (6.1%)
- The domestic country's goods became **1.191/1.061 = 1.122** — about **12.2% more expensive** relative to the partner's

**The practical consequences:**
- **Exports are less competitive** — the country's goods cost 12% more in the partner's market
- **Imports are more attractive** — foreign goods are relatively cheaper domestically
- The **trade balance deteriorates**
- Pressure builds for a **nominal devaluation** to restore competitiveness

**This is exactly the dilemma of a fixed exchange rate regime with higher inflation than the anchor country.** The nominal peg prevents adjustment, so competitiveness erodes until either the peg breaks (a devaluation, usually abrupt and disorderly) or the country undergoes **internal devaluation** — falling domestic wages and prices, which is slow and produces high unemployment.

**The lesson: the real rate, not the nominal, determines trade flows.**

</details>

**3.** Explain the J-curve and the Marshall-Lerner condition.

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**The J-curve** describes the time path of the trade balance after a currency **depreciation**: it **worsens first**, then improves, tracing a J shape.

**Why it worsens initially:**
- **Contract prices are fixed** in the short run — existing trade is already priced
- **Volumes are fixed** — supply chains, orders, and consumption habits take time to change
- So the same **volume** of imports now costs **more** in domestic currency, while export revenue in domestic currency rises less (if exports are priced in domestic currency, revenue is unchanged)
- **Net effect: the trade balance deteriorates**

**Why it then improves:**
- Over months and quarters, **volumes adjust**: exports become cheaper abroad and volumes rise; imports become dearer and volumes fall
- The volume effect eventually **outweighs** the price effect, and the balance improves past its starting level

**The Marshall–Lerner condition** states when the eventual improvement occurs:

```
|export demand elasticity| + |import demand elasticity| > 1
```

If combined demand is sufficiently **inelastic** — the sum below 1 — volumes never adjust enough to offset the adverse price effect, and **the depreciation never improves the balance**. It simply makes imports more expensive permanently.

**When this matters practically:** a country importing **essential, inelastic** goods — energy, food, critical inputs with no domestic substitute — may fail the condition. For such a country, devaluation raises the import bill and delivers inflation without the compensating trade improvement, which is why devaluation is not a universal remedy for an external deficit.

</details>

**4.** A country runs a persistent current account deficit of 5% of GDP. Is this a problem?

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**It depends entirely on how it is financed.** The accounting identity guarantees a matching financial account surplus — the deficit **must** be financed by capital inflows. The analytical question is what kind.

**Potentially benign financing:**
- **Foreign direct investment** — stable, long-term, and typically productive. It builds capacity that generates future export earnings. A developing economy importing capital goods to industrialise, funded by FDI, is running a deficit for the right reason.
- **Long-term debt at sustainable rates** in the country's **own currency**
- A deficit driven by strong **investment** rather than by consumption or fiscal profligacy

**Dangerous financing:**
- **Short-term portfolio flows** — 'hot money' that can reverse abruptly. This is the classic emerging market crisis mechanism: the flows stop, the currency collapses, and the deficit must close violently through a recession
- **Short-term FOREIGN-CURRENCY debt** — the country cannot print what it owes, and a devaluation **raises the real burden** exactly as the crisis hits (Fixed Income LM15)
- Financing a **consumption boom** or a fiscal deficit, which builds no capacity to repay

**What to examine:**
- The **composition** of the financial account — FDI versus portfolio versus short-term debt
- **Foreign exchange reserves** relative to short-term external debt
- The **currency composition** of external liabilities
- Whether the deficit is **widening or stabilising**
- The **exchange rate regime** — a peg with a widening deficit is a crisis waiting to happen

**The example that settles it:** the United States has run large persistent current account deficits for decades without crisis, because it borrows in its **own currency** and attracts stable inflows. Countries running 5% deficits financed by short-term foreign-currency borrowing have repeatedly had crises. **The deficit is not the problem; the financing is.**

</details>
