## Core concepts

### The cooperation–competition framework

Geopolitics is analysed on two dimensions:

| Dimension | Question |
| --- | --- |
| **Cooperation** | To what extent do actors work together toward mutual benefit? |
| **Globalisation** | To what extent are actors integrated into global markets, supply chains, and institutions? |

Combining them gives four archetypes:

| | **High cooperation** | **Low cooperation** |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **High globalisation** | **Globalisation** — integrated and cooperative. The post-Cold War norm |
| **Low globalisation** | **Multilateralism** — cooperative but with limited integration | **Autarky** — self-sufficient and non-cooperative (low globalisation, low cooperation) |

And the fourth: **high globalisation with low cooperation** is **bilateralism / hegemony** —
integrated markets but transactional, power-based relationships rather than rules-based ones.

> **The useful insight is that cooperation and integration are independent.** Countries can be deeply
> economically integrated while politically hostile — which describes much of the current
> relationship between major economies, and is precisely what makes trade a usable instrument of
> coercion.

**Actors** are not only states: multinational corporations, supranational organisations, non-state
actors, and increasingly large technology firms all exercise geopolitical influence.

### Globalisation

**The increasing integration of economies through trade, capital flows, labour movement, and
technology.**

| Benefits | Costs |
| --- | --- |
| Lower consumer prices | **Distributional effects** — losses concentrated in specific industries and regions |
| Access to larger markets | **Supply chain fragility** — efficiency achieved at the cost of resilience |
| Efficiency from **comparative advantage** | Loss of domestic policy autonomy |
| Technology and knowledge transfer | Environmental and labour standard arbitrage |
| Faster growth in developing economies | Cultural and political backlash |

> **Deglobalisation and the resilience trade-off.** The efficiency case for globalisation assumes
> supply chains function. The pandemic and subsequent geopolitical tensions demonstrated that
> **efficiency and resilience trade off**: a single-source, just-in-time supply chain is the
> cheapest and the most fragile. The responses — **reshoring**, **nearshoring**, and
> **friendshoring** — deliberately accept higher costs for greater security of supply, and they are
> structurally inflationary.

### The international organisations

| Organisation | Function |
| --- | --- |
| **International Monetary Fund (IMF)** | Promotes **monetary cooperation and exchange rate stability**. Provides **balance of payments support** to countries in crisis, usually conditional on policy reform. Surveillance of member economies |
| **World Bank** | Promotes **long-term economic development and poverty reduction**. Provides **project finance**, technical assistance, and policy advice to developing economies |
| **World Trade Organization (WTO)** | Administers **trade agreements**, provides a forum for negotiation, and operates a **dispute resolution** mechanism. Successor to GATT |

> **The distinction most often confused: the IMF handles SHORT-TERM crisis and balance of payments
> support; the World Bank handles LONG-TERM development finance.** Both were established at Bretton
> Woods in 1944.

**Also worth recognising:** the Bank for International Settlements (the "central bank for central
banks"), the OECD, and regional development banks.

### Geopolitical risk

> **Geopolitical risk is the risk of political actions — by states or non-state actors — disrupting
> the peaceful course of international relations and affecting investments.**

**The taxonomy:**

| Type | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| **Event risk** | A **discrete, dated** event with a known timing — an election, a referendum, a scheduled trade negotiation |
| **Exogenous risk** | A **sudden, unanticipated** shock — an invasion, a coup, a terrorist attack |
| **Thematic risk** | A **slow-building, long-term** trend — climate change, demographic shifts, a gradual realignment of trading blocs |

**Assessing geopolitical risk along three dimensions:**

| Dimension | Question |
| --- | --- |
| **Likelihood** | How probable is it? |
| **Velocity** | How quickly will the impact be felt? **High-velocity** risks give no time to react |
| **Size and nature of the impact** | Discrete or broad? Reversible or permanent? |

> **Velocity is the dimension investors most often underweight.** A slow-building thematic risk can
> be hedged, diversified, or adapted to over years. A high-velocity exogenous shock — a border
> closure, a sanctions announcement, an asset freeze — arrives with **no opportunity to
> reposition**, and markets gap rather than trend.

### Tools of geopolitics

| Category | Tools |
| --- | --- |
| **National security** | Military action and the threat of it; espionage; defence alliances; cyber operations |
| **Economic** | **Tariffs**, quotas, **sanctions**, export controls, **capital controls**, asset freezes, currency manipulation, investment screening |
| **Financial** | Exclusion from payment systems, restrictions on reserve access, credit and aid conditionality |
| **Cultural / soft power** | Diplomacy, education exchange, media, development aid |

> **Economic and financial tools have become the dominant instruments of geopolitical competition**,
> precisely because deep economic integration makes them effective. Exclusion from the dominant
> payment system or the freezing of foreign reserves are extraordinarily powerful levers that exist
> **only because** of globalisation — which is the cooperation/globalisation framework in action.
>
> Their use has a second-order consequence: it accelerates efforts to build alternative payment and
> reserve arrangements, which is itself a deglobalising force.

### Impact on investments

| Channel | Effect |
| --- | --- |
| **Direct asset impact** | Expropriation, nationalisation, asset freezes, forced divestment |
| **Supply chain disruption** | Input shortages, production halts, forced supplier changes |
| **Commodity prices** | Energy and food are the most geopolitically sensitive markets |
| **Currency** | Capital flight, devaluation, the imposition of capital controls |
| **Market access** | Sanctions, delisting, and trading restrictions can leave assets unsellable at any price |
| **Risk premia** | Country risk premia widen, raising the cost of capital and lowering valuations |
| **Inflation** | Supply disruption and reshoring are structurally inflationary |
| **Sector effects** | Defence, energy, and commodities often benefit; import-dependent and export-oriented sectors suffer |

**Managing geopolitical risk in a portfolio:**

- **Diversification** across countries, currencies, and supply chains
- **Scenario analysis** rather than point forecasts — geopolitical outcomes are not normally
  distributed and are poorly suited to probability-weighted expected values
- **Hedging** with currency forwards, commodity exposure, and options
- Assessing **supply chain concentration** at the company level as part of fundamental analysis
- Understanding **jurisdiction** — where are the assets, and whose law governs them?
- Maintaining **liquidity**, because the ability to act matters more than the ability to predict

> **The honest analytical position:** geopolitical events are extremely difficult to forecast, and
> the record of expert prediction is poor. The useful work is **not prediction but preparation** —
> understanding **exposures**, running **scenarios**, and maintaining the flexibility to respond.
> An investor who knows which of their holdings are vulnerable to a specific disruption is far
> better placed than one who has a view on whether it will happen.

## Exam traps

> **Trap 1 — Confusing the IMF and the World Bank.** **IMF: short-term balance of payments crisis
> support.** **World Bank: long-term development finance.** Asked directly.

> **Trap 2 — Missing that cooperation and globalisation are independent dimensions.** High
> integration with low cooperation is a distinct and currently very relevant state.

> **Trap 3 — Underweighting velocity.** A high-velocity shock gives **no time to reposition** —
> markets gap rather than trend. This is what makes exogenous risk qualitatively different.

> **Trap 4 — Confusing the three risk types.** **Event = dated. Exogenous = sudden and
> unanticipated. Thematic = slow-building.**

> **Trap 5 — Treating globalisation as unambiguously good.** Its **distributional effects** and the
> **efficiency-versus-resilience** trade-off are real, and they drive the political backlash.

> **Trap 6 — Thinking geopolitical risk can be forecast.** The record of expert prediction is poor.
> The useful work is **understanding exposures and running scenarios**, not predicting events.

> **Trap 7 — Missing that economic tools depend on integration.** Sanctions and payment system
> exclusion are powerful **only because** of globalisation — and using them accelerates
> deglobalisation.

## Self-check

*Closed book. Commit to an answer before opening the fold.*

**1.** Distinguish the roles of the IMF, the World Bank, and the WTO.

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**International Monetary Fund (IMF)** — the **short-term** institution. It promotes international monetary cooperation and exchange rate stability, conducts surveillance of member economies, and provides **balance of payments support** to countries in financial crisis. Its lending is **conditional** on policy reform — the conditionality that makes IMF programmes politically contentious. Think: a country cannot meet its external obligations and needs emergency financing.

**World Bank** — the **long-term** institution. It finances **development projects** — infrastructure, health, education — in developing economies, and provides technical assistance and policy advice, with the objective of long-run growth and poverty reduction. Think: a country needs to build a power grid.

**World Trade Organization (WTO)** — the **trade rules** institution. It administers multilateral trade agreements, provides a forum for negotiating new ones, and — its most distinctive function — operates a **dispute resolution** mechanism through which members can challenge each other's trade practices and obtain binding rulings.

**The distinction most often confused is the first two.** Both were established at **Bretton Woods in 1944**, and both lend to developing countries — but the IMF addresses **short-term crises** and the World Bank funds **long-term development**.

</details>

**2.** Why is velocity the most important dimension of geopolitical risk for an investor?

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Because **velocity determines whether you can respond at all**.

**A low-velocity (thematic) risk** — demographic decline, climate transition, a gradual realignment of trading blocs — unfolds over years or decades. An investor can:
- **Reposition gradually** at reasonable prices
- **Hedge** incrementally
- **Adapt** the portfolio's structure
- Even **profit** from the transition by positioning early

Time converts a risk into a manageable exposure.

**A high-velocity (exogenous) risk** — an invasion, a sanctions announcement, a border closure, an asset freeze — arrives **overnight**, often without warning. There is:
- **No time to reposition**
- **No liquidity** — everyone wants out simultaneously and markets **gap** rather than trend. Prices move discontinuously through the level at which you would have sold
- **Possibly no market at all** — sanctioned or delisted assets can become **unsellable at any price**

**Why investors underweight it:** risk is typically assessed on **likelihood and magnitude**, both of which can be estimated for a slow-moving trend. Velocity does not enter the standard framework, yet it determines whether the other two matter.

**The practical implication:** high-velocity risks must be managed **ex ante** — through position sizing, jurisdictional diversification, and maintained liquidity — because there is no ex post response available. This is why exposure mapping beats forecasting.

</details>

**3.** Explain the efficiency-versus-resilience trade-off in supply chains and its investment implications.

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**The efficiency case for globalisation** assumes supply chains function. It produces genuinely lower costs through **comparative advantage** (produce where it is cheapest), **scale** (concentrate production), **just-in-time inventory** (minimal working capital), and **single-sourcing** (maximum volume discounts).

**Every one of those efficiency gains is a resilience loss.** Concentrating production in one location maximises scale **and** maximises the impact of a disruption there. Minimal inventory frees cash **and** removes the buffer against a supply interruption. Single-sourcing lowers unit cost **and** eliminates the alternative.

**The trade-off became visible** in the pandemic and subsequent geopolitical tensions: supply chains optimised over three decades for cost proved extremely fragile to disruptions that the optimisation had never priced.

**The responses, all of which deliberately accept higher costs:**
- **Reshoring** — bringing production home
- **Nearshoring** — moving it to a neighbouring country
- **Friendshoring** — moving it to politically aligned countries
- **Dual-sourcing** and **strategic inventory** buffers

**Investment implications:**
- **Structurally inflationary.** Reversing decades of cost optimisation raises the cost base permanently. This is a supply-side change that monetary policy cannot address (LM4).
- **Margin pressure** for companies that cannot pass the cost through — which depends on pricing power (Equity LM10).
- **Capital expenditure** rises as production capacity is duplicated.
- **Winners and losers by geography** — nearshoring destinations benefit; the displaced locations lose.
- **Supply chain concentration becomes a fundamental analysis item**, not an operational detail. Where are a company's critical inputs sourced, and what is the alternative?

</details>

**4.** Why have economic and financial tools become the dominant instruments of geopolitical competition?

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**Because deep economic integration makes them effective — which is the cooperation/globalisation framework in action.**

In a world of autarkic economies, an economic sanction is toothless: there is nothing to cut off. In a deeply integrated world, exclusion from trade, payment systems, or reserve access is devastating, and it can be imposed **without military action**.

**The specific tools and why they bite:**
- **Sanctions and export controls** — denying access to critical technology or markets
- **Exclusion from the dominant payment system** — a country cut off cannot settle international transactions at all
- **Freezing foreign exchange reserves** — reserves held in another jurisdiction can be immobilised, removing a central bank's ability to defend its currency
- **Investment screening and asset freezes**
- **Tariffs** — the traditional tool, now used as coercion rather than protection

**Why they are preferred over military tools:** lower cost, no casualties, fewer domestic political constraints, reversibility, and calibrated intensity.

**The second-order consequence, which is the analytically interesting part:** the more these tools are used, the stronger the incentive for affected countries to **build alternatives** — alternative payment systems, reserve diversification away from the dominant currency, and reduced dependence on sanctioned supply chains.

**So using the tools of integration as weapons accelerates deglobalisation.** The leverage exists only because of integration, and exercising it erodes the integration that created it. That is a genuine strategic dilemma rather than an incidental effect.

</details>
