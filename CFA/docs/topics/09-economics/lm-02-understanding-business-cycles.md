## Core concepts

### The business cycle

**Fluctuations in aggregate economic activity around its long-run trend.** Cycles are recurrent but
**not periodic** — they vary in length and amplitude and cannot be reliably timed.

| Phase | Characteristics |
| --- | --- |
| **Expansion / recovery** | Output rising, employment growing, confidence improving, inflation initially subdued then building |
| **Peak** | Growth slows, capacity constraints bind, inflation pressure highest, inventories begin rising involuntarily |
| **Contraction / recession** | Output falls, unemployment rises, confidence collapses, inflation moderates |
| **Trough** | The low point; conditions stop deteriorating and begin to stabilise |

**A recession** is conventionally defined as a significant decline in activity spread across the
economy, lasting more than a few months — often approximated as two consecutive quarters of negative
GDP growth, though official dating bodies use a broader set of indicators.

### How the sectors behave through the cycle

| Sector | Expansion | Contraction |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Resource use / capacity utilisation** | Rises toward full capacity; labour shortages emerge | Falls; spare capacity opens up |
| **Consumer activity** | Spending rises; **durables and discretionary** rise most | Durables and discretionary fall first and hardest; **staples hold up** |
| **Business activity** | **Capital investment rises**, hiring accelerates, inventories deliberately built | Investment cut first; hiring frozen then reversed; inventories liquidated |
| **Housing** | Starts and prices rise; construction employment grows | **Leads the downturn** — highly interest-rate sensitive |
| **External trade** | Imports rise with domestic demand; the trade balance often **deteriorates** | Imports fall; the balance often improves |

> **Inventories are the most informative cyclical indicator.** Near a peak, **involuntary** inventory
> accumulation occurs — goods are produced but not sold, so inventories rise while sales slow. That
> is a classic late-cycle signal. Near a trough, inventories have been drawn down so far that even a
> modest demand recovery triggers a disproportionate production rebound.
>
> This is the same signal as FSA LM6: finished goods rising faster than sales is a warning, at the
> firm level and at the economy level.

### The credit cycle

**Fluctuations in the availability and cost of credit**, which interact with but are distinct from
the business cycle.

| Phase | Characteristics |
| --- | --- |
| **Expansion** | Credit standards loosen, spreads narrow, leverage rises, lending volumes grow |
| **Peak** | Credit is freely available at very tight spreads; underwriting quality deteriorates; the most marginal borrowers obtain funding |
| **Contraction** | Standards tighten abruptly, spreads widen sharply, lending contracts, refinancing becomes difficult |
| **Trough** | Credit is scarce and expensive even for good borrowers; defaults peak |

> **The credit cycle amplifies the business cycle.** Easy credit finances investment and consumption,
> extending the expansion. When credit contracts, otherwise viable businesses fail because they
> cannot refinance — the mechanism turns a slowdown into a recession.
>
> **The credit cycle typically leads the business cycle**, and it has its own distinct rhythm: credit
> cycles tend to be **longer** than business cycles, and credit conditions often deteriorate before
> real activity does.

**Observable credit cycle indicators:** credit spreads (Fixed Income LM14), bank lending standards
surveys, loan growth, covenant quality (covenant-lite issuance rising is a late-cycle signal), and
leverage multiples on new deals.

### Economic indicators — the classification

**This is the most directly examinable content in the module.**

| Type | Meaning | Use |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Leading** | **Turn BEFORE** the economy | Forecasting |
| **Coincident** | Turn **WITH** the economy | Confirming the current phase |
| **Lagging** | **Turn AFTER** the economy | Confirming that a turn has occurred |

**Leading indicators:**

- **Stock market returns** (equities discount the future)
- **Building permits and housing starts**
- **New orders for consumer goods and capital goods**
- **Average weekly hours in manufacturing** (hours are adjusted before headcount)
- **Initial unemployment claims** (inverted)
- **The yield curve slope** — an **inverted** curve has historically been among the most reliable
  recession signals (Fixed Income LM9)
- Consumer expectations; supplier delivery times

**Coincident indicators:**

- **Industrial production**
- **Real personal income** less transfer payments
- **Manufacturing and trade sales**
- **Non-agricultural employment**

**Lagging indicators:**

- **Average duration of unemployment**
- **Inventory-to-sales ratio**
- **Change in unit labour costs**
- **Commercial and industrial loans outstanding**
- **The average prime rate**
- **Consumer credit outstanding relative to income**

> **The reasoning behind the classification is what to learn, not the list.** Decisions that are
> **easy to reverse and forward-looking** — ordering materials, adjusting hours, granting a building
> permit — move **first**. Decisions that are **costly and irreversible** — hiring permanent staff,
> committing capital — move **with** the cycle. Measures that are **consequences** of the cycle —
> the duration of unemployment, unit labour costs — move **last**.
>
> Apply that logic and you can classify an unfamiliar indicator without having memorised it.

**Why lagging indicators are useful despite lagging:** they **confirm** that a turn has genuinely
occurred. Leading indicators give false signals; a shift confirmed by coincident and then lagging
indicators is far more reliable.

### Theories of the business cycle

| School | Cause of cycles | Policy prescription |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Keynesian** | **Shifts in aggregate demand**, driven by volatile business expectations ("animal spirits"). Wages are sticky downward, so recessions persist | **Active fiscal policy** to restore demand |
| **Neo-Keynesian / New Keynesian** | Adds sticky prices as well as wages | Monetary and fiscal stabilisation |
| **Monetarist** | **Inappropriate monetary policy** — the money supply growing too fast or too slowly | **Steady, predictable money supply growth**; limited discretion |
| **Austrian** | **Excessive credit creation** and artificially low interest rates cause malinvestment; the bust is the necessary correction | Allow the correction; avoid artificial stimulus |
| **New Classical / Real Business Cycle** | **Real shocks** (technology, productivity) to which rational agents optimally adjust. Cycles are efficient responses | **No intervention** — policy cannot improve on the market outcome |

> **The Austrian view of the credit cycle is worth noting** because it connects directly to the
> credit cycle material above: the argument is that the **cycle originates in credit**, not in real
> activity, and that suppressing the correction merely stores up a larger one.

## Formulas to know cold

```
BUSINESS CYCLE PHASES
  EXPANSION → PEAK → CONTRACTION → TROUGH   (recurrent but NOT periodic)

SECTOR BEHAVIOUR
  Housing LEADS the downturn (highly interest-rate sensitive)
  Consumer DURABLES and discretionary fall first and hardest; staples hold up
  Business INVESTMENT is cut first in a downturn
  INVENTORIES: involuntary accumulation near the PEAK; depletion near the TROUGH

CREDIT CYCLE — amplifies and typically LEADS the business cycle; longer than it
  Indicators: credit spreads · bank lending standards · loan growth · covenant quality

ECONOMIC INDICATORS — the classification logic
  LEADING     turn BEFORE  — easily reversed, forward-looking decisions
              stock market · building permits · new orders · weekly hours
              · initial claims (inverted) · YIELD CURVE SLOPE
  COINCIDENT  turn WITH    — costly, committed decisions
              industrial production · real personal income · manufacturing sales
              · non-farm employment
  LAGGING     turn AFTER   — CONSEQUENCES of the cycle
              unemployment DURATION · inventory-to-sales · unit labour costs
              · C&I loans outstanding · prime rate

BUSINESS CYCLE THEORIES
  Keynesian — demand shifts, sticky wages → ACTIVE FISCAL policy
  Monetarist — inappropriate money growth → STEADY money supply rule
  Austrian — EXCESSIVE CREDIT causes malinvestment → allow the correction
  New Classical / RBC — real shocks, efficient adjustment → NO intervention
```

## Exam traps

> **Trap 1 — Misclassifying indicators.** Use the **logic**, not memorisation: easily reversed and
> forward-looking → **leading**; costly and committed → **coincident**; a consequence of the cycle →
> **lagging**.

> **Trap 2 — Inventory-to-sales ratio.** It is a **LAGGING** indicator. (Inventory *levels* behave
> cyclically, but the ratio confirms a turn after it has happened.)

> **Trap 3 — Unemployment duration vs initial claims.** **Initial claims** are **leading**;
> **average duration of unemployment** is **lagging**. Both concern unemployment; they sit at
> opposite ends.

> **Trap 4 — Treating cycles as periodic.** They are **recurrent but not periodic** — variable in
> length and amplitude, and not reliably timeable.

> **Trap 5 — Conflating the credit cycle with the business cycle.** The credit cycle is **distinct**,
> typically **longer**, and typically **leads**.

> **Trap 6 — Missing housing's leading role.** Housing is highly **interest-rate sensitive** and
> typically **leads the downturn**.

> **Trap 7 — Confusing the policy prescriptions.** **Keynesian → active fiscal.** **Monetarist →
> steady money rule.** **Austrian → allow the correction.** **New Classical → no intervention.**

## Self-check

*Closed book. Commit to an answer before opening the fold.*

**1.** Classify each as leading, coincident, or lagging, and justify from first principles: (a) building permits, (b) industrial production, (c) average duration of unemployment, (d) new orders for capital goods.

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**(a) Building permits — LEADING.** A permit is applied for months before construction begins and long before the economic activity appears in output. It is a cheap, forward-looking commitment that reflects expectations, and it can be abandoned. Housing is also highly interest-rate sensitive, so it responds early to policy.

**(b) Industrial production — COINCIDENT.** It *is* current economic activity, measured directly. It moves with the cycle by construction.

**(c) Average duration of unemployment — LAGGING.** It is a **consequence** of the cycle, and a slow-moving one. In a recovery, firms first restore hours and hire the recently unemployed; the long-term unemployed are hired last, so average duration keeps **rising** well after the trough.

**(d) New orders for capital goods — LEADING.** An order precedes production, delivery, and installation by months. Placing one is a forward-looking decision that can be cancelled, so it reflects current expectations about future demand.

**The general test:** is the indicator a **forward-looking, reversible decision** (leading), **current activity** (coincident), or a **consequence that resolves slowly** (lagging)?

</details>

**2.** Why does the credit cycle amplify the business cycle?

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Because **credit availability determines whether economic activity can be financed**, and it moves procyclically — expanding when times are good and contracting when they are not.

**In an expansion:**
- Credit standards **loosen**, spreads narrow, and leverage rises
- Cheap, abundant credit finances **investment, acquisitions, and consumption** that would not otherwise happen
- Asset prices rise, which increases collateral values, which supports **more** lending — a self-reinforcing loop
- The expansion runs **further and longer** than it otherwise would

**In a contraction:**
- Standards tighten **abruptly** — often more than the deterioration in fundamentals warrants
- **Otherwise viable businesses fail** because they cannot refinance maturing debt. This is the critical mechanism: the failure is caused by credit availability, not by the business's own economics
- Falling asset prices reduce collateral values, prompting **further** tightening
- Defaults rise, which makes lenders more cautious still
- The contraction is **deeper and longer** than the underlying shock required

**Why it leads:** credit conditions are set by lenders assessing **future** repayment capacity, so deterioration in underwriting appetite typically appears **before** real activity turns. Spreads widening and lending standards tightening are among the more reliable early warnings.

**The Austrian account** goes further: the credit cycle is not an amplifier but the **cause**. Artificially cheap credit produces **malinvestment** — projects that appear viable only at suppressed interest rates — and the bust is the necessary correction of those errors.

</details>

**3.** Describe how inventories behave through a business cycle and why they are informative.

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**Late expansion, approaching the peak:** **involuntary accumulation**. Firms continue producing at the rate that recent demand justified, but sales begin to slow. Goods pile up **unintentionally**. Inventories rise while sales growth decelerates — the inventory-to-sales ratio climbs.

**Early contraction:** firms recognise the overhang and **cut production sharply** — by more than the fall in demand, because they must work off the excess as well as match the lower run rate. This is why industrial production falls faster than final demand in a downturn.

**Late contraction, approaching the trough:** inventories have been **drawn down** to very low levels. Firms have cut production below the rate of sales for several quarters.

**Early recovery:** even a **modest** pickup in demand requires firms to produce both for current sales **and** to rebuild depleted stocks. Production rebounds **disproportionately** — which is why early-cycle industrial recoveries are often sharp.

**Why it is informative:** inventories are the **buffer between production and sales**, so they reveal the gap between what firms **expected** and what actually happened. An unexpected inventory build is direct evidence that demand has disappointed, ahead of any other confirmation.

**The firm-level parallel** (FSA LM6): finished goods rising faster than sales is a warning signal for a company, for exactly the same reason. The economy-wide version is the same diagnostic aggregated.

</details>

**4.** Contrast the Keynesian, Monetarist, and Austrian explanations of business cycles and their policy implications.

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**Keynesian.** Cycles are caused by **shifts in aggregate demand**, driven by volatile business and consumer expectations — Keynes's 'animal spirits'. Because **wages and prices are sticky downward**, a fall in demand produces unemployment rather than an immediate price adjustment, and the economy can remain below full employment for a prolonged period without self-correcting.
**Policy: active fiscal intervention** — government spending and tax cuts to restore demand directly.

**Monetarist.** Cycles are caused by **inappropriate monetary policy**. The private economy is fundamentally stable; instability comes from the money supply growing too fast (producing inflation and an unsustainable boom) or too slowly (producing contraction). Friedman's argument was that the Great Depression was caused by monetary contraction, not by demand failure.
**Policy: a steady, predictable money supply growth rule**, with limited central bank discretion — because policy acts with 'long and variable lags' and discretionary intervention often destabilises.

**Austrian.** Cycles originate in **excessive credit creation** and artificially suppressed interest rates. Cheap credit distorts the price signal that coordinates investment across time, producing **malinvestment** — projects that appear viable only at the artificial rate. The bust is not a failure but the **necessary correction** of those errors.
**Policy: allow the correction to proceed.** Stimulus merely prevents the liquidation of malinvestment and stores up a larger dislocation.

**The essential disagreement is about where instability originates:** in **private demand** (Keynesian), in **policy errors** (Monetarist), or in **credit itself** (Austrian) — and correspondingly, whether intervention is the remedy or the cause.

</details>
