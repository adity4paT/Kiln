## Core concepts

### Monetary versus fiscal policy

| | **Monetary policy** | **Fiscal policy** |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Conducted by** | The **central bank** | The **government** (treasury/finance ministry) |
| **Tools** | Policy rate, open market operations, reserve requirements, quantitative easing | **Government spending** and **taxation** |
| **Speed of implementation** | **Fast** — a committee can act within weeks | **Slow** — requires legislation |
| **Speed of impact** | **Slow** — long and variable lags | Can be **fast** once enacted (direct spending) |
| **Political independence** | Usually **independent** | **Inherently political** |
| **Reversibility** | Easy — raise the rate back | **Hard** — spending programmes and tax cuts are politically difficult to reverse |
| **Targeting** | **Blunt** — affects the whole economy | **Can be targeted** at sectors, regions, or income groups |
| **Main objective** | **Price stability** (and often employment) | Growth, redistribution, public goods provision |

> **The key asymmetry:** monetary policy is **quick to decide and slow to work**; fiscal policy is
> **slow to decide and quick to work**. Neither is well suited to fine-tuning a cycle.

### Objectives of fiscal policy

| Objective | Detail |
| --- | --- |
| **Influence aggregate demand** | Countercyclical stabilisation |
| **Redistribute income and wealth** | Progressive taxation and transfers |
| **Allocate resources** | Provide public goods and correct externalities |
| **Support employment and growth** | Infrastructure, education, research |

### The tools

**Spending tools:**

| Tool | Detail |
| --- | --- |
| **Transfer payments** | Unemployment benefits, pensions, welfare. Not counted in GDP directly, but they support consumption and are strongly **countercyclical** |
| **Current spending** | Ongoing government consumption — salaries, goods, services |
| **Capital spending** | Infrastructure investment. Raises **productive capacity** as well as demand, so it has the largest long-run effect — but the longest implementation lag |

**Revenue (tax) tools:**

| Tool | Detail |
| --- | --- |
| **Direct taxes** | Income, corporate, wealth, capital gains. Usually **progressive** |
| **Indirect taxes** | VAT, sales, excise duties. Usually **regressive**, but can be adjusted quickly and are hard to avoid |

**Desirable attributes of a tax:** simple, efficient (minimal distortion of economic decisions),
fair (horizontal and vertical equity), and revenue-sufficient.

> **The trade-off in tools:** **indirect taxes** can be changed almost immediately and take effect
> at once, but are regressive. **Capital spending** has the largest and most durable effect but the
> longest lag — infrastructure projects take years to design, approve, and build. **Transfer
> payments** are the fastest spending tool and are automatically countercyclical.

### Automatic stabilisers versus discretionary policy

| | Mechanism |
| --- | --- |
| **Automatic stabilisers** | Work **without any policy decision**. In a downturn, **tax receipts fall** (progressive rates mean falling incomes pay proportionally less) and **transfer payments rise** (more unemployment benefit claims). Both cushion demand automatically |
| **Discretionary policy** | Deliberate changes to spending or tax rates, requiring legislation |

> **Automatic stabilisers are the more effective countercyclical tool in practice**, precisely because
> they have **no recognition, action, or implementation lag**. They begin working the moment activity
> slows.

### Is the policy expansionary or contractionary?

> **Judge by the change in the deficit, not its level** — and ideally by the change in the
> **structural (cyclically adjusted) deficit**, which strips out the automatic stabilisers.

| Situation | Stance |
| --- | --- |
| Deficit **increasing** (beyond automatic stabilisers) | **Expansionary** |
| Deficit **decreasing** | **Contractionary** |
| A deficit that widens purely because of a recession | **Neutral** — the automatic stabilisers are working as designed |

The **structural deficit** is the deficit that would exist at full employment. A rising structural
deficit is genuine discretionary stimulus; a rising headline deficit in a recession may simply be
the stabilisers operating.

### The fiscal multiplier

```
Fiscal multiplier = 1 / [1 − MPC(1 − t)]

where  MPC = marginal propensity to consume
       t   = marginal tax rate
```

A higher **MPC** raises the multiplier (more of each unit of income is respent); a higher **tax
rate** lowers it (more leaks to government each round). Imports also leak, reducing the multiplier
in open economies.

> **Transfers to lower-income households have a higher multiplier** than tax cuts for high earners,
> because the MPC out of low incomes is higher — the money is spent rather than saved.

### Does the size of national debt relative to GDP matter?

**The exam wants both sides of this argument.**

**Arguments that it DOES matter:**

- **Higher debt requires higher future taxes**, which distort incentives and reduce growth
- **Crowding out** — government borrowing raises interest rates and displaces private investment
- **Default or inflation risk** if debt becomes unsustainable
- **Ricardian equivalence** — if households anticipate future tax rises, they save the stimulus
  rather than spending it, neutralising the policy entirely
- A large **interest burden** consumes fiscal capacity (Fixed Income LM15)
- Reduced **fiscal space** to respond to the next crisis

**Arguments that it does NOT matter, or matters less:**

- **Much of the debt is owed domestically** — "we owe it to ourselves", so it is a transfer within
  the economy rather than a claim on it
- If borrowing finances **productive investment** (infrastructure, education), the resulting growth
  services the debt
- In a **recession with idle resources**, deficit spending crowds nothing out — there is spare
  capacity
- A government borrowing in **its own currency** cannot be forced to default (Fixed Income LM15)
- The **debt-to-GDP ratio falls** if nominal growth exceeds the interest rate, without any repayment
- **Interest as a share of revenue** is the binding constraint, not the stock of debt — Japan's
  ratio far exceeds many defaulters'

> **The balanced conclusion:** what matters is not the **level** of debt but the **interest burden
> relative to revenue**, the **currency composition**, the **maturity profile**, and whether the
> borrowing financed something productive. This is exactly the sovereign credit framework from
> **Fixed Income LM15**.

### Difficulties of implementation

**The three lags — memorise these:**

| Lag | What it is |
| --- | --- |
| **Recognition lag** | Time to recognise that the economy needs intervention. Data arrives with a delay and is revised |
| **Action lag** | Time to legislate and enact. **The longest lag for fiscal policy** |
| **Impact lag** | Time for the policy to affect the economy |

**Other difficulties:**

- **Misreading the economy** — acting on data that is later revised
- **Political constraints** — tax rises and spending cuts are unpopular; stimulus is popular even
  when unwarranted
- **Crowding out** of private investment
- **Ricardian equivalence** partially offsetting the stimulus
- **Supply-side versus demand-side** confusion — fiscal stimulus cannot fix a supply constraint
- **Timing risk** — stimulus arriving after the recovery has begun is **procyclical**, amplifying
  rather than damping the cycle

> **The timing problem is the fundamental objection to discretionary fiscal policy.** By the time a
> recession is recognised, legislated for, and the money actually spent, the economy may already be
> recovering — so the stimulus arrives into an expansion, adding inflationary pressure rather than
> supporting demand. This is the central argument for relying on **automatic stabilisers** instead.

## Formulas to know cold

```
MONETARY vs FISCAL — the key asymmetry
  MONETARY: QUICK to decide, SLOW to work (long and variable lags)
  FISCAL:   SLOW to decide (legislation), QUICK to work once enacted

FISCAL TOOLS
  SPENDING: transfer payments (fastest, auto-countercyclical) · current · CAPITAL (largest
            long-run effect, LONGEST lag)
  REVENUE:  direct taxes (progressive) · indirect taxes (regressive, but FAST to change)

AUTOMATIC STABILISERS vs DISCRETIONARY
  Stabilisers work with NO lag: in a downturn tax receipts FALL and transfers RISE automatically.
  More effective in practice precisely because there is no recognition, action, or impact lag.

STANCE — judge by the CHANGE in the STRUCTURAL deficit, not the level
  Deficit increasing (beyond stabilisers) → EXPANSIONARY
  Deficit decreasing                       → CONTRACTIONARY
  Deficit widening purely from recession   → NEUTRAL (stabilisers working)

FISCAL MULTIPLIER
  Multiplier = 1 / [1 − MPC(1 − t)]
  Higher MPC → larger. Higher tax rate → smaller. Imports also leak.
  Transfers to LOW-income households have the HIGHEST multiplier (highest MPC).

THE THREE LAGS
  RECOGNITION (spotting the need) → ACTION (legislating — longest for fiscal) → IMPACT

DEBT/GDP — both sides
  MATTERS: future taxes distort · crowding out · default/inflation risk
           · Ricardian equivalence · interest burden · lost fiscal space
  MATTERS LESS: owed domestically · finances productive investment · idle resources in recession
           · own-currency borrowing · ratio falls if nominal growth > interest rate
  THE REAL TEST: interest / REVENUE, currency composition, maturity profile (Fixed Income LM15)
```

## Exam traps

> **Trap 1 — Judging fiscal stance by the deficit's level.** Judge by the **change** in the
> **structural (cyclically adjusted)** deficit. A deficit widening purely because of a recession is
> **neutral** — the automatic stabilisers are doing their job.

> **Trap 2 — Getting the speed asymmetry backwards.** **Monetary: quick to decide, slow to work.**
> **Fiscal: slow to decide, quick to work.**

> **Trap 3 — Forgetting automatic stabilisers.** They are the most effective countercyclical tool
> in practice because they have **no lags**.

> **Trap 4 — Multiplier direction.** A **higher MPC raises** the multiplier; a **higher tax rate
> lowers** it.

> **Trap 5 — Assuming all fiscal tools work at the same speed.** **Indirect taxes and transfers are
> fast; capital spending is very slow.**

> **Trap 6 — Missing Ricardian equivalence.** If households anticipate future tax rises, they
> **save** the stimulus and the policy is neutralised.

> **Trap 7 — Treating debt/GDP as decisive.** The binding constraint is **interest relative to
> revenue**, plus currency composition and maturity profile.

> **Trap 8 — Overlooking the timing risk.** Stimulus arriving after recovery has begun is
> **procyclical** — it amplifies the cycle rather than damping it.

## Self-check

*Closed book. Commit to an answer before opening the fold.*

**1.** A country's budget deficit widens from 3% to 6% of GDP during a recession. Is this expansionary fiscal policy?

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**Not necessarily — you must decompose it.**

A deficit widens in a recession **automatically**, without any policy decision:
- **Tax receipts fall** — incomes and profits decline, and progressive rate structures mean receipts fall more than proportionally
- **Transfer payments rise** — more unemployment benefit and welfare claims

These are the **automatic stabilisers**, and their operation is **neutral policy** — it is the system working as designed, not a discretionary choice.

**To judge the stance, look at the STRUCTURAL (cyclically adjusted) deficit** — the deficit that would exist at full employment, which strips out the cyclical component.

- If the **structural** deficit also widened by 3 points, the government has enacted genuine **discretionary stimulus** → **expansionary**
- If the structural deficit is **unchanged**, the entire widening is automatic → **neutral**
- If the structural deficit **narrowed** — say the government raised taxes while the cyclical component widened — the discretionary stance is actually **contractionary**, even though the headline deficit rose

**The general rule: judge by the CHANGE in the STRUCTURAL deficit, never by the level of the headline deficit.**

</details>

**2.** Explain the three lags and why they are the central objection to discretionary fiscal policy.

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**Recognition lag** — time to recognise the economy needs intervention. GDP data arrives with a quarter's delay, is subsequently **revised**, and turning points are only clear in hindsight. Several months, typically.

**Action lag** — time to legislate and enact. For fiscal policy this is the **longest** lag: drafting, parliamentary debate, committee stages, negotiation, and passage. Often six months to a year, sometimes more.

**Impact lag** — time for the enacted policy to reach the economy. A tax cut works relatively quickly; a capital spending programme requires design, procurement, and construction, taking years.

**Why this is the central objection:**

Sum the lags and discretionary fiscal stimulus can easily take **12 to 24 months** to reach the economy. The average post-war recession has lasted about that long.

So the stimulus frequently arrives **after the recovery has already begun** — into an expanding economy, adding to demand when demand is already recovering. It becomes **procyclical**, amplifying the cycle rather than damping it, and adding inflationary pressure.

**Friedman's 'long and variable lags'** argument applies to both monetary and fiscal policy: because the lags are both long **and** unpredictable, discretionary fine-tuning is as likely to destabilise as to stabilise.

**The practical conclusion:** rely primarily on **automatic stabilisers**, which have **no lags** — they begin working the moment incomes fall, without any decision being taken.

</details>

**3.** Give the strongest argument on each side of whether a high debt-to-GDP ratio matters.

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**The strongest argument that it MATTERS: lost fiscal space and the interest burden.**

A high debt stock means a high **interest bill**. When debt service consumes a large share of government revenue, the government's capacity to respond to the **next** crisis is gone — it cannot borrow more without raising doubts about sustainability, and it cannot cut existing spending because the interest is contractual.

This matters most at the moment of maximum need. A country entering a recession with 30% of revenue going to interest has no room; one at 4% has full flexibility. And the constraint can bind suddenly — if markets reprice the credit, the interest burden rises further, creating a self-reinforcing dynamic.

**Ricardian equivalence** strengthens this: if households rationally anticipate that today's borrowing means tomorrow's taxes, they **save** the stimulus rather than spending it, neutralising the policy while still accumulating the debt.

**The strongest argument that it MATTERS LESS: the ratio depends on growth and the interest rate, not on repayment.**

The debt-to-GDP ratio **falls automatically** whenever **nominal GDP growth exceeds the effective interest rate on the debt** — with no repayment at all. Countries have reduced very high ratios this way historically, through growth and moderate inflation rather than austerity.

And a government borrowing in **its own currency** cannot be forced into default (Fixed Income LM15) — the constraint is inflation, not solvency.

**The synthesis:** the **level** of debt is the wrong metric. What binds is **interest relative to revenue**, the **currency composition**, the **maturity profile** (refinancing risk), and whether the borrowing financed something **productive**. That is precisely the sovereign credit framework.

</details>

**4.** Why do transfer payments to low-income households have a higher fiscal multiplier than tax cuts for high earners?

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Because of the **marginal propensity to consume (MPC)**.

```
Fiscal multiplier = 1 / [1 − MPC(1 − t)]
```

The multiplier depends on how much of each additional unit of income is **respent** rather than saved. Each round of spending becomes someone else's income, which is partly respent, and so on — and the size of the total effect depends on the fraction respent at each round.

**Low-income households have a high MPC — close to 1.** They are typically liquidity-constrained, with unmet consumption needs and little or no savings buffer. An additional unit of income is **spent almost immediately**, on goods and services that generate further income for others.

**High-income households have a low MPC.** Their consumption is already largely unconstrained by income. An additional unit is more likely to be **saved or invested** — which does not enter the spending multiplier chain in the same way, and in an open economy may be invested abroad.

**The implication:** for a given fiscal cost, transfers targeted at low-income households generate substantially more aggregate demand than an equivalent tax cut for high earners. Estimates of the difference vary but are frequently a factor of two or more.

**The qualification:** this is a statement about **short-run demand stimulus**, not about the total economic effect. Tax cuts on capital may have supply-side effects on investment and productivity that the multiplier framework does not capture — which is a genuine argument, though it operates on a different timescale.

</details>
