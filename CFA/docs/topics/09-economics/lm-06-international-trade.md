## Core concepts

### Why countries trade

**Absolute advantage:** a country can produce a good using **fewer resources** than another.

**Comparative advantage:** a country can produce a good at a **lower opportunity cost** than
another.

> **Comparative advantage is the decisive concept, and it is the most counter-intuitive result in
> economics.** Even if a country has an **absolute advantage in EVERYTHING**, it still gains from
> trade by specialising in what it produces at the **lowest relative opportunity cost**, and
> importing the rest.
>
> The intuition: a surgeon may also be the fastest typist in the hospital (absolute advantage in
> both), but they should still spend their time on surgery and hire a typist — because the
> **opportunity cost** of an hour spent typing is an hour of surgery forgone.

**The two classic models:**

| Model | Source of comparative advantage |
| --- | --- |
| **Ricardian** | Differences in **labour productivity** (technology) |
| **Heckscher–Ohlin** | Differences in **factor endowments** — capital-abundant countries export capital-intensive goods; labour-abundant countries export labour-intensive goods |

### Benefits and costs of trade

| Benefits | Costs |
| --- | --- |
| **Gains from specialisation** — higher total output | **Distributional effects** — losses are **concentrated** in specific industries and regions while gains are **diffuse** |
| **Lower prices** and greater variety for consumers | **Job displacement** and adjustment costs in import-competing sectors |
| **Economies of scale** from larger markets | **Loss of policy autonomy** |
| **Greater competition**, driving efficiency and innovation | **Dependence** on foreign supply for critical goods |
| **Technology and knowledge transfer** | Vulnerability to external shocks |
| **Efficient capital allocation** | Environmental and labour standard arbitrage |

> **The political economy of trade follows entirely from the asymmetry in the first row.** Gains are
> **diffuse** — every consumer saves a small amount — while losses are **concentrated** — a specific
> factory closes in a specific town. Concentrated losers organise and lobby; diffuse winners do not
> notice. This is why trade restrictions persist despite a near-universal economic consensus that
> the aggregate gains exceed the losses, and it is a better explanation than any failure of
> economic argument.

### Types of trade restriction

| Restriction | Mechanism | Who gains | Effect on domestic price |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Tariff** | A **tax on imports** | Domestic producers; **the government** (tariff revenue) | **Rises** |
| **Quota** | A **quantity limit** on imports | Domestic producers; **whoever holds the import licence** (quota rents) | **Rises** |
| **Voluntary export restraint (VER)** | The **exporting** country limits its own exports | Domestic producers; **the FOREIGN exporter** captures the rents | **Rises** |
| **Export subsidy** | A **payment to domestic exporters** | Domestic producers; foreign consumers | **Rises domestically** (output diverted to export) |
| **Minimum domestic content** | A requirement to use domestic inputs | Domestic input producers | Rises |

> **The distinction that matters most: WHO CAPTURES THE RENT.**
> - A **tariff** generates revenue for the **domestic government**
> - A **quota** generates **quota rents** for whoever holds the import licence — a domestic importer
>   if licences are allocated domestically, or a **foreign exporter** if they are not
> - A **VER** gives the rent to the **foreign exporter** by construction, which is why exporting
>   countries sometimes prefer them to tariffs
>
> The **deadweight loss is similar** across the three; the difference is **distributional**.

**The economic effects of a tariff, in every case:**

- Domestic **price rises**
- Domestic **production rises** (producers gain)
- Domestic **consumption falls** (consumers lose)
- **Imports fall**
- **Government collects revenue**
- **Net welfare loss** — a **deadweight loss** — because the consumer loss exceeds the producer gain
  plus government revenue

> A **large country** that can influence world prices may improve its terms of trade enough to gain
> from a tariff — but only at the expense of its trading partner, and only if there is no
> retaliation. The **small country** case always produces a net loss.

**Arguments made for trade restrictions** (and the standard counterarguments):

| Argument | Counterargument |
| --- | --- |
| **Infant industry** — protect a new industry until it reaches scale | Protection rarely ends; the industry does not become competitive |
| **National security** — maintain domestic capacity in critical goods | Legitimate, but frequently invoked as cover for ordinary protectionism |
| **Protecting jobs** | Preserves jobs in the protected sector at a **higher cost per job** than the jobs are worth, paid by consumers |
| **Anti-dumping** — countering below-cost foreign pricing | Often used protectively; genuine predatory dumping is rare |
| **Retaliation / leverage** | Risks escalation, and retaliation harms both parties |

### Trading blocs — the levels of integration

**Learn the ordering. Each level includes everything in the levels above it.**

| Level | Adds |
| --- | --- |
| **1. Free trade area** | **No tariffs between members**; each keeps its **own external tariffs** |
| **2. Customs union** | **+ a COMMON external tariff** |
| **3. Common market** | **+ free movement of FACTORS** (labour and capital) |
| **4. Economic union** | **+ common economic institutions and coordinated economic policy** |
| **5. Monetary union** | **+ a COMMON CURRENCY** |

**Examples:** a free trade area (NAFTA/USMCA); a customs union (Mercosur); a common market; an
economic union (the EU); a monetary union (the eurozone).

> **The two boundaries to remember:** **free trade area → customs union** adds the **common external
> tariff**. **Common market → economic union** adds **policy coordination**. These are the two
> distinctions the exam tests.

### Trade creation and trade diversion

| | Meaning | Welfare effect |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Trade creation** | Trade **shifts from a high-cost domestic producer to a lower-cost member country** | **Positive** — resources move to the more efficient producer |
| **Trade diversion** | Trade **shifts from a low-cost NON-member to a higher-cost member**, because the member now faces no tariff | **Negative** — a less efficient producer displaces a more efficient one |

> **A trading bloc improves welfare only if trade creation exceeds trade diversion.** This is why
> regional trade agreements are not unambiguously beneficial: by discriminating against non-members,
> they can redirect trade **away** from the genuinely lowest-cost supplier. A preferential agreement
> is second-best to genuine multilateral liberalisation.

### Costs and benefits of monetary union

Joining a monetary union is the most consequential step, and the trade-offs are sharp:

| Benefits | Costs |
| --- | --- |
| Eliminates **exchange rate risk** between members | **Loss of independent monetary policy** — a single rate for divergent economies |
| Removes currency **transaction costs** | **Loss of the exchange rate as an adjustment mechanism** |
| **Price transparency** across members | Vulnerability to **asymmetric shocks** — a shock hitting one member but not others |
| Deeper, more liquid capital markets | Fiscal constraints imposed by membership |
| May **import monetary credibility** | Adjustment must occur through **wages and prices**, which are sticky |

> **The impossible trinity again** (LM4): a monetary union member has a **fixed exchange rate** with
> other members and **free capital movement**, so it necessarily has **no independent monetary
> policy**. When an asymmetric shock hits one member, it cannot devalue or cut rates — adjustment
> must occur through **internal devaluation**: falling wages and prices, which is slow and produces
> high unemployment.

  where its opportunity cost is lowest.

  Ricardian model      → differences in LABOUR PRODUCTIVITY
  Heckscher-Ohlin      → differences in FACTOR ENDOWMENTS

TRADE RESTRICTIONS — who captures the rent
  TARIFF  — tax on imports        → revenue to the DOMESTIC GOVERNMENT
  QUOTA   — quantity limit        → QUOTA RENTS to the licence holder
  VER     — exporter self-limits  → rents to the FOREIGN EXPORTER
  EXPORT SUBSIDY — payment to exporters → raises the DOMESTIC price
  All raise the domestic price and create a DEADWEIGHT LOSS; they differ DISTRIBUTIONALLY.

LEVELS OF INTEGRATION — each includes all the previous ones
  1. FREE TRADE AREA  — no internal tariffs; own external tariffs
  2. CUSTOMS UNION    — + COMMON EXTERNAL TARIFF

  CREATION  — shifts from a high-cost DOMESTIC producer to a lower-cost MEMBER → POSITIVE
  DIVERSION — shifts from a low-cost NON-MEMBER to a higher-cost MEMBER        → NEGATIVE

## Exam traps

> **Trap 1 — Confusing absolute and comparative advantage.** **Comparative advantage is about
> OPPORTUNITY COST.** A country with an absolute advantage in everything still gains from trade.

> **Trap 2 — Missing who captures the rent.** **Tariff → domestic government revenue.
> Quota → quota rents to the licence holder. VER → rents to the FOREIGN exporter.**

> **Trap 3 — Getting the integration levels out of order.** **Free trade area → customs union**
> (common external tariff) **→ common market** (free factor movement) **→ economic union** (policy
> coordination) **→ monetary union** (common currency).

> **Trap 4 — Assuming trading blocs always improve welfare.** Only if **trade creation exceeds trade
> diversion**. Diversion redirects trade away from the genuinely lowest-cost producer.

> **Trap 5 — Thinking a tariff benefits the country overall.** In the small-country case there is
> always a **net deadweight loss**. Only a large country able to move world prices can gain, and
> only absent retaliation.

> **Trap 6 — Forgetting the political economy.** **Gains are diffuse, losses concentrated** — which
> is why restrictions persist, not because the economics are disputed.

> **Trap 7 — Underestimating the cost of monetary union.** Members lose **both** independent
> monetary policy **and** the exchange rate as an adjustment mechanism, leaving only slow internal
> devaluation.

## Self-check

*Closed book. Commit to an answer before opening the fold.*

**1.** Country A can produce both wheat and cloth more efficiently than Country B in absolute terms. Should they trade?

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**Yes — provided their opportunity costs differ.**

Absolute advantage in both goods is irrelevant to whether trade is beneficial. What matters is **comparative advantage** — the **opportunity cost** of producing one good in terms of the other.

**Illustration.** Suppose Country A can produce either 10 wheat or 5 cloth with its resources; Country B can produce either 4 wheat or 3 cloth. A is absolutely better at both.

But the **opportunity costs** differ:
- In A, one cloth costs **2 wheat** (giving up 10 wheat to gain 5 cloth)
- In B, one cloth costs **1.33 wheat** (giving up 4 wheat to gain 3 cloth)

**B has the comparative advantage in cloth** — it gives up less wheat to produce it. And A has the comparative advantage in wheat.

So A should specialise in **wheat**, B in **cloth**, and they should trade. Total output of both goods rises relative to each producing both.

**The intuition:** a surgeon who is also the hospital's fastest typist should still spend their time on surgery and hire a typist — because the **opportunity cost** of an hour spent typing is an hour of surgery forgone. Absolute superiority does not change that.

**The only case with no gain:** if opportunity costs are **identical** in both countries, there is no comparative advantage and no gain from specialisation.

</details>

**2.** Compare a tariff, a quota, and a voluntary export restraint on their economic effects.

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**All three have similar effects on prices and quantities:**
- The domestic price **rises**
- Domestic production **rises** (producers gain)
- Domestic consumption **falls** (consumers lose)
- Imports **fall**
- A **deadweight loss** arises — the consumer loss exceeds the producer gain plus any revenue

**They differ in WHO CAPTURES THE RENT** — the difference between the world price and the higher domestic price:

**Tariff:** the **domestic government** collects the tariff revenue. From the importing country's perspective this is the best of the three, because the rent stays at home and can be redistributed.

**Quota:** the rent goes to **whoever holds the import licence**. If licences are auctioned by the government, it captures the rent (equivalent to a tariff). If they are allocated to domestic importers, those firms capture it. If they are allocated to **foreign exporters**, the rent leaves the country entirely.

**Voluntary export restraint:** the **foreign exporter** captures the rent **by construction** — they restrict their own volume and sell at the higher price. This is the **worst** outcome for the importing country, which bears the consumer loss **and** transfers the rent abroad.

**Why VERs are used anyway:** they are politically convenient. They avoid the formal appearance of protectionism, are not subject to the same WTO challenge, and the exporting country often **accepts them willingly** — precisely because it captures the rent. Both governments can present it as a negotiated accommodation.

**The ranking for the importing country: tariff > quota (if licences auctioned) > quota (allocated abroad) ≈ VER.**

</details>

**3.** List the five levels of economic integration and explain the two most-tested boundaries.

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**The five levels, each including everything in the previous ones:**

**1. Free trade area** — no tariffs between members, but each member keeps its **own external tariffs** against non-members.
**2. Customs union** — adds a **common external tariff**.
**3. Common market** — adds **free movement of factors of production** (labour and capital).
**4. Economic union** — adds **common economic institutions and coordinated economic policy**.
**5. Monetary union** — adds a **common currency**.

**The two most-tested boundaries:**

**Free trade area → customs union: the COMMON EXTERNAL TARIFF.** This is more consequential than it sounds. In a free trade area, members have different external tariffs, so goods can be imported into the lowest-tariff member and shipped tariff-free to the others — **trade deflection**. Preventing this requires **rules of origin** and customs checks at internal borders. A common external tariff eliminates the problem and allows internal borders to open properly.

**Common market → economic union: POLICY COORDINATION.** A common market frees the movement of goods, services, labour, and capital, but members still set their own fiscal, competition, and regulatory policy. An economic union adds **common institutions** and coordinated policy — a substantial transfer of sovereignty, which is why the step is politically difficult.

**Examples:** USMCA (free trade area), Mercosur (customs union), the EU (economic union), the eurozone (monetary union — a subset of EU members).

</details>

**4.** What is trade diversion, and why does it mean trading blocs are not unambiguously beneficial?

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**Trade diversion** occurs when a trading bloc causes imports to shift from a **low-cost non-member** to a **higher-cost member**, purely because the member now faces no tariff while the non-member still does.

**Illustration.** Country A imports widgets from Country C (the world's lowest-cost producer at 10) with a 30% tariff, so the domestic cost is 13. Country B produces widgets at 12 and also faces the tariff, costing 15.6. A imports from C.

A and B now form a customs union. B's widgets enter A tariff-free at **12**; C's still cost **13**. A now imports from **B**.

**The result:** production has shifted from the genuinely efficient producer (10) to a less efficient one (12). Global resources are used **worse**. A's government also loses the tariff revenue it was collecting on imports from C.

**Contrast with trade creation:** if A had previously produced widgets domestically at 14 and now imports from B at 12, that is **trade creation** — a high-cost domestic producer replaced by a lower-cost member. Unambiguously positive.

**Why blocs are therefore ambiguous:** a preferential agreement improves welfare only if **trade creation exceeds trade diversion**. Because blocs **discriminate against non-members**, they can systematically redirect trade away from the lowest-cost global supplier.

**The policy implication:** regional agreements are **second-best** to genuine multilateral liberalisation, which lowers barriers against everyone and cannot cause diversion. This is a substantial part of the economic case for the WTO's multilateral approach over proliferating bilateral deals.

</details>
