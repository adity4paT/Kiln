## Core concepts

### The four market structures

| | **Perfect competition** | **Monopolistic competition** | **Oligopoly** | **Pure monopoly** |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Number of sellers** | Very many | Many | **Few** | **One** |
| **Product** | **Identical** | **Differentiated** | Identical or differentiated | Unique, no close substitute |
| **Barriers to entry** | **None** | Low | **High** | **Very high** |
| **Pricing power** | **None** — price taker | **Some** | **Significant**, but interdependent | **Substantial** |
| **Demand curve facing the firm** | **Horizontal** (perfectly elastic) | Downward sloping | Downward sloping, possibly **kinked** | Downward sloping (the market demand curve) |
| **Long-run economic profit** | **Zero** | **Zero** | **Positive** possible | **Positive** |
| **Non-price competition** | None | **Heavy** — advertising, branding | Substantial | Limited need |
| **Examples** | Agricultural commodities | Restaurants, retail clothing | Airlines, telecoms, autos | Regulated utilities, patented drugs |

> **The two variables that determine everything: the number of sellers and the barriers to entry.**
> Barriers determine whether economic profit can persist; product differentiation determines whether
> the firm faces a downward-sloping demand curve and therefore has any pricing power at all.

### Profit maximisation — the universal rule

**Every firm in every market structure maximises profit where:**

```
Marginal revenue (MR) = Marginal cost (MC)
```

The structures differ only in **what marginal revenue looks like**:

- **Perfect competition:** the firm is a price taker, so `MR = Price`. It produces where `P = MC`.
- **All other structures:** the demand curve slopes downward, so selling one more unit requires
  lowering the price on **all** units. `MR < Price`, and the firm produces where `MR = MC` but
  charges the **higher** price the demand curve permits at that quantity.

> **This is why imperfect competition produces less output at a higher price than perfect
> competition** — the source of the deadweight loss that motivates competition regulation.

### Breakeven and shutdown

| Condition | Decision |
| --- | --- |
| **Price > Average total cost (ATC)** | **Economic profit.** Produce |
| **Price = ATC** | **Breakeven** — zero economic profit (normal profit). Produce |
| **AVC < Price < ATC** | **Loss, but continue producing in the short run.** Revenue covers all variable costs and contributes to fixed costs |
| **Price < Average variable cost (AVC)** | **SHUT DOWN** in the short run — producing loses more than closing |
| **Price < ATC in the long run** | **Exit the market** |

```
Breakeven point:  Price = ATC  = minimum average total cost
Shutdown point:   Price = AVC  = minimum average variable cost
```

> **The distinction between the short and long run is the fixed costs.** In the **short run** fixed
> costs are **sunk** — they are incurred whether you produce or not — so the only question is
> whether revenue covers the **variable** costs. If it does, producing reduces the loss. In the
> **long run** all costs are variable and there are no sunk costs, so the firm exits unless price
> covers **total** cost.
>
> This is the sunk cost principle from Corporate Issuers LM5, applied to production.

### Economies and diseconomies of scale

**The long-run average total cost (LRATC) curve** is U-shaped:

```
LRATC
  │╲                              ╱
  │ ╲                            ╱
  │  ╲______________________ ___╱
  │   ↑        ↑              ↑
  │ ECONOMIES  minimum      DISECONOMIES
  │ of scale   efficient    of scale
  │            scale (MES)
  └────────────────────────────── Output
```

| | Cause |
| --- | --- |
| **Economies of scale** (LRATC falling) | Specialisation and division of labour, bulk purchasing power, spreading fixed costs, better technology at scale, lower financing costs |
| **Diseconomies of scale** (LRATC rising) | Coordination and communication costs, bureaucracy, duplication, reduced flexibility, management span of control |
| **Minimum efficient scale (MES)** | The output level at which LRATC is minimised |

> **MES relative to market size is what determines market structure.** If MES is a small fraction of
> total demand, many firms can operate efficiently → competitive structure. If MES is a large
> fraction, only a few firms can be efficient → **natural oligopoly**. If MES exceeds total demand,
> one firm is efficient → **natural monopoly**, which is why utilities are regulated rather than
> broken up.

### Monopolistic competition

Many firms selling **differentiated** products with **low barriers to entry**.

- Each firm faces a **downward-sloping but highly elastic** demand curve — it has some pricing power
  because its product is differentiated, but close substitutes limit it.
- **Heavy non-price competition:** advertising, branding, product features, service, location.
- **Short run:** economic profit is possible.
- **Long run: economic profit is competed away to ZERO**, because low barriers allow entry. Entrants
  take share, shifting each firm's demand curve left until price equals ATC.
- The long-run equilibrium has price **above** marginal cost and output **below** the minimum-ATC
  level — **excess capacity**, which is the cost society pays for product variety.

### Oligopoly

**Few sellers, high barriers to entry, and — the defining feature — strategic interdependence.**
Each firm's optimal action depends on what the others do.

**Models worth knowing:**

| Model | Mechanism |
| --- | --- |
| **Kinked demand curve** | Rivals **match price cuts** (so demand is inelastic below the current price) but **do not match increases** (so demand is elastic above it). The result is a **kink** and price rigidity — prices stay stable even as costs change |
| **Cournot** | Firms compete on **quantity**, each assuming the other's output is fixed. Price ends between monopoly and competitive levels |
| **Nash equilibrium** | No firm can improve by unilaterally changing strategy, given the others' choices |
| **Stackelberg** | A **leader** moves first and a **follower** responds. The leader gains an advantage |
| **Dominant firm** | A large firm sets the price; a competitive fringe takes it |
| **Collusion / cartel** | Firms coordinate to act as a monopoly. Illegal in most jurisdictions, and **inherently unstable** because each member has an incentive to cheat |

**Conditions that make collusion more likely to succeed:** few firms, similar cost structures, a
homogeneous product, credible retaliation threats, frequent and observable interactions, and no
regulatory enforcement.

### Concentration measures and their limits

```
Concentration ratio CRn = Σ market shares of the largest n firms
Herfindahl-Hirschman Index HHI = Σ (market share as a percentage)²  over ALL firms
```

**HHI interpretation:** below 1,500 unconcentrated; 1,500–2,500 moderately concentrated; above 2,500
highly concentrated.

**Why HHI is superior to CRn:** the concentration ratio treats four equal firms the same as one
dominant firm plus three small ones. HHI **squares** the shares, so it reflects the **size
distribution**, not merely the top-n total.

**The limitations of both — an explicit LOS:**

- **Neither reflects barriers to entry.** An industry with two firms and no barriers may be
  fiercely competitive, because the **threat** of entry disciplines pricing (a **contestable
  market**).
- **Market definition is contentious.** Is the market national or local? Does it include substitutes
  and imports? Widening or narrowing the definition can change concentration dramatically.
- **They are static** — they say nothing about whether shares are stable or shifting, which is often
  more informative than the level (Equity LM9).
- **They ignore product differentiation** and the elasticity of demand.

> **The practical conclusion:** concentration measures are a **starting point**, not a conclusion.
> Barriers to entry matter more than the number of firms, which is why Porter's framework (Equity
> LM9) is the more useful analytical tool.

## Formulas to know cold

```
PROFIT MAXIMISATION — universal across all structures
  MR = MC
    Perfect competition:  MR = Price  →  produce where P = MC
    All others:           MR < Price  →  produce where MR = MC, charge what demand permits

BREAKEVEN AND SHUTDOWN
  Price > ATC        → economic profit
  Price = ATC        → BREAKEVEN (normal profit)
  AVC < Price < ATC  → loss, but CONTINUE in the short run (covers variable costs)
  Price < AVC        → SHUT DOWN in the short run
  Price < ATC long run → EXIT

  Breakeven point = minimum ATC   |   Shutdown point = minimum AVC

SCALE
  Economies of scale    → LRATC falling (specialisation, bulk buying, spreading fixed costs)
  Diseconomies of scale → LRATC rising (coordination, bureaucracy, duplication)
  Minimum efficient scale (MES) = output at minimum LRATC
  MES relative to MARKET SIZE determines the market structure:
     MES small → many firms | MES large → oligopoly | MES > demand → NATURAL MONOPOLY

CONCENTRATION
  CRn = Σ shares of the largest n firms
  HHI = Σ (share as %)²  over ALL firms
     < 1,500 unconcentrated | 1,500-2,500 moderate | > 2,500 highly concentrated
  HHI is SUPERIOR because it reflects the SIZE DISTRIBUTION.
  BOTH ignore BARRIERS TO ENTRY, which matter more.
```

## Exam traps

> **Trap 1 — The shutdown condition.** Shut down when **Price < AVC**, not when price is below ATC.
> Between AVC and ATC the firm loses money but **continues producing** in the short run, because
> revenue covers all variable costs and contributes toward the sunk fixed costs.

> **Trap 2 — Thinking perfect competitors maximise revenue.** All firms maximise profit at
> **MR = MC**. Only in perfect competition does that coincide with `P = MC`.

> **Trap 3 — Assuming zero long-run profit means zero accounting profit.** Zero **economic** profit
> means the firm earns exactly its **normal** return — a competitive return on capital. It is not
> losing money.

> **Trap 4 — Confusing monopolistic competition with monopoly.** **Monopolistic competition has
> many firms and low barriers**, so long-run economic profit is **zero**. Only the differentiated
> product is "monopolistic".

> **Trap 5 — Reading concentration without barriers to entry.** A two-firm industry with **no
> barriers** can be fiercely competitive — the threat of entry disciplines pricing. Concentration
> alone tells you very little.

> **Trap 6 — Using CRn when HHI is available.** CRn ignores the size distribution among the top n.

> **Trap 7 — Missing the kinked demand curve's implication.** It explains **price rigidity** —
> oligopoly prices stay stable even when costs change, because neither raising nor cutting is
> attractive.

> **Trap 8 — Forgetting that MES relative to market size determines structure.** This is the link
> between cost curves and market structure, and it explains why utilities are natural monopolies.

## Self-check

*Closed book. Commit to an answer before opening the fold.*

**1.** A firm's price is 18, average total cost is 22, and average variable cost is 15. What should it do in the short run and in the long run?

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**Short run: CONTINUE PRODUCING.**

Price (18) is **above** AVC (15), so each unit sold contributes 3 toward the fixed costs. The firm is losing money — price is below ATC of 22, so it loses 4 per unit — but **shutting down would lose more**. Fixed costs are **sunk** in the short run and must be paid whether or not the firm produces. Producing recovers 3 per unit of them; shutting down recovers none.

**Long run: EXIT.**

In the long run **all costs are variable** and there are no sunk costs. If price remains below ATC, the firm cannot earn a normal return on its capital and should redeploy it elsewhere.

**The decision rule, restated:** in the short run, compare price to **AVC** (the avoidable cost). In the long run, compare price to **ATC** (the total cost). This is the sunk cost principle from Corporate Issuers LM5 applied to production.

</details>

**2.** Why is long-run economic profit zero under monopolistic competition but potentially positive under oligopoly?

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**The difference is barriers to entry.**

**Monopolistic competition has LOW barriers.** If incumbent firms are earning economic profit, new firms enter — opening a restaurant or a clothing shop requires modest capital and no licence. Entry takes share from incumbents, shifting each firm's demand curve **left** until price equals ATC and economic profit is **competed away to zero**.

The firm still has **some pricing power** (its product is differentiated, so its demand curve slopes downward), but that power cannot generate lasting profit because entry is unrestricted.

**Oligopoly has HIGH barriers.** These may be:
- **Economies of scale** — MES is a large fraction of market demand, so a new entrant cannot reach efficient scale
- **Capital requirements** — building an airline, a telecoms network, or a car plant
- **Regulatory** — licences, spectrum, route rights
- **Network effects** or established brands
- **Control of distribution** or of an essential input

Entry is therefore blocked, and incumbents can sustain economic profit **indefinitely**.

**The general principle, which is the whole point of the LOS:** **barriers to entry, not the number of firms, determine whether economic profit persists.** This is the same insight as Porter's threat of new entrants (Equity LM9) and the 'name the mechanism' test for durable competitive advantage (Equity LM10).

</details>

**3.** Explain the kinked demand curve model and what it predicts.

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**The asymmetric assumption:** each oligopolist believes that
- **If it CUTS its price, rivals will MATCH** — to avoid losing share. So the firm gains little volume, and demand below the current price is **inelastic**.
- **If it RAISES its price, rivals will NOT follow** — they are happy to take share. So the firm loses a lot of volume, and demand above the current price is **elastic**.

The result is a **kink** in the demand curve at the prevailing price, and a corresponding **discontinuity (vertical gap) in the marginal revenue curve** at that quantity.

**What it predicts: PRICE RIGIDITY.**

Because marginal revenue jumps at the kink, the marginal cost curve can shift **up or down within that gap** without changing the profit-maximising quantity. So the firm's optimal price stays **unchanged** even when costs change.

This explains an observed feature of oligopolistic industries: **prices are stickier than cost movements would suggest**. Firms absorb moderate cost changes in their margins rather than repricing.

**The limitations:**
- It **explains** why prices are stable at a given level but not **how that level was reached**
- It assumes rivals react in a specific asymmetric way without explaining why
- It has been criticised as descriptive rather than predictive

But as a description of oligopolistic behaviour — and for exam purposes — it is the model to know.

</details>

**4.** Industry A has an HHI of 2,800 but no barriers to entry. Industry B has an HHI of 1,200 with very high barriers. Which is more likely to sustain high margins?

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**Industry B**, despite its much lower concentration.

**Industry A** is highly concentrated on paper, but with **no barriers to entry** it is a **contestable market**. If incumbents raise prices above competitive levels, new firms enter and compete the profit away. Crucially, the **threat alone disciplines pricing** — incumbents price as if competitors were present, because they could be. High concentration with free entry does not produce sustained economic profit.

**Industry B** is only moderately concentrated, but **high barriers** mean the existing firms are protected. Even with several competitors, none faces the threat of new entry, so the competitive equilibrium settles at a level that sustains economic profit indefinitely.

**What this demonstrates — and it is the explicit point of the LOS:** **concentration measures do not capture barriers to entry**, which matter far more for sustained profitability.

**Both HHI and CRn also miss:**
- **Market definition** — is it national, local, or global? Does it include imports and substitutes?
- **Whether shares are stable or shifting** — a settled equilibrium versus a live battle (Equity LM9)
- **Product differentiation** and demand elasticity
- **Buyer and supplier power** (Porter)

**The practical conclusion:** use concentration as a **screen**, then do the structural analysis with Porter's five forces. The number of competitors is far less informative than why there are not more of them.

</details>
