## Core concepts

### Market segments and participants

**Issuers, by sector:**

| Sector | Who |
| --- | --- |
| **Sovereign governments** | National governments, in their own currency and in foreign currency |
| **Non-sovereign governments** | States, provinces, regions, municipalities |
| **Quasi-government agencies** | Government-sponsored or government-owned entities |
| **Supranationals** | World Bank, IMF, European Investment Bank, regional development banks |
| **Corporates** | Financial and non-financial companies |
| **Structured finance** | Special purpose entities issuing ABS and MBS (LM17–LM19) |

**Investors:**

| Investor | Typical motivation |
| --- | --- |
| **Central banks** | Monetary policy operations; foreign reserve management |
| **Institutional — pension funds, insurers** | **Liability matching**. Long-dated, predictable cash flows |
| **Banks** | Liquidity buffers; regulatory capital requirements; the asset side of their spread |
| **Mutual funds and ETFs** | On behalf of retail and institutional investors |
| **Sovereign wealth funds** | Reserve investment |
| **Hedge funds** | Relative value, credit, and distressed strategies |
| **Retail** | Income, directly or through funds |

> **The dominant fact about the investor base: it is overwhelmingly institutional.** Pension funds
> and insurers buy bonds to **match liabilities**, not to maximise return — which is why demand for
> very long-dated bonds can be strong even at yields that look unattractive on a return basis. This
> also explains why the retail presence is far smaller than in equities.

**Segments by credit quality and maturity:**

- **Investment grade** (BBB−/Baa3 and above) versus **high yield** (below). The boundary matters
  enormously because many institutional mandates prohibit holding below investment grade — a
  downgrade across it forces selling regardless of price (a **fallen angel**).
- **Money market** (one year or less at issuance) versus **capital market** (longer).

### Primary market

| Mechanism | How it works |
| --- | --- |
| **Underwritten offering** | An investment bank **buys the issue** and resells it, bearing the risk. Includes the **bookbuilding** process — gauging demand and setting the price |
| **Best efforts** | The bank acts as **agent** only, with no commitment to buy |
| **Auction** | Used for most **government** bonds. **Single-price (Dutch)** — all winning bidders pay the same clearing price; **multiple-price** — each pays their own bid |
| **Private placement** | Sold directly to a small number of institutional investors; no public offering, minimal disclosure |
| **Shelf registration** | Register once, then issue in tranches as needed |

**The grey market** is the pre-issuance forward market in a new bond, which helps establish the price
before formal issuance.

### Secondary market

> **The defining contrast with equities: most bond trading is over-the-counter and dealer-based, not
> exchange-based.**

| | **Bonds** | **Equities** |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Venue | Predominantly **OTC**, dealer networks | Predominantly **exchange** |
| Market structure | **Quote-driven** — dealers post bid and ask and hold inventory | **Order-driven** — a central order book matches buyers and sellers |
| Number of instruments | **Enormous** — one issuer may have dozens of outstanding bonds, each with different maturity, coupon, and covenants | One or a few classes per company |
| Liquidity | **Much lower and highly uneven.** Recent large issues trade; older small ones may not trade for weeks | Generally high for listed names |
| Transparency | **Lower.** Pre-trade transparency is limited; post-trade reporting varies by market | High |
| Bid-ask spreads | **Wider**, and highly variable by issue | Narrower |
| Settlement | T+1 or T+2 for most; **same day** for many government bonds | T+1 in most major markets |

**Why bond liquidity is structurally lower:** the number of distinct instruments vastly exceeds the
number of listed equities, each bond is a different contract, most investors are **buy-and-hold**
liability matchers who do not trade, and dealers must commit balance sheet to hold inventory —
capacity that has fallen since post-crisis capital regulation.

**On-the-run versus off-the-run:** the **most recently issued** government bond of a given maturity
is **on-the-run** and is by far the most liquid. Older issues of similar maturity are **off-the-run**
and trade at a small yield premium purely for their lesser liquidity. The on-the-run bond is the
usual benchmark for spread quotation.

### Fixed-income indexes

| Type | Basis |
| --- | --- |
| **Broad market / aggregate** | The whole investable universe in a currency |
| **Sector** | Government, corporate, securitised, municipal |
| **Credit quality** | Investment grade, high yield |
| **Maturity** | Short, intermediate, long |
| **Geographic** | Domestic, global, emerging market |
| **Inflation-linked** | Index-linked government bonds |
| **Custom / liability-driven** | Built to match a specific liability profile |

**Why bond indexes are harder to construct than equity indexes:**

- The universe **turns over constantly** — bonds mature and new ones are issued continuously, so
  the index must be reconstituted frequently
- Many constituents **do not trade**, so prices are **matrix-priced** or model-estimated rather than
  observed
- The number of constituents runs into the thousands or tens of thousands
- **Replication is impractical** — index funds must use stratified sampling rather than full
  replication

> **The perverse feature of market-value weighting in bond indexes:** weighting by **amount
> outstanding** gives the **largest weight to the largest borrowers**. An investor tracking such an
> index is most exposed to whoever has issued the most debt — the opposite of what a credit-conscious
> investor would choose. This is a genuine criticism and a favourite exam point. Alternative
> schemes (GDP-weighting for sovereigns, fundamental weighting for corporates) exist to address it.

## Formulas to know cold

No formulas. The comparison table is what must be memorised:

```
BOND MARKETS vs EQUITY MARKETS
  Venue:         OTC / dealer          vs  Exchange
  Structure:     QUOTE-driven          vs  ORDER-driven
  Instruments:   many per issuer       vs  one or few per company
  Liquidity:     lower, uneven         vs  generally high
  Transparency:  lower                 vs  higher
  Spreads:       wider, variable       vs  narrower

PRIMARY MARKET MECHANISMS
  Underwritten (bank BUYS the issue) · Best efforts (agent only)
  · Auction — single-price/Dutch (all pay the clearing price) or multiple-price
  · Private placement · Shelf registration

ON-THE-RUN = the most recently issued government bond of a maturity; most liquid; the benchmark
OFF-THE-RUN = older issues; small yield premium for lower liquidity

BOND INDEX PROBLEM: market-value weighting gives the LARGEST weight to the LARGEST BORROWERS.
```

## Exam traps

> **Trap 1 — Assuming bonds trade like equities.** Most bond trading is **OTC and dealer-based
> (quote-driven)**, not on an exchange with a central order book.

> **Trap 2 — Overstating bond liquidity.** It is **much lower and far more uneven** than equity
> liquidity. Many issues do not trade for extended periods.

> **Trap 3 — Underwritten vs best efforts.** In an **underwritten** offering the bank **buys the
> issue** and bears the resale risk. In **best efforts** it is merely an agent.

> **Trap 4 — Single-price vs multiple-price auctions.** In a **single-price (Dutch)** auction **all
> winning bidders pay the same clearing price**. In a multiple-price auction each pays their own bid.

> **Trap 5 — Forgetting the bond index weighting problem.** Market-value weighting tilts the index
> toward the **most indebted** issuers.

> **Trap 6 — Missing the on-the-run/off-the-run distinction.** The yield difference is a **liquidity**
> premium, not a credit one — the issuer is identical.

## Self-check

*Closed book. Commit to an answer before opening the fold.*

**1.** Why is the bond market predominantly over-the-counter while the equity market is exchange-based?

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Because of the **number and heterogeneity of instruments**.

A company typically has **one** class of ordinary shares. The same company may have **dozens** of outstanding bonds, each with a different maturity, coupon, currency, seniority, and covenant package — every one a distinct contract. Across a market that produces tens of thousands of distinct instruments, most of which trade rarely.

An **exchange with a central order book** needs continuous two-sided interest in a standardised instrument to function. That condition simply does not hold for most bonds. A **dealer market** works instead: dealers hold inventory, quote bid and ask on request, and provide immediacy by taking the other side themselves.

**Reinforcing factors:** the investor base is dominated by **buy-and-hold** liability matchers (pensions, insurers) who rarely trade; and institutional trade sizes are large, favouring negotiated bilateral execution over a public order book that would reveal intent.

**The consequence:** lower pre-trade transparency, wider and more variable spreads, and liquidity that is concentrated in recent large issues.

</details>

**2.** Explain the difference between a single-price and a multiple-price auction, and which favours the bidder.

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**Single-price (Dutch) auction:** bids are ranked, the clearing price is the lowest accepted price (highest accepted yield), and **all winning bidders pay that same clearing price** — even those who bid more aggressively.

**Multiple-price (discriminatory) auction:** each winning bidder pays **their own bid price**. Bidders who bid aggressively pay more than those who bid at the margin.

**Which favours the bidder: the single-price auction.** It eliminates the **winner's curse** — the risk of bidding well above the clearing level and paying for it. That reduces the incentive to shade bids downward defensively, which in turn tends to produce **more aggressive bidding and a better price for the issuer**.

This is why most government bond auctions, including US Treasury auctions, use the **single-price** format. It is a case where making the auction more favourable to bidders improves the outcome for the seller.

</details>

**3.** Why does the most recently issued 10-year government bond yield slightly less than a 10-year bond issued two years ago by the same government?

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**Liquidity**, not credit.

The most recently issued bond is **on-the-run**. It is the benchmark: it has the largest outstanding size, the tightest bid-ask spread, the most active repo market, and it is what dealers and investors use to express views on that maturity point. That liquidity is valuable, and investors pay for it — a higher price, therefore a **lower yield**.

The older bond is **off-the-run**. Much of it has been absorbed into buy-and-hold portfolios, so less floats freely, spreads are wider, and it is harder to trade in size. Investors demand a small yield premium as compensation.

**The credit risk is identical** — same issuer, same currency, nearly the same maturity. The entire difference is the **liquidity premium**, which is one of the components of required return from QM LM1.

The spread typically widens in stressed markets, when liquidity is most prized — which is also when it becomes a tradeable signal of market stress.

</details>

**4.** What is the criticism of market-value weighting in bond indexes, and what alternatives exist?

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**The criticism:** weighting by **amount outstanding** gives the **largest index weight to the issuers who have borrowed the most**. An investor tracking such an index is therefore most exposed to the most indebted governments and companies — which is precisely the opposite of what a credit-conscious investor would choose.

In equities, market-cap weighting at least tilts toward companies the market values highly. In bonds, the analogous weighting tilts toward whoever has issued the most **liabilities**. The 'bums problem', as it is sometimes called.

**Alternatives:**
- **GDP-weighting** for sovereign indexes — weight countries by economic size rather than debt outstanding
- **Fundamental weighting** for corporates — weight by revenue, cash flow, or assets
- **Equal weighting** by issuer, capping any single name
- **Credit-quality caps** — limiting exposure to lower-rated issuers
- **Liability-driven custom indexes** built to match a specific cash flow profile rather than a market

Each introduces its own tilts and turnover, so none is a clean solution — but the criticism of market-value weighting is real and worth being able to state.

</details>
