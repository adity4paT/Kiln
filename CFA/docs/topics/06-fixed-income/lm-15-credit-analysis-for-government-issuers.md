## Core concepts

### What makes sovereign credit different

Three features distinguish sovereign analysis from corporate analysis:

1. **The ability to create money.** A government borrowing in **its own currency** can, in the last
   resort, print it. Local-currency default is therefore a **policy choice**, not an inability.
2. **No enforceable claim on assets.** There is no bankruptcy court with jurisdiction over a
   sovereign, and no mechanism to seize its assets. Recovery depends on **negotiation**, and defaults
   are usually resolved through restructuring rather than liquidation.
3. **Willingness matters as much as ability.** A sovereign may be able to pay and choose not to,
   weighing the cost of repayment against the cost of losing market access.

> **Consequence: rating agencies assign separate local-currency and foreign-currency ratings.**
> Foreign-currency debt cannot be printed and must be earned or borrowed, so foreign-currency
> defaults are far more common historically. A eurozone member is in an intermediate position: it
> does not control its own currency, so its "local-currency" debt behaves more like
> foreign-currency debt.

### The five areas of sovereign analysis

| Area | What to examine |
| --- | --- |
| **Institutional effectiveness** | Political stability, rule of law, property rights, policy predictability, corruption, transparency of statistics, track record of honouring obligations |
| **Economic structure and growth** | GDP per capita (the single best predictor of rating), growth trend, diversification of the economy, dependence on a single commodity or sector, demographics |
| **External position** | Current account balance, **foreign exchange reserves**, external debt as a share of GDP and of exports, reliance on foreign-currency funding, exchange rate regime |
| **Fiscal position** | **Debt/GDP**, fiscal deficit, **interest burden as a share of revenue**, debt maturity profile, contingent liabilities (state-owned enterprises, bank guarantees, pensions) |
| **Monetary flexibility** | Central bank independence and credibility, inflation record, ability to run countercyclical policy, depth of the domestic capital market |

**The ratios that matter most:**

```
Debt / GDP
Fiscal deficit / GDP
Interest payments / Government revenue      ← an underrated and very informative measure
External debt / Exports
Foreign exchange reserves / Short-term external debt
Current account balance / GDP
```

> **Interest as a share of revenue is the one to watch.** Debt/GDP is widely quoted but says little
> on its own — Japan's is enormous and its funding cost is trivial. What binds is the **interest
> bill relative to the government's income**: when a large share of revenue goes to debt service, the
> fiscal space to respond to a shock disappears, and the position becomes fragile.

**Warning signs:**

- Heavy and rising **foreign-currency** borrowing
- Short **debt maturity profile** — large amounts to refinance in a single year
- Low or falling **reserves** relative to short-term external debt
- Persistent large **current account deficits** funded by portfolio flows rather than direct investment
- Dependence on a **single commodity** whose price has fallen
- **Political instability** or a weak track record of honouring obligations
- Large **contingent liabilities** — state enterprises, a banking system needing support

### Non-sovereign government issuers

States, provinces, regions, and municipalities **cannot create money** and generally have limited
taxing autonomy, so they carry genuine credit risk.

**Two structures, analysed differently:**

| | **General obligation (GO)** | **Revenue bond** |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Backed by | The issuer's **full faith, credit, and taxing power** | The cash flow from a **specific project** |
| Analyse | The **tax base**: its size, diversity, growth, and wealth. Plus the expenditure structure and pension obligations | The **project's economics**: demand, pricing, cost structure, and the **debt service coverage ratio** |
| Typical yield | Lower | **Higher** — no general claim on the issuer |

```
Debt service coverage ratio = Net project revenue / Annual debt service
```

A DSCR meaningfully above 1.0 (often a covenanted minimum of 1.2× or higher) is required for a
revenue bond.

**Additional considerations for non-sovereigns:**

- **Support from the national government** — explicit, implicit, or none. This can dominate the
  analysis.
- **Economic and tax base concentration** — a municipality dependent on one employer or one industry
- **Pension and other post-employment benefit obligations** — frequently the largest liability, and
  frequently understated (FSA LM8)
- **Legal framework for default** — some jurisdictions have a municipal bankruptcy process; many do
  not, which changes recovery prospects entirely
- **Willingness to raise taxes**, which is political rather than financial

## Exam traps

> **Trap 1 — Treating all sovereign debt as risk-free.** Only **local-currency** debt of a sovereign
> that controls its own currency approaches it. **Foreign-currency** sovereign default is common.

> **Trap 2 — Forgetting currency union members.** A eurozone member does **not** control its
> currency, so its euro-denominated debt behaves more like foreign-currency debt.

> **Trap 3 — Reading debt/GDP in isolation.** Japan's is far above most defaulters'. **Interest as a
> share of revenue** and the **maturity profile** are more informative.

> **Trap 4 — Ignoring willingness to pay.** A sovereign may be **able** to pay and choose not to.
> There is no court to compel it.

> **Trap 5 — General obligation vs revenue bonds.** GO is backed by the **taxing power**; revenue
> bonds only by a **specific project's cash flows**, and therefore yield more.

> **Trap 6 — Overlooking contingent liabilities.** State-owned enterprises, banking system
> guarantees, and unfunded pensions can dwarf the reported debt.

> **Trap 7 — Assuming national government support for a municipality.** It may be **implicit**, and
> implicit support has failed before.

## Self-check

*Closed book. Commit to an answer before opening the fold.*

**1.** Country A has debt/GDP of 230% and interest payments of 4% of revenue. Country B has debt/GDP of 65% and interest payments of 28% of revenue. Which is more fragile?

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**Country B**, decisively.

**Country A** carries an enormous debt stock but services it almost costlessly — interest consumes only 4% of revenue. This is the profile of a country borrowing in its **own currency** at very low rates, from a largely **domestic** investor base, with credible monetary institutions (Japan being the obvious example). It has ample fiscal space to respond to a shock.

**Country B** has a modest debt stock but **28% of every unit of government revenue goes to interest**. That is a severe constraint: more than a quarter of its fiscal capacity is pre-committed before a single service is delivered. It implies a high **borrowing cost**, which in turn implies the market already regards it as risky — often because the debt is in **foreign currency**, the maturity profile is short, or institutional credibility is weak.

**The lesson:** debt/GDP is a stock measure with no information about the cost of carrying it. **Interest as a share of revenue is a flow measure that captures both the stock and its price**, and it is what actually binds a government's hands.

Also check: the currency composition of the debt, the maturity profile, and reserves against short-term external debt.

</details>

**2.** Why does a sovereign default on foreign-currency debt more often than on local-currency debt?

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Because the **ability to pay** differs fundamentally.

**Local-currency debt:** the government controls the currency it owes. It can create money to repay. Default is therefore a **policy choice** — a judgement that the cost of repaying (inflation, currency depreciation, loss of monetary credibility) exceeds the cost of defaulting. Governments generally prefer inflation to explicit default, so local-currency defaults are rare.

**Foreign-currency debt:** the government **cannot create** the currency it owes. It must earn it through exports, borrow it from abroad, or draw down reserves. If export earnings collapse (a commodity price fall), reserves are exhausted, and international markets close, the government simply **cannot pay** — regardless of willingness.

**The compounding dynamic:** foreign-currency debt becomes *harder* to service exactly when the currency weakens, because the local-currency cost of the foreign obligation rises just as the economy is struggling. A 30% devaluation raises the real debt burden by 43% overnight. This is why heavy foreign-currency borrowing is treated as a serious warning sign — it converts a monetary problem into a solvency problem.

**Note:** a currency union member is in the foreign-currency position with respect to its own union currency.

</details>

**3.** Contrast the analysis of a general obligation bond and a revenue bond.

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**General obligation bond** — backed by the issuer's **full faith, credit, and taxing power**. The analysis is essentially about the **tax base**:
- **Size, diversity, and growth** of the economy being taxed — is it dependent on one employer or one industry?
- **Wealth levels** and income trends of the population
- **Expenditure structure** — how much is fixed and non-discretionary?
- **Pension and other post-employment obligations**, which are frequently the largest liability and frequently understated
- **Willingness to raise taxes**, which is political rather than financial
- **Legal framework**: is there a municipal bankruptcy process, and what does it do to recovery?

**Revenue bond** — backed **only** by the cash flow from a specific project (a toll road, an airport, a water utility). There is **no general claim on the issuer**, which is why it yields more. The analysis is essentially **project finance**:
- **Demand** for the service and its elasticity — will volumes hold in a recession?
- **Pricing power** and any regulatory cap on rates
- **Operating cost structure** and maintenance capital requirements
- **Debt service coverage ratio** = net project revenue / annual debt service, against the covenanted minimum
- **Competition** — a new parallel road destroys a toll road's economics
- **Rate covenant** — an undertaking to set charges at a level sufficient to cover debt service by a stated margin

The practical difference: a GO bond is a credit on a **community**; a revenue bond is a credit on a **business**.

</details>
