## Core concepts

### Why this module is first

Every LOS here uses the verb *describe*. There is nothing to calculate. That makes it tempting to
skim — don't. This module installs the vocabulary and the mental filing system that the next eleven
modules hang off. Four hours, mostly memorisation, high return.

### The role of financial statement analysis

Financial statements are produced by the **company**, audited by an **external auditor**, and used
by an **analyst** who was in the room for none of it. That asymmetry is the whole subject.

The analyst's job is to convert general-purpose financial reports into an input for a specific
economic decision:

- **Equity analysis** — is the security mispriced? Forecast earnings and cash flow, then value.
- **Credit analysis** — will this issuer pay? Assess capacity (cash flow, leverage, coverage) and
  willingness.
- **Other uses** — M&A due diligence, assessing a subsidiary, evaluating a supplier or customer,
  regulatory and internal assessment.

The key framing: statements tell you about **past performance** and **current financial position**;
the analyst uses them to form a view about the **future**. Everything in FSA is in service of that
translation.

### The financial statement analysis framework (six steps)

You will be asked to identify a step, or put steps in order. Memorise it.

| # | Step | Output |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | **State the objective and context** | What question am I answering? For whom? |
| 2 | **Gather data** | Statements, filings, questionnaires, industry and economic data |
| 3 | **Process the data** | Adjusted statements, common-size statements, ratios, graphs, forecasts |
| 4 | **Analyze / interpret** | Analytical results — the *meaning* of the processed data |
| 5 | **Develop and communicate conclusions** | A report, a recommendation, a credit opinion |
| 6 | **Follow up** | Repeat periodically; revise as new information arrives |

> A useful mnemonic: **S**tate, **G**ather, **P**rocess, **A**nalyze, **C**ommunicate, **F**ollow up.
> Note that **processing** and **analyzing** are distinct steps — computing a ratio is step 3;
> concluding that leverage is unsustainable is step 4. The exam tests that distinction.

### The major financial statements

| Statement | Answers | Nature |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Balance sheet** (statement of financial position) | What does it own and owe *right now*? | A snapshot at a point in time |
| **Income statement** | How profitable was it over the period? | A flow over a period |
| **Statement of comprehensive income** | Profit *plus* items bypassing P&L (OCI) | A flow over a period |
| **Statement of cash flows** | Where did cash actually come from and go? | A flow over a period |
| **Statement of changes in equity** | How did owners' claims change? | A flow over a period |

Only the balance sheet is a snapshot. Everything else covers a period — which is why ratios mixing
the two (e.g. inventory turnover = COGS ÷ *average* inventory) use an **average** balance.

### The supporting information — where the real analysis lives

The statements themselves are the least informative part of an annual report. The analyst's
material is around them.

**Financial statement notes (footnotes).** Audited, and part of the statements. They disclose:
accounting policies and methods, the estimates and assumptions behind reported numbers, business
acquisitions and disposals, legal proceedings, segment data, and subsequent events. **Two companies
with identical operations can report very different numbers purely through policy choice** — the
notes are how you find out and normalise for it. This is the single highest-value section of a filing.

**Supplementary schedules.** Not always audited. Segment breakdowns, reserve disclosures (extractive
industries), reconciliations under alternative standards.

**Management's commentary** (MD&A in the US, "management report" / "operating and financial review"
elsewhere). **Unaudited**, and that is the point — treat it as informed but interested. It should
cover the nature of the business, objectives and strategies, resources and risks, results and
prospects, and the critical performance measures management uses. In the US, the MD&A must discuss
trends, significant events and uncertainties, the effects of inflation and changing prices where
material, off-balance-sheet obligations, and critical accounting estimates.

**Audit report.** An independent opinion on whether the statements are fairly presented in
accordance with the applicable standards. Learn the four outcomes:

| Opinion | Meaning | Analyst reaction |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Unqualified** (clean) | Fairly presented, no reservations | Normal. This is what you expect. |
| **Qualified** | Fairly presented *except for* a specific item | Read the exception carefully; adjust |
| **Adverse** | Not fairly presented; materially non-conforming | Statements are not usable as-is. Serious red flag. |
| **Disclaimer** | Auditor unable to form an opinion | Equally serious — usually a scope limitation |

Two further points reliably tested: an **emphasis of matter** paragraph highlights something (such
as substantial doubt about **going concern**) *without* qualifying the opinion; and under US
standards the auditor also opines on **internal control over financial reporting**. The audit gives
reasonable, not absolute, assurance — it is a sampling exercise, not a guarantee against fraud.

> **Trap:** "going concern doubt" is regularly paired with "qualified opinion" in the answer options.
> Going-concern doubt normally appears as an *emphasis of matter* within an **unqualified** opinion.

### Regulatory filings

Securities regulators (the SEC in the US, and equivalents elsewhere; IOSCO coordinates
internationally) mandate periodic filings. The US set worth recognising:

| Filing | Contents |
| --- | --- |
| **10-K / 20-F / 40-F** | Annual report (domestic / foreign / Canadian issuers) |
| **10-Q** (6-K abroad) | Quarterly, typically unaudited |
| **8-K** | Material events — acquisitions, disposals, auditor changes, director departures |
| **DEF-14A** (proxy) | Board, executive compensation, related-party transactions, matters to vote on |
| **S-1** | Registration statement for a new securities offering |
| **Forms 3, 4, 5** | Beneficial ownership and insider transactions |

The proxy statement is underrated: compensation structure tells you what management is actually
incentivised to maximise, which is a leading indicator of accounting choices.

### Alternative reporting systems: IFRS vs US GAAP

Two dominant frameworks. **IFRS** (IASB) is used in ~140+ jurisdictions; **US GAAP** (FASB) applies
to US domestic registrants. They have converged substantially but material differences remain —
inventory (IFRS prohibits LIFO), reversal of impairments (IFRS permits, US GAAP generally does not),
development costs (IFRS may capitalise; US GAAP generally expenses), and the classification of
interest and dividends in the cash flow statement.

The analytical implication is the point: **cross-border comparisons require adjustment.** You cannot
compare a LIFO-reporting US company's gross margin to an IFRS peer's without converting to a common
basis (the LIFO reserve makes this possible — LM6).

**Monitoring developments** matters because standards change, and a change in standard can move
reported earnings without any change in the underlying business. Analysts follow new standards,
exposure drafts, and company disclosures of the expected impact of standards not yet effective.

### Information sources beyond the financial reports

| Source | What it adds |
| --- | --- |
| **Earnings calls and transcripts** | Management tone, and the Q&A — where analysts ask what the filing avoided |
| **Earnings press releases / 8-K** | Earliest numbers, often with non-GAAP measures attached |
| **Investor presentations and days** | Strategy, segment detail, guidance |
| **Proxy statements** | Governance, compensation incentives, related-party dealings |
| **Corporate/ESG/sustainability reports** | Non-financial disclosures; usually unaudited |
| **Industry and trade data** | Volumes, pricing, market share — the *drivers* behind revenue |
| **Competitor filings** | The best benchmark for accounting policy and margin comparison |
| **Macroeconomic data** | Rates, FX, inflation — inputs to forecasts |
| **Credit rating agency reports** | Third-party credit view and peer comparison |

A practical hierarchy: audited statements are the most reliable but least timely; press releases and
calls are timely but least constrained; industry data is the only independent check on whether
management's story about demand is true.

## Formulas to know cold

There are no formulas in this module — it is entirely descriptive. The formula work begins in LM2.

The one thing to commit to memory as if it were a formula is the **six-step framework**:

```
1 State objective & context  →  2 Gather data  →  3 Process data
       →  4 Analyze/interpret  →  5 Conclude & communicate  →  6 Follow up
```

## Exam traps

> **Trap 1 — Processing vs. analyzing.** Computing ratios and building common-size statements is
> **step 3 (process)**. Drawing a conclusion from them is **step 4 (analyze)**. Questions deliberately
> describe an activity and ask which step it belongs to.

> **Trap 2 — Audited vs. unaudited.** The **notes are audited** and are part of the financial
> statements. **MD&A is not audited.** Supplementary schedules may or may not be. This distinction is
> asked directly.

> **Trap 3 — Going concern.** Substantial doubt about going concern typically appears as an
> *emphasis of matter* paragraph in an **unqualified** opinion — not as a qualified opinion.

> **Trap 4 — "Reasonable assurance".** An audit provides *reasonable*, not absolute, assurance, and
> is not designed to detect all fraud. Any answer implying the audit guarantees accuracy is wrong.

> **Trap 5 — Confusing statements with periods.** The balance sheet is a point-in-time snapshot;
> every other statement is a flow. This drives the use of *average* balances in ratios later.

> **Trap 6 — Assuming IFRS/US GAAP are interchangeable.** Any question about comparing companies
> across reporting frameworks wants you to say "adjust first".

## Self-check

*Closed book. Commit to an answer before opening the fold.*

**1.** List the six steps of the financial statement analysis framework in order.

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

1. State the objective and context. 2. Gather data. 3. Process the data. 4. Analyze and interpret. 5. Develop and communicate conclusions. 6. Follow up.

</details>

**2.** An analyst computes a common-size income statement. Which framework step is that?

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**Step 3 — process the data.** Interpreting what the common-size statement means would be step 4.

</details>

**3.** Which parts of an annual report are covered by the audit opinion?

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

The financial statements **and the notes**. MD&A / management commentary is *not* audited; supplementary schedules may or may not be.

</details>

**4.** An auditor concludes the statements are fairly presented but includes a paragraph noting substantial doubt about the company's ability to continue as a going concern. What type of opinion is this?

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

An **unqualified (clean) opinion with an emphasis of matter paragraph**. Going-concern doubt does not by itself qualify the opinion.

</details>

**5.** Name three differences between IFRS and US GAAP that affect comparability, and say why they matter.

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(1) **LIFO** — permitted under US GAAP, prohibited under IFRS, so COGS, inventory, and every margin and turnover ratio differ. (2) **Impairment reversals** — IFRS permits reversal of previously recognised impairments (except goodwill); US GAAP generally prohibits it, so carrying values and future earnings differ. (3) **Development costs** — IFRS may capitalise once criteria are met; US GAAP generally expenses (outside software), affecting both assets and operating margin. All three mean cross-framework ratios must be adjusted before comparison.

</details>

**6.** Why is the proxy statement (DEF-14A) valuable even though it contains no financial statements?

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

It discloses board composition, executive compensation structure, and related-party transactions. Compensation structure reveals what management is incentivised to maximise, which predicts the accounting choices and business decisions you will see reflected in the statements.

</details>

**7.** Distinguish a qualified opinion from an adverse opinion.

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

A **qualified** opinion says the statements are fairly presented *except for* one identified item — the rest is usable. An **adverse** opinion says the statements are *not* fairly presented and are materially misstated — they are not usable as a basis for analysis.

</details>
