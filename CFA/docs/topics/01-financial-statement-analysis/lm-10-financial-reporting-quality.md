## Learning Outcome Statements

*Verbatim from the 2027 Level I Topic Outlines. The candidate should be able to:*

- compare financial reporting quality with the quality of reported results (including quality of earnings, cash flow, and balance sheet items)
- describe a spectrum for assessing financial reporting quality
- explain the difference between conservative and aggressive accounting
- describe motivations that might cause management to issue financial reports that are not high quality and conditions that are conducive to issuing low-quality, or even fraudulent, financial reports
- describe mechanisms that discipline financial reporting quality and the potential limitations of those mechanisms
- describe presentation choices, including non-GAAP measures, that could be used to influence an analyst's opinion
- describe accounting methods (choices and estimates) that could be used to manage earnings, cash flow, and balance sheet items
- describe accounting warning signs and methods for detecting manipulation of information in financial reports

## Core concepts

### Two different questions

Candidates conflate these constantly, and the first LOS exists to separate them.

| | **Financial reporting quality** | **Quality of reported results (earnings quality)** |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Asks | Is the *reporting* decision-useful — relevant and faithfully representative? | Are the *results themselves* good — sustainable, adequate, and cash-backed? |
| About | The **information** | The **performance** |
| Example of low | Fabricated revenue | Honestly reported, genuinely terrible earnings |

The two combine into a 2×2, and only one cell is safe:

| | **High earnings quality** | **Low earnings quality** |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **High reporting quality** | Best case: good results, faithfully reported | Bad results, honestly reported — **still useful to an analyst** |
| **Low reporting quality** | Unknowable — you cannot trust the numbers either way | Worst case |

> The key insight: **reporting quality is the binding constraint.** If reporting quality is low, you
> cannot assess earnings quality at all, because the inputs are unreliable. Low reporting quality is
> therefore always worse than low earnings quality.

### The quality spectrum

From best to worst, as a continuum, not four discrete boxes:

| Level | Description |
| --- | --- |
| **1. GAAP-compliant, decision-useful, sustainable and adequate returns** | The ideal |
| **2. GAAP-compliant, decision-useful, but low-quality earnings** | Honest reporting of poor or unsustainable results |
| **3. Within GAAP, but biased choices** | Aggressive or conservative estimates; earnings smoothing. Technically compliant, decreasingly useful |
| **4. Within GAAP, but "earnings management"** | Deliberately structuring real transactions or choosing estimates to hit a target |
| **5. Non-compliant accounting** | Departs from the standards |
| **6. Fictitious transactions** | Fraud |

The important boundary is between **3/4 and 5**: a great deal of low-quality reporting is entirely
legal. Waiting for an auditor to find non-compliance means missing everything above that line.

### Conservative vs. aggressive accounting

**Aggressive** choices increase current-period reported performance: recognise revenue earlier,
defer expenses, capitalise rather than expense, assume longer useful lives, under-provide for bad
debts and warranties.

**Conservative** choices do the opposite.

Two points candidates get wrong:

1. **Conservative is not automatically "good quality".** Persistent excessive conservatism —
   overstating provisions in a good year to release them in a bad one — is **cookie-jar reserving**.
   It is earnings *smoothing*, and it is just as much a distortion of the reported trend.
2. **Bias in either direction reduces quality**, because reporting should be **neutral**. The
   IFRS Conceptual Framework explicitly requires neutrality as part of faithful representation.

The **big bath** is the aggressive-conservative hybrid: in a year already lost, take every possible
charge at once (restructuring, impairment, write-downs). Current-year results were bad anyway, and
the depressed asset base flatters every subsequent year's ROA and net income.

### Motivations and conditions — the fraud triangle

Level I frames this as three conditions that together make low-quality reporting likely.

| Condition | What it looks like |
| --- | --- |
| **Motivation / incentive / pressure** | Meet analyst consensus; hit debt covenants; earn a compensation bonus or trigger an option vest; support the share price ahead of an issuance or acquisition; career self-preservation |
| **Opportunity** | Weak internal controls; a passive or unqualified board and audit committee; a dominant CEO/founder with no check; complex structures; standards that allow wide discretion |
| **Rationalisation** | "Everyone in the industry does it"; "it's only timing, it will reverse next quarter"; "it's temporary until business recovers" |

All three usually have to be present. Opportunity is the one an outside analyst can actually assess
before the fact — which is why governance quality is a leading indicator of reporting quality.

### Disciplining mechanisms, and their limits

| Mechanism | Limitation |
| --- | --- |
| **Securities regulators** (SEC and equivalents) | Resource-constrained; act mostly **after the fact** |
| **Auditors** | **Reasonable**, not absolute, assurance; sample-based; **paid by the company they audit**; cannot easily detect collusion or management override |
| **Standard setters** | Standards are principles requiring judgement; always lag new transaction types |
| **Private contracting** (covenants) | Creates its *own* incentive to manage earnings to stay in compliance |
| **Boards and audit committees** | Only as effective as their independence and expertise |
| **Short sellers, analysts, the press** | No authority, and frequently ignored for years |

The auditor limitation is the one most often tested: the audit is designed to give **reasonable
assurance** that the statements are free of material misstatement, not to detect all fraud.

### Presentation choices and non-GAAP measures

Non-GAAP measures (adjusted EBITDA, adjusted EPS, "core" earnings, free cash flow definitions) are
not inherently illegitimate — but they are **unaudited, undefined, and chosen by the company**.

Warning signs:

- **Exclusions that recur every year.** "Non-recurring restructuring charges" for five consecutive
  years are recurring operating costs.
- **Asymmetry.** Excluding charges but keeping gains.
- **Excluding share-based compensation** — non-cash, but a genuine cost to shareholders (LM8).
- **Definitions that change** between periods, or differ from peers.
- **The adjusted figure given prominence** over the statutory one in the release and presentation.
- **A widening gap** between the GAAP and non-GAAP number over time.

Regulators require reconciliation to the nearest GAAP measure and prohibit giving non-GAAP figures
undue prominence. **Read the reconciliation, not the headline.**

### Accounting choices used to manage earnings, cash flow, and the balance sheet

**Earnings:**
- Revenue: timing of recognition, bill-and-hold, channel stuffing, **gross vs. net** (principal/agent)
- Expenses: capitalise vs. expense; useful lives and salvage values; bad debt and warranty estimates
- Inventory: cost-flow method; **LIFO liquidation**
- Provisions: over-provide then release (cookie jar)
- Deferred tax **valuation allowance** releases
- **Big bath** restructuring charges

**Cash flow — specifically CFO**, because analysts trust it:
- **Stretching payables** (delaying supplier payment) boosts CFO with no economic improvement
- **Securitising or factoring receivables** converts a slow-collecting asset into immediate CFO
- **Capitalising** costs a peer expenses moves the outflow from CFO to CFI
- Classifying interest paid as **financing** under IFRS
- Shifting the timing of discretionary payments across the period end

**Balance sheet:**
- Off-balance-sheet structures and unconsolidated entities
- Classification of financial instruments (**FVOCI vs FVPL**) to route volatility away from earnings
- Understating provisions and contingent liabilities
- Aggressive fair-value estimates for illiquid (Level 3) assets

### Warning signs — the screening checklist

Run these as a routine on any company you cover:

| Signal | What it suggests |
| --- | --- |
| **Revenue growing faster than receivables collection** (rising DSO) | Revenue recognised but not collected |
| **Net income persistently above CFO** | Accruals doing the work; earnings not cash-backed |
| **Inventory growing faster than sales** (rising DOH) | Obsolescence and a future write-down |
| **Repeated "non-recurring" charges** | They are recurring |
| **Frequent changes in estimates**, especially useful lives | Estimate management |
| **A widening GAAP vs non-GAAP gap** | Increasing reliance on exclusions |
| **Fourth-quarter surprises** | Plugs and true-ups in the unaudited-until-year-end quarter |
| **Auditor or CFO resignation**, or a change of auditor | Serious. One of the strongest single signals |
| **A restatement** | The reporting already failed once |
| **Unusually smooth earnings in a cyclical industry** | Smoothing |
| **Barely beating consensus every single quarter** | Management, not performance |
| **Related-party transactions** (per the proxy) | Value leakage and opacity |
| **Low ETR sustained by one-off items** | Non-recurring boost being extrapolated |

**The single highest-value detection method** is the **accruals test**: compare net income to CFO
over several years. Accruals must reverse. A company with large, persistent, and *growing* positive
accruals — net income well above cash — is on a path that ends in either a slowdown or a
restatement. This is the empirical regularity behind the whole module.

## Formulas to know cold

```
Aggregate accruals = Net income − CFO − CFI       (balance-sheet-independent version: uses both)
Simple accruals proxy = Net income − CFO

Accruals ratio (balance sheet approach)
    = (NOA_end − NOA_begin) / Average net operating assets
      where NOA = (Total assets − Cash) − (Total liabilities − Total debt)

Accruals ratio (cash flow approach)
    = (Net income − CFO − CFI) / Average net operating assets

Interpretation: a HIGH and RISING accruals ratio = lower earnings quality
                (more of reported income is accrual-based, less is cash-backed)
```

## Exam traps

> **Trap 1 — Reporting quality vs. earnings quality.** A company can report **terrible results with
> excellent reporting quality**. Low *reporting* quality is always worse, because it makes the
> results unassessable.

> **Trap 2 — Conservative = good.** No. **Neutrality** is the standard. Excessive conservatism
> (cookie-jar reserving) is earnings management in the other direction.

> **Trap 3 — Thinking manipulation requires breaking the rules.** Most of the spectrum's low-quality
> territory is **fully GAAP-compliant**. Waiting for non-compliance means missing almost everything.

> **Trap 4 — Trusting CFO unconditionally.** CFO is harder to manipulate than net income, not
> impossible. **Stretching payables, factoring receivables, and capitalising costs** all inflate it.

> **Trap 5 — Treating non-GAAP measures as illegitimate per se.** They can be informative. The test
> is whether exclusions are genuinely non-recurring, symmetric, consistently defined, and reconciled.

> **Trap 6 — Auditor as guarantor.** The audit gives **reasonable** assurance on material
> misstatement. It is sample-based, and the auditor is paid by the client.

> **Trap 7 — The big bath looks like bad news only.** It depresses one year and **flatters several
> following years** through lower depreciation and a smaller asset base.

## Self-check

*Closed book. Commit to an answer before opening the fold.*

**1.** A company reports a large loss, fully and transparently disclosed, with every estimate neutral. Rate its reporting quality and its earnings quality.

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**Reporting quality: high.** **Earnings quality: low.**

This is a perfectly analysable company — you can trust the numbers and act on them. It belongs in the 'GAAP-compliant, decision-useful, but low-quality earnings' level of the spectrum. Contrast it with a company reporting strong profits through aggressive estimates: better earnings, far worse reporting quality, and **less useful** to an analyst because you cannot rely on any of it.

</details>

**2.** Over four years, net income grew 12% a year while CFO was flat and receivables grew 22% a year. What is the accruals story and what do you expect next?

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Accruals are large, positive, and **growing** — an increasing share of reported income is not cash-backed, and the receivables growth identifies where it is sitting.

Accruals must eventually reverse. Expect one of: (a) a **sharp slowdown** in reported earnings as the accruals unwind, (b) a large **bad-debt provision or write-off** as the receivables prove uncollectable, or (c) a **restatement** if recognition was not supportable.

This is the single highest-value screen in the module. Check DSO explicitly, and read the revenue recognition policy in the notes.

</details>

**3.** Name three ways a company can inflate CFO without any improvement in the business.

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(1) **Stretch payables** — delay supplier payments past period end. CFO rises, nothing improves, and it reverses next period.
(2) **Factor or securitise receivables** — sell the receivable for immediate cash, converting a future operating collection into CFO today.
(3) **Capitalise costs** a peer would expense — moves the outflow from CFO to CFI, raising CFO and net income simultaneously.

Also: classifying interest paid as **financing** under IFRS, and timing discretionary payments around the period end. This is why CFO, though more robust than net income, is not manipulation-proof.

</details>

**4.** A company excludes 'restructuring charges' from adjusted EPS in each of the last five years. What is your conclusion?

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

If a charge occurs every year for five years, it is **not non-recurring** — it is a recurring cost of running this business, and excluding it **overstates sustainable earnings**.

Treat the GAAP figure as the base, not the adjusted one. Quantify the cumulative exclusion: five years of 'one-off' charges often sums to a material fraction of cumulative reported adjusted profit. Also check whether gains are being excluded symmetrically — they usually are not.

</details>

**5.** State the three conditions of the fraud triangle and say which one an outside analyst can best assess before the fact.

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**Motivation/incentive/pressure**, **opportunity**, and **rationalisation**.

An outsider can best assess **opportunity** — it is observable from public disclosure: board independence and expertise, audit committee composition, internal control reports, the concentration of power in a dominant CEO or founder, complexity of the corporate structure, and related-party transactions in the proxy.

Incentives are partly observable too (compensation structure in the proxy, covenant proximity). Rationalisation is essentially unobservable from outside. That is why **governance assessment is a leading indicator of reporting quality** — it is the one leg of the triangle you can see coming.

</details>

**6.** Why is a change of auditor, or the resignation of a CFO, one of the strongest single warning signs?

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Both represent an **informed insider choosing to leave** — someone with full access to the books deciding the association is not worth continuing. An auditor resignation in particular usually follows a dispute over a reporting position that the auditor lost or refused to accept.

Neither is proof of anything, and there are innocent explanations (fee disputes, a genuine career move, a merger of audit firms). But the base rate of subsequent restatements following auditor changes and abrupt CFO departures is high enough that it warrants immediate investigation: read the 8-K, look for a disclosed disagreement, and check whether the successor auditor is a step down in size or reputation.

</details>

**7.** A company takes a very large restructuring and impairment charge in a year when it has already announced disappointing results. What is the technique, and what should you expect in following years?

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

A **big bath**. The year is already lost, so management takes every available charge at once — impairments, restructuring provisions, inventory write-downs — clearing the decks.

Expect the following years to show **artificially strong results**: lower depreciation and amortisation (smaller asset base), no further charges against over-provided reserves (which may be quietly released into income), and **higher ROA and ROE** on a reduced asset and equity base.

Normalise by adding back the excess charge and re-computing returns on the pre-bath asset base before concluding that a turnaround has occurred.

</details>
