## Core concepts

### Private equity — the stages

| Stage | Target companies | Characteristics |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Venture capital** | Early stage, often pre-revenue | Very high risk; most investments fail; returns come from a **small number of very large winners** |
| **Growth (expansion) capital** | Established, growing companies | Minority stakes, usually no control; less leverage |
| **Buyout (LBO)** | Mature, cash-generative companies | **Control** stakes, financed heavily with **debt** |
| **Special situations / distressed** | Companies in difficulty | Turnaround, restructuring, or debt-to-equity conversion |

**Venture capital sub-stages:** angel/seed (idea and team), early stage (product development, Series
A/B), later stage (scaling, Series C+), and mezzanine/pre-IPO.

> **Venture returns are driven by a power law, not an average.** In a typical VC fund, a large
> majority of investments return less than capital and a very small number return 20× or more. The
> fund's return is determined almost entirely by those few. This is why VC portfolios are
> deliberately built with many positions, and why the median VC fund underperforms while the top
> quartile dramatically outperforms — **manager selection matters more here than in any other asset
> class**.

### The leveraged buyout

**The structure:** acquire a mature, stable, cash-generative company using a large proportion of
**debt** secured on the target's own assets and cash flows, with a relatively small equity cheque.

**The three sources of return:**

| Source | Mechanism |
| --- | --- |
| **1. Operational improvement** | Raising EBITDA — cost reduction, margin expansion, revenue growth, bolt-on acquisitions |
| **2. Debt paydown (deleveraging)** | Using the company's own cash flow to repay debt. As debt falls, the equity value rises even with a flat enterprise value |
| **3. Multiple expansion** | Exiting at a higher EV/EBITDA multiple than the entry multiple |

> **Deleveraging is the mechanically reliable one**, and it is what makes an LBO work even without
> operational heroics. If a company is bought for 10× EBITDA with 6× of debt (a 4× equity cheque)
> and pays down 3× of debt over five years with no change in EBITDA or the exit multiple, the equity
> goes from 4× to 7× EBITDA — a **75% gain** on the equity from debt repayment alone.
>
> **Multiple expansion is the least reliable** and is outside the sponsor's control. Returns
> attributable to it are market beta, not skill.

**What makes a good LBO candidate:**

- **Stable, predictable cash flows** to service the debt
- **Low existing leverage** and low capital intensity
- **Strong market position** with a defensible moat
- Identifiable **operational improvement** opportunities
- **Hard assets** to secure the debt against
- A **clear exit route**

**Types:** a **management buyout (MBO)** is led by the existing management team; a **management
buy-in (MBI)** installs an external team.

### Exit routes

| Exit | Detail |
| --- | --- |
| **Trade sale** | Sale to a strategic (corporate) buyer. Often the **highest price**, because a strategic buyer can realise **synergies** a financial buyer cannot |
| **IPO** | Public listing. Potentially the highest valuation in a strong market, but the sponsor usually cannot exit fully at once — **lock-ups** and market risk remain |
| **Secondary sale** | Sale to **another private equity firm**. Fast and certain, and increasingly common |
| **Recapitalisation** | The company raises new debt to pay a **dividend** to the sponsor. Not a true exit — the sponsor retains ownership while extracting cash |
| **Write-off / liquidation** | The failure case |

### Private debt

**Lending to companies outside the public bond and syndicated loan markets.** It has grown enormously
as banks retreated from leveraged lending after the financial crisis.

| Type | Detail |
| --- | --- |
| **Direct lending** | Senior secured loans to mid-market companies, usually **floating rate** with a spread over a reference rate. The largest segment |
| **Mezzanine debt** | **Subordinated** debt, often with **equity warrants** attached. Sits between senior debt and equity |
| **Venture debt** | Lending to venture-backed companies, typically alongside equity rounds, with warrants |
| **Distressed debt** | Buying the debt of troubled companies, either to trade the recovery or to convert to equity and take control ("loan to own") |
| **Unitranche** | A single blended facility combining senior and subordinated debt at one rate. Simpler and faster than a layered structure |
| **Special situations** | Bespoke financing for complex circumstances |

**Investment characteristics:**

- **Higher yields** than comparable public debt — an **illiquidity premium** plus a complexity
  premium
- **Floating rate** in most cases, so limited interest rate duration (Fixed Income LM8)
- **Stronger covenants** than public high yield, because a small lender group can negotiate and
  enforce them — though covenant erosion has occurred as the asset class has grown
- **Direct negotiation** with the borrower, and an ongoing relationship
- **Illiquid** — typically held to maturity
- **Credit risk is the dominant risk**, assessed with the tools of Fixed Income LM16

> **The core analytical point:** private debt returns are **credit returns**, not equity returns.
> They are bounded above by the contractual coupon and exposed below to default. The asset class
> performs well in benign credit conditions and poorly in a downturn — and its short history is
> almost entirely within a benign period, which is the main reason to be cautious about extrapolating
> its record.

### Diversification benefits — and the honest caveat

**The claimed benefits:**

| Benefit | Rationale |
| --- | --- |
| **Access to companies unavailable publicly** | The vast majority of companies of meaningful size are private |
| **Low reported correlation** with public markets | See the caveat |
| **Illiquidity premium** | Compensation for accepting long lock-ups |
| **Sector and stage exposure** | Early-stage technology, for instance, is barely represented in public indices |
| **Active governance** | Control stakes allow direct operational intervention, unavailable to a public minority holder |
| **Vintage diversification** | Spreading commitments across years reduces the risk of deploying entirely into one valuation environment |

**The caveat, stated plainly:**

> **The reported low correlation is substantially an artefact of appraisal-based valuation.**
> Private capital NAVs are marked quarterly by the GP, using models anchored on prior marks. The
> return series is therefore **smoothed**, which mechanically understates both volatility and
> correlation with public equities (LM2, Portfolio Management LM1).
>
> **Economically, a leveraged buyout of a mid-market company is a leveraged equity position.** Its
> true correlation with public equity is high — arguably **higher** than 1 once leverage is
> accounted for. The diversification benefit that appears in a mean-variance optimisation built on
> reported data is largely spurious, and it disappears in a downturn precisely when it is needed.
>
> **What is genuinely there:** the **illiquidity premium**, **access** to companies and stages not
> available publicly, and the **governance** advantage of control. Those are real. The low
> correlation is mostly a measurement artefact, and an honest analysis says so.

## Formulas to know cold

```
PRIVATE EQUITY STAGES
  Venture (seed → early → later) · Growth capital · BUYOUT (LBO) · Special situations/distressed

THE THREE LBO RETURN SOURCES
  1. OPERATIONAL IMPROVEMENT — raise EBITDA
  2. DEBT PAYDOWN (deleveraging) — the mechanically reliable one
  3. MULTIPLE EXPANSION — least reliable, outside the sponsor's control (this is market beta)

  Illustration: buy at 10x EBITDA with 6x debt (4x equity). Repay 3x of debt over five years
  with flat EBITDA and a flat exit multiple → equity goes 4x → 7x = a 75% gain from deleveraging alone.

EXIT ROUTES
  TRADE SALE (often highest — synergies) · IPO (lock-ups, market risk) · SECONDARY sale
  · RECAPITALISATION (dividend, not a true exit) · write-off

PRIVATE DEBT TYPES
  DIRECT LENDING (senior secured, floating rate, mid-market — the largest segment)
  · MEZZANINE (subordinated + warrants) · VENTURE DEBT · DISTRESSED ("loan to own")
  · UNITRANCHE (blended single facility) · special situations

DIVERSIFICATION — the honest position
  REAL:     illiquidity premium · access to private companies and stages · governance/control
  ARTEFACT: the low reported correlation, caused by APPRAISAL SMOOTHING.
            An LBO is economically a LEVERAGED EQUITY position.
```

## Exam traps

> **Trap 1 — Treating the low reported correlation as real.** It is largely an artefact of
> **appraisal smoothing**. An LBO is economically a **leveraged equity** position with high true
> correlation to public equity.

> **Trap 2 — Missing deleveraging as a return source.** It is the **mechanically reliable** LBO
> driver and works even with flat EBITDA and a flat exit multiple.

> **Trap 3 — Crediting multiple expansion as skill.** It is **market beta** — outside the sponsor's
> control and dependent on exit-market conditions.

> **Trap 4 — Judging VC by the average.** Returns follow a **power law**: a few investments drive
> everything. Median VC funds underperform; top-quartile funds dramatically outperform.

> **Trap 5 — Confusing a recapitalisation with an exit.** A dividend recap extracts cash **while
> retaining ownership**. It increases leverage and is a creditor concern (Fixed Income LM16).

> **Trap 6 — Treating private debt as an equity-like return.** It is a **credit** return: capped
> above at the coupon, exposed below to default.

> **Trap 7 — Extrapolating private debt's track record.** Almost all of it falls within a benign
> credit period. It has not been tested through a serious default cycle at current scale.

> **Trap 8 — Assuming a trade sale always beats an IPO.** A trade sale often achieves a higher price
> (synergies) **and** a clean full exit; an IPO can price higher in a strong market but leaves the
> sponsor with lock-ups and residual market risk.

## Self-check

*Closed book. Commit to an answer before opening the fold.*

**1.** A sponsor buys a company for 800m at 10x EBITDA, funded with 550m of debt and 250m of equity. Over five years EBITDA grows 25%, 200m of debt is repaid, and the exit multiple is 10x. Compute the equity return and decompose it.

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**Entry:** EBITDA = 800/10 = **80m**. Debt 550m, equity 250m.

**Exit:** EBITDA = 80 × 1.25 = **100m**. Enterprise value = 100 × 10 = **1,000m**. Debt = 550 − 200 = **350m**.

**Exit equity value = 1,000 − 350 = 650m**

**Return: 650/250 = 2.6× over five years**, an IRR of about **21.1%** (2.6^(1/5) − 1).

**Decomposition of the 400m equity gain:**
- **EBITDA growth:** +20m of EBITDA × 10× multiple = **+200m** of enterprise value
- **Debt paydown:** **+200m** directly to equity
- **Multiple expansion:** **0** (flat at 10×)

**So half the gain came from operations and half from deleveraging** — and none from market conditions. This is a well-executed deal. Note how much work the leverage does: without any debt, the same EBITDA growth would have produced a return of 1,000/800 = 1.25×, not 2.6×.

</details>

**2.** Why do private equity NAVs show low correlation with public equity markets, and is that correlation real?

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**Why it appears low:** private capital NAVs are marked **quarterly** by the GP, using valuation models — comparable multiples, DCFs, or recent transaction prices — that **anchor on the previous mark** and adjust only partially toward current conditions.

The resulting return series is **smoothed**: it moves less than the underlying economic value, and it moves with a **lag**. When public markets fall 25% in a quarter, private marks might fall 8%, with the remainder appearing over the following two quarters — by which time public markets may have recovered.

A correlation computed on these two series is **mechanically low**, even if the underlying economic exposures are nearly identical.

**Is it real? Largely no.**

Economically, a **leveraged buyout of a mid-market company is a leveraged equity position**. It has the same exposure to economic growth, credit conditions, and equity risk premia as a public company — with **more** leverage. Its true correlation with public equity is high, and once leverage is accounted for, its true **beta may exceed 1**.

**What follows:**
- A mean-variance optimisation using reported private equity data will **over-allocate** to it, because the inputs understate both risk and correlation
- The diversification benefit **disappears in a downturn**, exactly when it is needed — private marks eventually follow public ones down
- **Un-smoothing** the return series, or using public-market proxies with comparable leverage, gives a far more honest input

**What IS real:** the **illiquidity premium**, **access** to companies and stages absent from public indices, and the **governance** advantage of control. Those justify an allocation. The low correlation does not.

</details>

**3.** Contrast direct lending with public high-yield bonds.

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**Direct lending:**
- **Senior secured**, typically first lien, with a claim on the borrower's assets
- **Floating rate** — a spread over a reference rate, so minimal interest rate duration (Fixed Income LM8)
- **Stronger covenants**, including maintenance covenants, because a small lender group can negotiate and enforce them
- **Direct negotiation** with the borrower and an ongoing relationship — the lender can engage early if performance deteriorates
- **Illiquid** — typically held to maturity, with no secondary market
- **Higher yield** — an illiquidity premium plus a complexity premium
- **Mid-market borrowers**, generally smaller and less diversified than public bond issuers

**Public high yield:**
- Usually **unsecured** or second lien
- **Fixed rate**, so full interest rate duration
- **Lighter covenants**, and increasingly covenant-lite, because a dispersed bondholder base cannot coordinate
- **No borrower relationship** — the trustee acts for a dispersed group
- **Liquid**, with observable market prices
- **Lower yield** for comparable credit quality
- **Larger issuers**, generally with more diversified operations

**The trade-off:** direct lending offers better **structural protection** (seniority, security, covenants) and a higher yield, at the cost of **illiquidity** and exposure to **smaller, less diversified borrowers**.

**The caution:** direct lending's track record is almost entirely within a **benign credit period** with very low default rates. It has not been tested through a serious downturn at anything like its current scale, and its marks are appraisal-based, so deterioration would appear with a lag.

</details>

**4.** Why does venture capital manager selection matter more than in any other asset class?

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Because **venture returns follow a power law**, and the dispersion between top and bottom managers is extreme.

**The return distribution within a fund:** in a typical VC portfolio, a large majority of investments return **less than the capital invested**, and a very small number — often one or two — return **20× or more**. The fund's entire return is determined by whether it caught those few.

**The dispersion across funds is correspondingly enormous.** The gap between top-quartile and bottom-quartile VC funds is measured in **tens of percentage points of IRR** — far wider than in buyout, real estate, or public equity, where the top and bottom quartiles are separated by a few percentage points.

**Why the dispersion persists:**
- **Deal flow is not evenly available.** The best founders choose their investors, and established firms with strong reputations see opportunities that others never do. This is a genuine, self-reinforcing advantage.
- **Access is rationed.** The best funds are oversubscribed and closed to new LPs, so the historically strong managers are often unavailable.
- **Skill in early-stage judgement** appears to persist more than in public equity, where the evidence for persistence is weak.

**The practical implications:**
- The **median** VC fund has historically underperformed public equity after fees. An allocation to 'venture capital' as an asset class is not a sensible proposition; an allocation to a specific top-tier manager may be.
- **Access, not analysis, is the binding constraint.** The question is not whether VC is attractive but whether you can get into the funds that make it so.
- This is the strongest case in the whole curriculum against the usual advice to focus on asset allocation over manager selection.

</details>
