## Core concepts

### Real estate — the four quadrants

Real estate exposure comes in four forms, and the distinction drives everything about liquidity,
volatility, and correlation:

| | **Equity** (ownership) | **Debt** (lending) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Private** | Direct ownership; private real estate funds | Mortgages; mezzanine real estate debt |
| **Public** | **REITs**; real estate operating companies | **CMBS**; mortgage REITs |

> **The public quadrants trade like securities; the private quadrants behave like private capital.**
> A REIT is priced continuously by the market and has high correlation with equities in the short
> run; direct property is appraised quarterly and appears far less volatile — the same smoothing
> problem as LM2.

**Property types:** residential (single-family and multifamily), office, retail, industrial and
logistics, hospitality, and specialised sectors (data centres, healthcare, self-storage, student
housing).

### Real estate investment characteristics

| Characteristic | Detail |
| --- | --- |
| **Income and appreciation** | Returns come from **rental income** plus **capital appreciation**. Income is typically the larger and more stable component |
| **Inflation hedge** | Rents often adjust with inflation, and replacement costs rise — genuinely one of the better inflation hedges, though the relationship is imperfect and weakens when rate rises accompany inflation |
| **Leverage** | Almost always used, which amplifies both return and risk |
| **Illiquidity** | Direct property takes months to transact, with high costs |
| **High transaction costs** | Agency fees, legal, taxes, and stamp duties can reach 5–8% of value |
| **Heterogeneity** | Every property is unique in location, quality, and tenancy |
| **Local and cyclical** | Driven by local supply and demand, not national aggregates |
| **Management intensive** | Requires active operational management |
| **Large unit size** | A single asset can be a substantial allocation — a diversification problem |

**Valuation approaches:**

| Approach | Method |
| --- | --- |
| **Income (capitalisation)** | `Value = Net operating income / Capitalisation rate` |
| **Discounted cash flow** | PV of projected net operating income plus a terminal value |
| **Comparable sales** | Price per square metre or per unit from recent transactions |
| **Cost** | Replacement cost less depreciation, plus land value |

```
Net operating income (NOI) = Rental income − Operating expenses
                             (BEFORE financing costs, taxes, and depreciation)

Capitalisation rate = NOI / Property value          → a LOWER cap rate = a HIGHER value

Value = NOI / Cap rate
```

> **The cap rate is real estate's version of an earnings yield** — the inverse of a P/E multiple.
> A lower cap rate means a higher price per unit of income, and cap rates compress when interest
> rates fall or when investors become more confident about rental growth. Note that **NOI is before
> financing**, so the cap rate values the **property**, not the equity in it.

### REITs

**Real estate investment trusts** are listed vehicles holding property portfolios.

| Feature | Detail |
| --- | --- |
| **Tax treatment** | Generally **exempt from corporate tax** provided they distribute most of their taxable income — typically **90%** or more |
| **High dividend yields** | A direct consequence of the distribution requirement |
| **Liquidity** | Traded on an exchange, unlike direct property |
| **Diversification** | Exposure to a portfolio of properties with a small investment |
| **Professional management** | No operational burden on the investor |
| **Transparency** | Public reporting requirements |

**REIT-specific metrics** (standard earnings measures are unhelpful because of the large
non-cash depreciation charge on property):

```
Funds from operations (FFO)          = Net income + Depreciation − Gains on property sales
Adjusted funds from operations (AFFO) = FFO − Recurring maintenance capex and leasing costs
Net asset value (NAV)                = Market value of assets − Liabilities
```

> **AFFO is the better measure** because it deducts the capital spending required to maintain the
> properties — the same maintenance-versus-growth capex distinction as Equity LM10. A REIT trading
> at a large premium to NAV on FFO but not on AFFO is a warning.

**The trade-off in one line:** REITs give **liquidity and diversification** at the cost of **higher
short-run correlation with equity markets**. They are, in the short run, priced like stocks; over
long horizons their returns converge toward the underlying property.

### Infrastructure

**Long-lived physical assets providing essential public services.**

| Category | Examples |
| --- | --- |
| **Economic — transportation** | Toll roads, airports, ports, rail |
| **Economic — utilities** | Water, electricity transmission and distribution, gas networks |
| **Economic — energy** | Pipelines, storage, renewable generation |
| **Economic — communications** | Towers, fibre networks, data centres |
| **Social** | Hospitals, schools, prisons, social housing |

**By stage:**

| Stage | Detail |
| --- | --- |
| **Greenfield** | **New construction.** Higher risk — construction, permitting, and demand risk — and higher return |
| **Brownfield** | **Existing, operating** assets. Lower risk, more predictable cash flows, lower return |

**Investment characteristics:**

| Characteristic | Detail |
| --- | --- |
| **Very long-lived** | Concessions of 25–99 years |
| **Stable, predictable cash flows** | Often regulated or contracted, with limited demand elasticity |
| **Inflation linkage** | Tariffs and tolls are frequently **contractually indexed to inflation** — one of the strongest inflation hedges available |
| **High barriers to entry** | Natural monopolies; nobody builds a second water network |
| **Regulated returns** | Often a permitted return on a regulated asset base |
| **High leverage** | Stable cash flows support substantial debt |
| **Low correlation** with other assets — subject to the usual smoothing caveat |
| **Illiquid**, with large unit sizes |

**The dominant risk: regulatory and political.**

> **Infrastructure's greatest strength is also its greatest vulnerability.** The stable, monopoly-like
> cash flows that make it attractive exist because a **government granted** them — through a
> concession, a licence, or a regulated tariff. Governments can and do **change the terms**: cap
> tariffs, impose windfall taxes, alter the permitted return, or in the extreme renationalise.
>
> The asset cannot be moved and has no alternative use. There is no competitive response available
> and no exit. **Political risk is not a tail scenario in infrastructure — it is the central risk**,
> and it is why sovereign and regulatory quality matter more here than the asset's own economics.

**Access routes:** direct ownership, unlisted infrastructure funds, listed infrastructure companies
and funds, and **public-private partnerships (PPPs)**, in which a private consortium finances,
builds, and operates a public asset under a long-term concession.

## Formulas to know cold

```
REAL ESTATE — THE FOUR QUADRANTS
                  EQUITY                          DEBT
  PRIVATE    direct ownership, PE funds      mortgages, mezzanine
  PUBLIC     REITs, REOCs                    CMBS, mortgage REITs

VALUATION
  NOI = Rental income − Operating expenses   (BEFORE financing, tax, depreciation)
  Cap rate = NOI / Value          →  LOWER cap rate = HIGHER value
  Value = NOI / Cap rate                     ← the income/capitalisation approach

  Other approaches: DCF · comparable sales · cost (replacement less depreciation + land)

REIT METRICS (standard earnings mislead because of large non-cash depreciation)
  FFO  = Net income + Depreciation − Gains on property sales
  AFFO = FFO − recurring maintenance capex and leasing costs      ← the BETTER measure
  NAV  = Market value of assets − Liabilities

INFRASTRUCTURE
  GREENFIELD = new construction → HIGHER risk and return
  BROWNFIELD = existing, operating → LOWER risk and return

  Characteristics: very long-lived · stable regulated/contracted cash flows
  · INFLATION-LINKED tariffs · high barriers to entry · high leverage · illiquid

  DOMINANT RISK: REGULATORY AND POLITICAL — the cash flows exist because a government
  granted them, and governments can change the terms. The asset cannot move.
```

## Exam traps

> **Trap 1 — Cap rate direction.** A **LOWER** cap rate means a **HIGHER** value. It is an earnings
> yield, so it moves inversely to price.

> **Trap 2 — NOI includes financing costs.** It does **not**. NOI is before financing, tax, and
> depreciation — it values the **property**, not the equity in it.

> **Trap 3 — Using net income to assess a REIT.** Property depreciation is a large **non-cash**
> charge that bears no relation to economic deterioration. Use **FFO**, and preferably **AFFO**.

> **Trap 4 — Treating REITs as equivalent to direct property.** In the **short run** REITs are priced
> like equities and correlate with them; direct property is appraisal-smoothed. Long-run returns
> converge.

> **Trap 5 — Greenfield vs brownfield.** **Greenfield is new construction** — higher risk, higher
> return. **Brownfield is existing and operating** — lower risk.

> **Trap 6 — Underestimating infrastructure's political risk.** It is the **central** risk, not a
> tail. The monopoly cash flows exist by government grant and can be altered by government action.

> **Trap 7 — Assuming real estate is a perfect inflation hedge.** Rents do adjust, but the
> relationship is imperfect — and when inflation brings rate rises, cap rates expand and values fall
> even as rents rise.

## Self-check

*Closed book. Commit to an answer before opening the fold.*

**1.** A property generates rental income of 4.2m with operating expenses of 1.5m. Comparable properties trade at a 6.5% cap rate. Value it. What happens if cap rates rise to 7.5%?

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**NOI** = 4.2 − 1.5 = **2.7m**

**Value** = NOI / Cap rate = 2.7 / 0.065 = **41.54m**

**If cap rates rise to 7.5%:**
Value = 2.7 / 0.075 = **36.00m**

**A fall of 5.54m — 13.3% — with no change whatsoever in the property's income.**

This is the real estate equivalent of a bond price falling when yields rise, and it is the mechanism by which rising interest rates damage property values. The income is unchanged; the **multiple** the market pays for that income has contracted.

Note also that this is an **unlevered** effect. With 60% leverage, the equity value falls from 16.62m to 11.10m — a **33% loss** on the equity from a 100bp cap rate move.

</details>

**2.** Why is net income a poor measure of a REIT's performance, and what should be used instead?

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Because **property depreciation is an enormous non-cash charge that bears little relation to economic reality**.

Accounting requires buildings to be depreciated over their useful lives, producing a large annual charge against net income. But well-maintained commercial property typically **appreciates** over time rather than declining — the depreciation charge reflects an accounting convention, not economic deterioration.

A REIT can therefore report **low or negative net income** while generating substantial cash and appreciating in value. Net income, and any multiple built on it, is close to meaningless.

**Funds from operations (FFO)** adds back depreciation and removes gains on property sales (which are one-off and not part of recurring operations):
```
FFO = Net income + Depreciation − Gains on property sales
```

**Adjusted FFO (AFFO)** goes further and deducts **recurring maintenance capital expenditure and leasing costs** — the real cash that must be spent to keep the properties leasable:
```
AFFO = FFO − Recurring maintenance capex and leasing costs
```

**AFFO is the better measure**, and for the same reason maintenance capex matters in Equity LM10: a REIT reporting strong FFO while under-investing in its buildings is borrowing from the future. The gap between FFO and AFFO is a useful quality signal — a widening gap means maintenance requirements are rising.

**Also used:** **NAV** (market value of the properties less liabilities), with the premium or discount to NAV as a market sentiment indicator.

</details>

**3.** Why is regulatory risk the central risk in infrastructure rather than a tail risk?

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Because **the very features that make infrastructure attractive exist by government grant, and can be withdrawn by government action**.

Infrastructure's appeal is stable, predictable, monopoly-like cash flows with high barriers to entry. But ask **why** those features exist:
- The **monopoly** exists because a government granted an exclusive concession or licence — nobody is permitted to build a competing water network
- The **stable tariffs** exist because a regulator sets them, often with an explicit permitted return on a regulated asset base
- The **inflation linkage** exists because it is written into a concession agreement

Every one of these is a **policy choice**, and policy changes. Governments have capped tariffs when consumer prices became politically difficult, imposed **windfall taxes** on energy and utility returns, revised permitted returns downward at periodic reviews, and in the extreme **renationalised** assets.

**Why the investor has no defence:**
- The asset **cannot be moved**
- It has **no alternative use** — a toll road is a toll road
- There is **no competitive response** available
- It is **illiquid**, so exiting ahead of a regulatory review is impractical
- The counterparty **is the sovereign**, and there is no court with jurisdiction to compel it (the same problem as sovereign credit, Fixed Income LM15)

**The practical consequence:** in infrastructure, **jurisdiction and regulatory quality matter more than the asset's own economics**. A mediocre asset in a stable, rule-of-law jurisdiction with a predictable regulator is often a better investment than an excellent asset where the regulatory settlement can be rewritten.

</details>

**4.** Contrast investing in a REIT with owning the same properties directly.

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**REIT:**
- **Liquid** — traded on an exchange, saleable in seconds
- **Diversified** across many properties with a small investment
- **Professionally managed**, no operational burden
- **Transparent** — public reporting requirements
- **Tax-efficient** at the entity level (generally exempt from corporate tax if distributing ~90%+ of taxable income)
- **No control** over which properties are held or how they are managed
- **High short-run correlation with equity markets** — it trades like a stock, and in a market sell-off it falls with everything else regardless of property fundamentals
- **Priced continuously**, so volatility is fully visible
- Can trade at a **premium or discount to NAV**

**Direct ownership:**
- **Illiquid** — months to transact, with 5–8% transaction costs
- **Concentrated** — a single asset may be a large allocation
- **Full control** over management, capital spending, tenancy, and timing of sale
- **Management intensive** — operational responsibility
- **Appraisal-priced**, so reported volatility is **understated** by smoothing (LM2)
- **Leverage** applied directly, at the investor's chosen level
- Potential **tax advantages** through depreciation, depending on jurisdiction

**The essential trade-off:** REITs convert an illiquid asset into a liquid one, and the price of that conversion is **exposure to equity market sentiment**. Over long horizons REIT returns converge toward the underlying property returns. Over short horizons they behave like equities — which is precisely why they provide less diversification than the direct holding appears to, and why the direct holding's apparent diversification is partly an artefact of how it is valued.

</details>
