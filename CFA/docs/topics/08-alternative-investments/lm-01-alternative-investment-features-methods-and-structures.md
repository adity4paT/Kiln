## Core concepts

### What makes an investment "alternative"

| Feature | Detail |
| --- | --- |
| **Illiquidity** | Long lock-ups, restricted redemption, no continuous market |
| **Limited transparency** | Less disclosure than public markets; positions often undisclosed |
| **Lighter regulation** | Restricted to qualified or accredited investors, so less protection is mandated |
| **Complex structures** | Limited partnerships, layered fees, capital calls |
| **Specialised expertise** | Manager skill matters far more than in index-tracking public markets |
| **Higher and more complex fees** | Management **and** performance fees |
| **Concentrated positions** | Less diversified than public portfolios |
| **Problematic historical data** | Short series, and biased (see LM2) |

### The categories

| Category | Contents |
| --- | --- |
| **Private capital** | **Private equity** (venture, growth, buyout) and **private debt** (direct lending, mezzanine, distressed) |
| **Real assets** | **Real estate**, **infrastructure**, **natural resources** (commodities, timberland, farmland) |
| **Hedge funds** | A heterogeneous set of strategies, defined more by structure than by asset class |
| **Digital assets** | Cryptocurrencies, tokens, and related instruments |

> **Hedge funds are a structure, not an asset class.** A hedge fund may hold equities, bonds,
> currencies, commodities, or derivatives. What defines it is the **vehicle** — a private
> partnership with performance fees, leverage, and the ability to go short — not what it holds.

### The three investment methods

| Method | What it is | Control | Fees | Diversification | Expertise required |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Fund investment** | Commit capital to a manager's pooled fund | **Lowest** | **Highest** — full management and performance fees | **Highest** | Lowest |
| **Co-investment** | Invest in a **specific deal** alongside the fund, in addition to the fund commitment | Moderate | **Reduced or zero** on the co-invested portion | Moderate | Moderate |
| **Direct investment** | Invest directly in the asset or company, with no intermediary | **Highest** | **None** paid externally | **Lowest** — concentrated | **Highest** |

> **Co-investment is the middle path, and it has grown enormously.** The investor keeps the fund
> relationship (and the manager's deal flow and diligence) while deploying additional capital into
> selected deals at **reduced or no fees**. The trade-off is that they must be able to evaluate the
> specific deal quickly, and there is a real **adverse selection** concern: the manager offers
> co-investment on deals it cannot or will not fund entirely itself, which may not be the best ones.

**Fund-of-funds** adds a further layer: diversification across managers and access to closed funds,
at the cost of a **second layer of fees**. The economics have become difficult as fee pressure has
grown.

### The limited partnership structure

The dominant vehicle for private capital and hedge funds.

| Party | Role |
| --- | --- |
| **General partner (GP)** | The manager. Makes investment decisions, runs the fund, has **unlimited liability** (though usually through a thin shell entity) |
| **Limited partners (LPs)** | The investors. **Liability limited to their commitment**, and they are **passive** — participating in management would forfeit limited liability (Corporate Issuers LM1) |

**The limited partnership agreement (LPA)** governs everything: the fee structure, the hurdle rate,
the investment period, restrictions on the GP, key-person provisions, and the distribution waterfall.

**Capital calls and the commitment structure:**

```
Committed capital  — the amount the LP has promised
Called (drawn) capital — the amount actually requested so far
Uncalled capital   — the remainder, which must be kept available
```

> **LPs do not pay upfront.** They **commit** capital, and the GP **calls** it down over the
> investment period (typically 3–5 years) as deals are found. The LP must hold liquidity against
> uncalled commitments, which is itself a portfolio management problem — holding cash drags returns,
> but failing to meet a capital call carries severe penalties.

### Fee structures

**The classic "2 and 20":**

```
Management fee    = 2% of committed (or invested, or net asset value) capital, annually
Performance fee   = 20% of profits above a hurdle
```

**Key terms:**

| Term | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| **Hurdle rate** | A **minimum return** the fund must achieve before the GP earns any performance fee. Typically 8% for private equity |
| **Hard hurdle** | The performance fee applies only to returns **above** the hurdle |
| **Soft hurdle** | Once the hurdle is cleared, the fee applies to **all** returns — including those below the hurdle |
| **Catch-up clause** | After the hurdle is met, the GP receives **100%** of subsequent profits until it has caught up to its full percentage of total profits |
| **High water mark** | The performance fee is paid only on gains **above the previous peak** value, so losses must be recovered before fees resume |
| **Clawback** | Requires the GP to **return** performance fees if later losses mean it was overpaid across the fund's life |

> **The hard/soft hurdle distinction is worth real money.** With an 8% soft hurdle and a 10% return,
> the GP earns 20% of the **full 10%**. With a hard hurdle, 20% of only the **2% excess** — a
> five-fold difference.
>
> The **high water mark** exists to prevent a manager being paid twice for the same gains: without
> it, a fund that fell 30% and then rose 30% would generate performance fees on the recovery despite
> the investor being underwater.

**The distribution waterfall** — the order in which proceeds are distributed:

```
1. Return of capital to LPs
2. Preferred return (the hurdle) to LPs
3. GP catch-up
4. Split of remaining profits (typically 80% LP / 20% GP)
```

**Deal-by-deal versus whole-of-fund waterfalls:** a **deal-by-deal** (American) waterfall pays the GP
as each investment exits, which favours the GP and makes clawback essential. A **whole-of-fund**
(European) waterfall returns all capital and the preferred return to LPs **first**, and is far more
LP-friendly.

### Why investors allocate to alternatives

| Reason | Detail |
| --- | --- |
| **Return enhancement** | Access to return sources unavailable in public markets |
| **Diversification** | Lower correlation with public equities and bonds — though see the caveat below |
| **Inflation protection** | Real assets in particular |
| **Illiquidity premium** | Compensation for accepting long lock-ups |
| **Access to skill** | In markets where manager skill genuinely persists more than in public equity |

> **The correlation caveat matters** (Portfolio Management LM1): reported correlations are
> **understated** because appraisal-based and stale pricing **smooths** returns. The diversification
> looks better on paper than it is, and the gap reveals itself in stress, when it is needed most.

```

  LP  = investor, LIABILITY LIMITED to commitment, must stay PASSIVE

```

## Exam traps

> **Trap 1 — Treating hedge funds as an asset class.** They are a **structure** — a private
> partnership with performance fees, leverage, and shorting — not a category of asset.

> **Trap 2 — Hard vs soft hurdle.** A **soft** hurdle pays the GP on **all** returns once cleared; a
> **hard** hurdle only on the **excess**. The difference can be five-fold.

> **Trap 3 — Forgetting the high water mark.** Without it a manager is paid twice for recovering the
> same losses.

> **Trap 4 — Assuming LPs pay upfront.** They **commit**; the GP **calls** capital over time. Uncalled
> commitments must be funded on demand.

> **Trap 5 — Missing that LPs must stay passive.** Participating in management can **forfeit limited
> liability** (Corporate Issuers LM1).

> **Trap 6 — Ignoring the adverse selection risk in co-investment.** GPs offer co-investment on deals
> they cannot or will not fund fully themselves.

> **Trap 7 — Trusting reported correlations.** Appraisal and stale pricing **understate** both
> volatility and correlation, overstating the diversification benefit.

> **Trap 8 — Deal-by-deal vs whole-of-fund waterfalls.** Deal-by-deal pays the GP early and makes
> **clawback** essential; whole-of-fund returns all LP capital first.

## Self-check

*Closed book. Commit to an answer before opening the fold.*

**1.** A fund charges 2% management and 20% performance with an 8% hurdle. The fund returns 12% on 100m of capital. Compute the total fee under a hard hurdle and under a soft hurdle.

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**Management fee** = 2% × 100m = **2.0m** (in both cases)

**Gross profit** = 12% × 100m = 12.0m

**HARD hurdle:** the performance fee applies only to the excess above 8%.
Excess = (12% − 8%) × 100m = 4.0m
Performance fee = 20% × 4.0m = **0.8m**
**Total fee = 2.0 + 0.8 = 2.8m**

**SOFT hurdle:** once the 8% hurdle is cleared, the fee applies to **all** profits.
Performance fee = 20% × 12.0m = **2.4m**
**Total fee = 2.0 + 2.4 = 4.4m**

**The soft hurdle costs the investor 1.6m more — a 57% higher total fee** on the identical performance. This single contractual term is worth reading carefully in any LPA, and it is precisely the kind of detail that gets skimmed.

</details>

**2.** Explain how a high water mark works and why it exists.

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**How it works:** a performance fee is paid only on gains that take the fund's value **above its previous peak**. If the fund falls, no performance fee is payable until the value has recovered past that prior high.

**Worked example.** A fund starts at 100.
- **Year 1:** rises to 130. Performance fee on the 30 gain. High water mark set to **130**.
- **Year 2:** falls to 90. **No fee** (and no rebate).
- **Year 3:** rises to 120. Still **below** the 130 high water mark → **no performance fee**, despite a 33% gain in the year.
- **Year 4:** rises to 145. Fee is charged on **145 − 130 = 15**, not on the full year's gain.

**Why it exists:** without it, the manager would be **paid twice for the same gains**. In the example above, the manager would collect a fee on the year-3 recovery from 90 to 120 — even though the investor is still 10 below where they were at the end of year 1, and has paid a fee on that ground once already.

**The limitation, which is real:** a manager far below their high water mark has **no prospect of a performance fee** for years. Some respond by taking **excessive risk** to recover quickly; others simply **close the fund and launch a new one** with a fresh high water mark. The investor is protected from double-charging but not from either of those responses.

</details>

**3.** Compare fund investment, co-investment, and direct investment for a pension fund with a growing private equity allocation.

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**Fund investment** — commit to a GP's pooled fund.
- **Highest diversification** across many deals, sectors, and vintages
- Requires the **least internal expertise** — the GP does the sourcing and diligence
- **Highest fees** — full management fee on commitments plus 20% carry
- **Least control** over which deals are made
- The default starting point for a fund building an allocation

**Co-investment** — invest in a **specific deal** alongside the fund, on top of the fund commitment.
- **Reduced or zero fees** on the co-invested portion — this is the main attraction
- Access to the **GP's deal flow and diligence**
- Requires the ability to **evaluate a deal quickly** — often on a two-week timeline
- **Adverse selection risk:** the GP offers co-investment on deals it cannot or will not fund entirely itself. Why this deal and not the others?
- Has grown rapidly as large LPs have built internal capability

**Direct investment** — buy the asset or company with no intermediary.
- **No external fees at all**
- **Full control**
- Requires **substantial internal expertise**: sourcing, diligence, structuring, governance, and eventually exit
- **Concentrated** — a small number of large positions
- Practical only for the largest institutions (major sovereign wealth funds, the biggest Canadian and Dutch pension plans)

**The typical progression:** start with funds, add co-investment as internal capability grows, and move to direct investment only at genuine scale. Moving down the list without the expertise substitutes fee saving for competence, which is a poor trade.

</details>

**4.** Why must limited partners keep uncalled capital available, and what problem does that create?

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**Why:** LPs **commit** capital rather than paying it upfront. The GP **calls** it down over the investment period — typically 3–5 years — as deals are identified. A capital call usually gives **10 business days'** notice.

**Failing to meet a call carries severe penalties** under the LPA: forfeiture of a substantial portion of the existing interest, forced sale at a discount, loss of future participation, and reputational damage that closes off access to future funds. It is treated as a serious default.

**The problem it creates — the 'cash drag' dilemma:**

The LP must hold **liquid assets** against uncalled commitments. But:
- Holding **cash** earns a low return, dragging down the overall portfolio return. If an LP holds 30% of its commitment in cash for three years, the effective return on the allocation is materially below the fund's reported return.
- Holding **liquid risk assets** (public equities) risks those assets being **down** exactly when a call arrives — and calls cluster in periods of market distress, when GPs see opportunities.

**The standard responses:**
- **Over-commit** — commit more than the target allocation, relying on distributions from older funds to fund newer calls. This works until a market dislocation stops distributions while calls accelerate, which is precisely what caught many institutions in 2008.
- Maintain a **credit facility** to bridge calls
- **Vintage-year diversification** — stagger commitments so calls and distributions offset

**The analytical point:** the fund's reported IRR is computed on **called** capital. The LP's actual return on the **committed** allocation is lower, because of the cash held against uncalled amounts. This gap is real and is frequently ignored.

</details>
