## Core concepts

### Standard II(A) — Material Nonpublic Information

> **Members and Candidates who possess material nonpublic information that could affect the value of
> an investment must not act or cause others to act on the information.**

**The two-part test — both must be satisfied for the prohibition to apply:**

| Element | Test |
| --- | --- |
| **MATERIAL** | Would a **reasonable investor** want to know it before making an investment decision? Would its disclosure **affect the price** of the security? If it is ambiguous whether information is material, **treat it as material** |
| **NONPUBLIC** | Has it been **disseminated to the marketplace in general**? Information given to a select group is **not** public, however many people are in that group |

**Examples of material information:** earnings and earnings guidance; mergers, acquisitions, tender
offers, and joint ventures; new products or discoveries; changes in management; changes in auditors
or an auditor's notification of unreliable reports; events affecting the firm's capital (default,
bankruptcy, a large new issue); significant legal proceedings; and orders or contracts of unusual
size.

> **The "mosaic theory" is the key defence and a favourite exam point.** An analyst may combine
> **public information** with **non-material non-public information** to reach a conclusion that
> would itself be material — and may act on it. That conclusion is the product of the analyst's
> **skill and diligence**, not of inside information.
>
> This is precisely what research is for. The distinction is that each **input** was either public
> or immaterial; only the **synthesis** is material, and the synthesis is the analyst's own work.
>
> **Practical protection:** keep detailed **records of the sources** supporting the conclusion.

**What a member must do on receiving material non-public information:**

1. **Do not trade** — not for clients, not for the firm, not personally
2. **Do not recommend** the security, or cause others to trade
3. **Do not pass it on** ("tipping")
4. **Make reasonable efforts to have the issuer disclose it publicly**
5. If it is not disclosed, **inform supervisors and compliance**, who will typically place the
   security on a **restricted list**

> **Disclosure does NOT cure this Standard.** You cannot resolve possession of inside information by
> telling the client you have it. The required remedy is **abstention**. This is the clearest example
> of the LM2 point that different Standards require different remedies.

**Recommended procedures for firms:**

| Procedure | Purpose |
| --- | --- |
| **Information barriers ("firewalls")** | Physically and procedurally separate departments that receive inside information (investment banking) from those that trade and advise (research, asset management) |
| **Restricted list** | Securities on which the firm will not trade or issue recommendations |
| **Watch list** | A confidential list monitored by compliance, with very limited circulation |
| **Rumour list** | Securities subject to market rumours, monitored for unusual activity |
| **Review of employee and proprietary trading** | Detect trading ahead of announcements |
| **Documented control of interdepartmental communications** | Record who was given what |
| **Prohibition on personnel overlap** between departments | |

> **A "wall-crossing" procedure** is the controlled exception: an individual may be brought across
> the barrier for a specific transaction, with compliance recording it, and is then restricted from
> trading the relevant security until the information is public.

### Standard II(B) — Market Manipulation

> **Members and Candidates must not engage in practices that distort prices or artificially inflate
> trading volume with the intent to mislead market participants.**

**Two types:**

| Type | Mechanism | Examples |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Information-based** | Spreading **false or misleading information** to move prices | Pump-and-dump schemes; spreading false rumours; publishing a misleading report to move a position you hold |
| **Transaction-based** | **Trades designed to mislead** rather than to invest | **Wash trading** (buying and selling with yourself to create apparent volume); **matched orders** with a colluding party; **marking the close** (trading late in the session to set a favourable closing price); **painting the tape** |

> **INTENT is the defining element.** Large trades, or trades that move the price, are not
> manipulation. Trading strategies that legitimately exploit market inefficiencies are not
> manipulation. **The violation requires an intent to deceive** — to create a false impression of
> price or volume in order to mislead others.
>
> This is what distinguishes aggressive but legitimate trading from a breach.

**What is NOT manipulation:**

- Legitimate trading strategies that exploit genuine inefficiencies
- Large orders that move the price because of their size
- Providing liquidity as a market maker
- Widely publicising a **genuinely held and supportable** investment view

**What IS manipulation:**

- Publishing a research report you know to be misleading, to benefit a position
- Trading to set an artificial closing price (which may affect fund valuations, index levels, or
  derivative settlements)
- Creating apparent volume through wash trades or matched orders
- Spreading rumours you know to be false

> **"Marking the close" is worth understanding specifically**, because it has non-obvious
> consequences: a trade placed in the last minutes of the session can set the closing price used to
> value an entire fund, to compute an index level, or to settle a derivative contract. A small trade
> can therefore move a very large amount of value — which is why it attracts regulatory attention
> out of proportion to the trade's size.

**Recommended procedures:** a clear firm policy prohibiting manipulation; surveillance of trading
patterns, particularly near the close and near period ends; review of research for accuracy and
support; documented rationale for unusual trading; and training on what distinguishes legitimate
strategy from manipulation.

## Formulas to know cold

```
II(A) MATERIAL NONPUBLIC INFORMATION — the TWO-PART test, BOTH required
  MATERIAL   — a reasonable investor would want to know; disclosure would affect the PRICE
               IF AMBIGUOUS, TREAT IT AS MATERIAL
  NONPUBLIC  — not yet DISSEMINATED TO THE MARKETPLACE IN GENERAL
               (a select group, however large, is NOT public)

  WHAT TO DO:  do NOT trade · do NOT recommend · do NOT tip
               · urge the ISSUER to disclose publicly · inform COMPLIANCE
               → the security goes on the RESTRICTED LIST

  DISCLOSURE DOES NOT CURE THIS. The remedy is ABSTENTION.

  MOSAIC THEORY — combining PUBLIC + NON-MATERIAL NON-PUBLIC information into a
  material conclusion is PERMITTED. It is the analyst's own work.
  → KEEP RECORDS OF SOURCES.

  FIRM PROCEDURES: information barriers (firewalls) · RESTRICTED list · WATCH list
  · rumour list · review of employee and proprietary trading · wall-crossing procedures

II(B) MARKET MANIPULATION — INTENT TO MISLEAD is the defining element
  INFORMATION-BASED — false or misleading information (pump-and-dump, false rumours)
  TRANSACTION-BASED — WASH TRADING · MATCHED ORDERS · MARKING THE CLOSE · painting the tape

  NOT manipulation: legitimate strategies exploiting inefficiencies · large orders that move
  price by size · market making · publicising a genuinely held, supportable view
```

## Exam traps

> **Trap 1 — Thinking disclosure cures inside information.** It does **not**. The remedy for II(A)
> is **ABSTENTION** — do not trade, do not recommend, do not tip.

> **Trap 2 — Treating information given to a large group as public.** **Nonpublic means not
> disseminated to the marketplace in general.** A selective disclosure to 200 analysts is still
> non-public.

> **Trap 3 — Missing the mosaic theory.** Combining **public** and **non-material non-public**
> information into a material conclusion is **permitted** — it is the analyst's own work. Keep
> source records.

> **Trap 4 — Resolving ambiguity the wrong way.** If it is unclear whether information is material,
> **treat it as material**.

> **Trap 5 — Forgetting the affirmative duty.** The member must make **reasonable efforts to have
> the issuer disclose** the information publicly, and must inform compliance.

> **Trap 6 — Missing that intent defines manipulation.** Large trades and aggressive strategies are
> **not** manipulation. The violation requires an **intent to mislead**.

> **Trap 7 — Underestimating marking the close.** A small trade near the close can set the price
> used to value a fund, compute an index, or settle a derivative — which is why it is treated so
> seriously.

> **Trap 8 — Confusing a restricted list with a watch list.** The **restricted list** blocks trading
> and recommendations. The **watch list** is confidential, narrowly circulated, and used for
> monitoring.

## Self-check

*Closed book. Commit to an answer before opening the fold.*

**1.** An analyst visits several suppliers and customers of a company and, from many small non-material observations, concludes that the company will badly miss its earnings guidance. May she act on this?

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**Yes — this is the MOSAIC THEORY, and it is permitted.**

The analyst has combined **public information** and **non-material non-public information** to reach a conclusion that is itself material. Crucially:
- **Each individual input** was either public, or non-public but **not material** on its own
- **The material conclusion is the product of her own skill, diligence, and synthesis**

This is exactly what investment research is **for**. If analysts could not draw material conclusions from immaterial inputs, fundamental research would be impossible and markets would be less efficient, not more.

**The contrast that defines the boundary:** if a company insider had **told** her the company would miss guidance, that single input would be **material and non-public**, and she would be prohibited from acting.

**What she must do to protect herself:**
- **Keep detailed records of her sources** and the analytical steps — the file is the evidence that the conclusion was built from permissible inputs
- Ensure each input genuinely was **non-material** individually. A supplier saying 'orders are down 40%' may itself be material
- Ensure no input came from someone **breaching a duty of confidentiality** — receiving information you know was improperly disclosed is not cured by the mosaic

**Recommended practice:** document the research trail contemporaneously, not after a question is raised.

</details>

**2.** A member learns from a friend at an investment bank that a takeover bid will be announced tomorrow. What must the member do?

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**Do not trade. Do not recommend. Do not tell anyone. Inform compliance.**

The information is clearly **material** (a takeover announcement moves the price substantially) and clearly **nonpublic** (it has not been disseminated to the marketplace). Both elements of the II(A) test are satisfied.

**The required actions:**

**(1) Do not act on it** — no trading for clients, for the firm's account, or personally.

**(2) Do not cause others to act** — no recommendation, no change in rating, no adjustment to a model portfolio.

**(3) Do not pass it on** ('tipping'). Telling a colleague, a client, or another friend is itself a violation, whether or not they trade.

**(4) Make reasonable efforts to have the issuer disclose it publicly.** This is an **affirmative duty** and is frequently overlooked.

**(5) Inform supervisors and compliance**, who will typically place the security on the **restricted list** so the whole firm is blocked from trading it.

**Three points that catch candidates out:**
- **Disclosure does not cure it.** Telling clients you possess inside information does not permit you to trade.
- **It does not matter that the member did not seek the information.** Receiving it unsolicited triggers the same obligations.
- **Selling an existing position is also prohibited.** Acting on the information includes getting out, not just getting in.

</details>

**3.** A trader places a series of small buy orders in the final two minutes of trading each month-end, lifting the closing price of a thinly traded stock held in the fund he manages. Is this a violation?

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**Yes — this is 'MARKING THE CLOSE', a transaction-based market manipulation under Standard II(B).**

**Why it is a violation:** the trades are not placed to acquire a position at an attractive price — they are placed to **set the closing price**, which is used to value the fund. The pattern makes the intent evident:
- **Timing** — the final two minutes, when a small order has maximum impact
- **Regularity** — every month-end, when the fund's performance is measured
- **The security** — thinly traded, so small orders move the price
- **The conflict** — he manages the fund whose valuation benefits

**Why the size of the trades is irrelevant:** a small trade in a thin stock near the close can set the price used to value the **entire position**, and therefore a large amount of value. The harm is disproportionate to the trade.

**The consequences of the conduct:**
- **Fund NAV is inflated**, misleading investors and potentially inflating **performance fees**
- It may also breach **Standard III(D) Performance Presentation** (misleading performance) and **Standard I(C) Misrepresentation**

**What would NOT be a violation:** genuinely wanting to build the position and buying near the close for legitimate execution reasons, provided the timing is not chosen to affect the mark. **Intent is the defining element** — but a pattern as specific as this one makes intent effectively demonstrable.

**Firm procedures:** surveillance of trading near the close and near period ends is a standard control precisely because of this practice.

</details>

**4.** Distinguish a restricted list from a watch list, and explain the role of information barriers.

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**Information barriers ('firewalls')** separate departments that routinely receive **material non-public information** — principally **investment banking**, which learns of mergers and financings before they are announced — from departments that **trade and advise**: research, asset management, and sales and trading.

The separation is **physical** (different floors, controlled access), **procedural** (documented and logged communications between sides), and **organisational** (no personnel overlap, no shared reporting lines into a single revenue-driven manager).

**Restricted list** — a list of securities on which the firm will **not trade and will not issue recommendations**. It is **circulated broadly** within the firm so everyone can comply. A security goes on it when the firm holds material non-public information about the issuer — typically because it is advising on a transaction.

**Watch list** — a **confidential** list with **very limited circulation**, usually to compliance only. Securities are placed on it for monitoring: compliance reviews trading in those names for unusual activity, without alerting the wider firm that the issuer is the subject of a potential transaction.

**Why both exist:** the **restricted list must be widely known** to be effective, but publishing it **signals** that the firm has inside information about those issuers — which is itself informative to the market. The **watch list** lets compliance monitor without that signal. Firms typically move a security from watch to restricted when a transaction becomes sufficiently advanced.

**A third list, the rumour list**, tracks securities subject to market rumours, for surveillance purposes.

**Wall-crossing** is the controlled exception: an individual is brought across the barrier for a specific transaction, the crossing is **recorded by compliance**, and they are restricted from trading the security until the information becomes public.

</details>
