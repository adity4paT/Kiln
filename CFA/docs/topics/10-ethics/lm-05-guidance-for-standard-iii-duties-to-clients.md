## Core concepts

### Standard III(A) — Loyalty, Prudence, and Care

> **Members and Candidates have a duty of loyalty to their clients and must act with reasonable care
> and exercise prudent judgment. Members and Candidates must act for the benefit of their clients
> and place their clients' interests before their employer's or their own interests.**

**The hierarchy, stated explicitly: CLIENT > EMPLOYER > SELF.** This ordering resolves a large share
of exam scenarios on its own.

**Who is the client?** This is the question that must be answered first, and it is frequently the
whole point of a scenario:

| Situation | The client is |
| --- | --- |
| Managing a **pension fund** | The **plan beneficiaries** — **not** the company that sponsors the plan, and not the trustees who hired you |
| Managing a **mutual fund** | The **fund itself** and its shareholders collectively — not any individual shareholder |
| Advising an **individual** | The individual |
| A **trust** | The **beneficiaries**, per the trust document |

> **The pension case is the classic trap.** The sponsoring company hires you, pays you, and can fire
> you — but the **beneficiaries** are the client. If the sponsor pressures you to invest in its own
> shares or to take risk to reduce its contributions, you must act for the **beneficiaries**.

**The components of the duty:**

- **Loyalty** — act for the client's benefit, not your own or your employer's
- **Prudence** — the **care, skill, and diligence** a prudent person would exercise. The standard is
  **not** a guarantee of good outcomes; it is a standard of **process**
- **Care** — manage assets consistently with the client's objectives and constraints

**Specific obligations:**

| Obligation | Detail |
| --- | --- |
| **Best execution** | Seek the most favourable terms available for client trades |
| **Soft dollars / client brokerage** | Client brokerage commissions **belong to the client** and may be used only to obtain **research and services that benefit the client**, not for the firm's general overheads |
| **Proxy voting** | Voting is part of managing client assets. Votes must be cast in the **client's best interest**, with a documented policy and cost-benefit analysis. **Failing to vote is a breach** where voting would benefit the client |
| **Suitability of the portfolio as a whole** | Judge holdings in the context of the total portfolio |
| **Separation of assets** | Keep client assets segregated |

> **Proxy voting and 'robo-voting' connects directly to Equity LM2.** Automatically implementing a
> proxy adviser's recommendation without independent review **delegates the judgement but not the
> duty**. It does not discharge III(A).

### Standard III(B) — Fair Dealing

> **Members and Candidates must deal fairly and objectively with all clients when providing
> investment analysis, making investment recommendations, taking investment action, or engaging in
> other professional activities.**

> **"Fairly" does NOT mean "equally".** Clients pay for different levels of service, and a firm may
> legitimately offer premium clients more frequent contact or more detailed reporting — provided
> **all clients have a fair opportunity to act** on recommendations and **no client is
> disadvantaged**.
>
> The distinction is the single most tested point in III(B).

**On disseminating recommendations:**

- Communicate a recommendation or a **change** in recommendation to **all clients simultaneously**,
  or as nearly so as practicable
- **Give clients a reasonable opportunity to act** before the firm acts for its own account
- **Changes are more sensitive than initiations** — a downgrade from buy to sell is the most
  time-critical information a firm produces, and clients who learn late are directly harmed
- **Limit the number of people with advance knowledge** of a pending change

**On allocating trades — where fairness becomes mechanical:**

| Requirement | Detail |
| --- | --- |
| Develop **written allocation procedures** in advance | |
| **Pro rata allocation** on the basis of order size | The standard method |
| **Block trades executed and allocated at the same average price** | So no account gets a better fill than another |
| **Partial fills allocated pro rata**, not to favoured accounts | |
| **No allocation by performance** or by fee level | Allocating the best fills to the highest-fee accounts is a breach |

**Recommended procedures:** limit advance knowledge of recommendations; shorten the time between
decision and dissemination; publish guidelines prohibiting pre-dissemination discussion; simultaneous
dissemination; maintain a list of clients and their holdings; develop and follow written trade
allocation procedures; disclose the allocation policy to clients; and conduct periodic reviews.

### Standard III(C) — Suitability

> **(1) When Members and Candidates are in an advisory relationship with a client, they must:
> (a) make a reasonable inquiry into a client's or prospective client's investment experience, risk
> and return objectives, and financial constraints prior to making any investment recommendation or
> taking investment action and must reassess and update this information REGULARLY; (b) determine
> that an investment is suitable to the client's financial situation and consistent with the
> client's written objectives, mandates, and constraints before making an investment recommendation
> or taking investment action; and (c) judge the suitability of investments IN THE CONTEXT OF THE
> CLIENT'S TOTAL PORTFOLIO.**
>
> **(2) When Members and Candidates are responsible for managing a portfolio to a specific mandate,
> strategy, or style, they must make only investment recommendations or take only investment actions
> that are consistent with the stated objectives and constraints of the portfolio.**

**The two distinct situations:**

| Situation | Obligation |
| --- | --- |
| **Advisory relationship with a client** | Know the client: inquire into objectives and constraints, **update regularly**, and assess suitability **in the context of the total portfolio** |
| **Managing to a stated mandate** (an index fund, a stated strategy) | Stay **consistent with the stated mandate**. The suitability question is about the **mandate**, not about individual investors in the fund |

> **The mandate case is the one candidates get wrong.** A manager running a fund with a stated
> small-cap value mandate is **not** required to assess whether small-cap value is suitable for each
> investor — that is the adviser's job. The manager's duty is to **stay within the mandate**. Buying
> large-cap growth because it looks attractive is a **breach**, however well it performs.

**Key requirements:**

- An **investment policy statement** (Portfolio Management LM4) is the practical vehicle
- **Update the IPS regularly** — at least annually, and whenever circumstances change
- Judge suitability **at the portfolio level**, not holding by holding. A volatile security can be
  suitable within a diversified portfolio
- **Unsolicited trade requests** that are unsuitable: explain the inconsistency, and if the client
  insists, either obtain written acknowledgement or consider whether the relationship can continue

### Standard III(D) — Performance Presentation

> **When communicating investment performance information, Members and Candidates must make
> reasonable efforts to ensure that it is FAIR, ACCURATE, and COMPLETE.**

**Requirements:**

- Do not **misstate** performance or mislead about what the member or firm has accomplished
- **Include terminated accounts** in historical performance — excluding them creates **survivorship
  bias** (Alternative Investments LM2)
- **Disclose whether performance is gross or net of fees**
- Disclose the **methodology** used
- Indicate clearly when performance is a **composite** rather than a single account, and when it is
  **simulated or back-tested** rather than actual
- **Do not present a partial period** in a way that implies it is representative
- Make no claim of **guaranteed** future results

> **Compliance with GIPS (Global Investment Performance Standards) is voluntary but is best practice
> and is the recommended route to compliance with III(D).** GIPS requires time-weighted returns,
> composite construction including all fee-paying discretionary portfolios, and specified
> disclosures.

### Standard III(E) — Preservation of Confidentiality

> **Members and Candidates must keep information about current, former, and prospective clients
> confidential UNLESS: (1) the information concerns illegal activities on the part of the client;
> (2) disclosure is required by law; or (3) the client or prospective client permits disclosure of
> the information.**

**Three points that are tested:**

1. **The duty extends to FORMER and PROSPECTIVE clients**, not only current ones
2. **The three exceptions are exhaustive.** Nothing else releases the duty
3. **Cooperating with a CFA Institute Professional Conduct Program investigation is NOT a violation**
   of III(E) — this is an explicit carve-out

> **Illegal activities by the client** permit disclosure but do not always **require** it. The member
> should consult compliance and, where appropriate, legal counsel. The Standard's purpose is to
> ensure confidentiality is not used as a shield for the client's wrongdoing.

**Recommended procedures:** avoid discussing client matters where they may be overheard; use secure
storage and transmission; limit access to client information within the firm; and understand the
firm's electronic information security arrangements.

## Exam traps

> **Trap 1 — Misidentifying the client in a pension scenario.** The client is the **BENEFICIARIES**,
> not the sponsoring company that hired and pays you.

> **Trap 2 — Reading "fairly" as "equally".** Different **service levels** are permitted. What is
> required is that all clients have a **fair opportunity to act** on recommendations.

> **Trap 3 — Allocating trades by fee level or performance.** Partial fills must be allocated **pro
> rata**, and block trades executed at the **same average price**.

> **Trap 4 — Applying advisory suitability to a stated mandate.** A manager running to a stated
> mandate must **stay within it**, even if a different investment looks better. The suitability
> question is about the **mandate**.

> **Trap 5 — Excluding terminated accounts from performance.** This creates **survivorship bias** and
> breaches III(D).

> **Trap 6 — Forgetting confidentiality extends to former and prospective clients.**

> **Trap 7 — Inventing a fourth confidentiality exception.** There are **three**: illegal activities,
> required by law, client permission. Nothing else.

> **Trap 8 — Thinking not voting proxies is costless.** Voting is part of managing client assets;
> failing to vote where it would benefit the client is a breach of III(A).

> **Trap 9 — Missing that soft dollars belong to the client.** Client brokerage may be used only for
> research and services that **benefit that client**, not for firm overheads.

## Self-check

*Closed book. Commit to an answer before opening the fold.*

**1.** A manager runs a corporate pension fund. The sponsoring company's CFO asks him to increase the fund's allocation to the company's own shares. How should he respond?

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**Refuse, unless it is independently justified as being in the beneficiaries' interest.**

**Standard III(A)** establishes that in a pension arrangement the **client is the plan BENEFICIARIES**, not the sponsoring company — even though the company hired the manager, pays the fees, and can terminate the mandate.

**Why the request is problematic:**
- It serves the **sponsor's** interest (supporting its share price, or signalling confidence), not the beneficiaries'
- It **concentrates risk catastrophically**: the beneficiaries' pensions and their **employment** already depend on the company. Adding their retirement assets creates a position where a single corporate failure destroys their income, their job, and their pension simultaneously
- It may breach the **suitability** and diversification requirements of the investment policy statement (III(C))

**What the manager should do:**
1. **Decline**, explaining the fiduciary duty to beneficiaries
2. **Document** the request and the response in writing
3. Refer to the **IPS** — if it specifies diversification constraints, the request may breach them directly
4. Escalate to the **plan trustees** (who have their own fiduciary duty) and to compliance
5. If pressure continues, consider whether the mandate can be retained consistently with III(A)

**The general principle: the person who pays you is not necessarily the client.** Identifying the client correctly is the first step in any III(A) scenario.

</details>

**2.** A firm sends a research note upgrading a stock to its largest clients by email at 9am and posts it to its general client website at 11am. Meanwhile the firm's proprietary desk buys the stock at 9:15am. What Standards are breached?

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**Standard III(B) Fair Dealing is breached, twice over.**

**(1) Non-simultaneous dissemination.** The largest clients received the upgrade **two hours** before everyone else, and could act on it before other clients even knew. Standard III(B) requires that recommendations be communicated to **all clients simultaneously, or as nearly so as practicable**.

Different **service levels** are permitted — premium clients may get more frequent contact or deeper analysis. But **all clients must have a fair opportunity to act** on a recommendation, and a two-hour head start destroys that.

**(2) The firm traded ahead of its own clients.** The proprietary desk bought at 9:15am — after the recommendation existed, and before most clients could act. This breaches the requirement to give clients a **reasonable opportunity to act before the firm acts for its own account**, and it also engages **Standard VI(B) Priority of Transactions**: client transactions take precedence over firm and personal transactions.

**What the firm should have done:**
- **Release the note to all clients at the same moment** — a single simultaneous distribution
- **Impose a blackout** on the proprietary desk until clients have had a reasonable opportunity to act
- **Limit the number of people** with advance knowledge of the pending upgrade
- **Document a written dissemination policy** and apply it consistently

**Note:** the fact that this was an **upgrade** (an initiation of a positive view) rather than a downgrade makes it no less serious — though **changes in recommendation**, particularly downgrades, are the most time-critical of all.

</details>

**3.** A manager runs a fund with a stated mandate of investment-grade corporate bonds. She identifies an attractive high-yield bond that she is confident will outperform. May she buy it?

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**No. Standard III(C)(2) prohibits it.**

When managing a portfolio to a **specific mandate, strategy, or style**, a member must make only investment recommendations and take only investment actions **consistent with the stated objectives and constraints of the portfolio**.

The mandate is **investment-grade corporate bonds**. A high-yield bond is outside it. The quality of the analysis and the manager's confidence are **irrelevant** — she has no authority to depart from the mandate.

**Why this is so strict:**
- Investors chose this fund **because of its stated mandate**. Someone allocating to investment-grade bonds as the defensive portion of their overall portfolio has made a deliberate asset allocation decision
- The manager's departure **silently changes the investor's asset allocation** without their knowledge or consent
- It would also **defeat the suitability work** done by whoever advised those investors
- Even if the trade **succeeds**, it is a violation — the breach is the unauthorised departure, not the outcome

**The distinction candidates miss:** the manager is **not** required to assess whether investment-grade bonds are suitable for each individual fund investor. That is the **adviser's** job under III(C)(1). Her duty is simply to **stay within the mandate**.

**What she may legitimately do:** propose a **change to the mandate** through the proper governance process, with full disclosure to investors — who can then decide whether to remain invested.

</details>

**4.** A former client asks a manager not to discuss their account with anyone. Separately, the manager discovers that a current client is using the account to launder money. What does Standard III(E) require in each case?

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**The former client: the duty of confidentiality applies fully.**

**Standard III(E)** covers **current, FORMER, and PROSPECTIVE** clients. Ending the relationship does not end the duty. The manager must keep the former client's information confidential unless one of the three exceptions applies.

**The money laundering: disclosure is permitted, and almost certainly required by law.**

The **first exception** to III(E) applies: the information concerns **illegal activities on the part of the client**. The confidentiality duty does not shield a client's wrongdoing.

The **second exception** is likely to apply as well: anti-money-laundering legislation in most jurisdictions **requires** reporting suspicious activity, often within a specified period and often with a prohibition on 'tipping off' the client.

**What the manager should do:**
1. **Consult compliance immediately** — most firms have mandated AML reporting procedures and a nominated officer
2. **Seek legal advice** where the obligation is unclear
3. **Comply with the statutory reporting requirements**, which typically override any contractual confidentiality
4. **Document** the discovery and the actions taken
5. **Do not alert the client** if 'tipping off' is prohibited — which it usually is

**The three exceptions, in full — and there are no others:**
1. The information concerns the client's **illegal activities**
2. Disclosure is **required by law**
3. The **client permits** disclosure

**One further carve-out:** cooperating with a **CFA Institute Professional Conduct Program investigation** is explicitly **not** a violation of III(E).

</details>

**5.** A firm's composite performance record excludes two accounts that terminated after poor results. Is this permissible?

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**No. This breaches Standard III(D) Performance Presentation.**

The Standard requires that performance information be **fair, accurate, and COMPLETE**. Excluding terminated accounts — particularly those that terminated **after poor results** — creates **survivorship bias**: the record shows only the accounts that stayed, which are systematically the better-performing ones (Alternative Investments LM2).

The resulting composite **overstates** what the firm actually achieved, and it misleads prospective clients about what they can expect.

**What is required:**
- **Include terminated accounts** in the historical record for the periods during which they were managed
- **Disclose whether performance is gross or net of fees**
- **Disclose the methodology** used to construct the composite
- Clearly identify **composites** as such, rather than implying a single account's record
- Clearly identify **simulated or back-tested** results as hypothetical
- Avoid presenting a **partial period** as though it were representative
- Make **no guarantee** of future results

**Best practice: comply with GIPS** (Global Investment Performance Standards). Compliance is **voluntary**, but GIPS is the recognised route to satisfying III(D): it requires **time-weighted returns** (QM LM3), composites that include **all fee-paying discretionary portfolios** meeting the composite definition, and a specified set of disclosures — precisely to prevent the selective presentation described here.

</details>
