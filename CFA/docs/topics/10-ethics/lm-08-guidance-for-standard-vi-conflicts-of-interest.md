## Core concepts

### Standard VI(A) — Disclosure of Conflicts

> **Members and Candidates must make FULL and FAIR disclosure of all matters that could reasonably
> be expected to impair their independence and objectivity or interfere with respective duties to
> their clients, prospective clients, and employer. Members and Candidates must ensure that such
> disclosures are PROMINENT, are delivered in PLAIN LANGUAGE, and communicate the relevant
> information EFFECTIVELY.**

**The three qualities of an adequate disclosure — each is a separate requirement:**

| Quality | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| **Prominent** | Not buried in a footnote or in small print at the end |
| **Plain language** | Comprehensible to the recipient, not legalistic boilerplate |
| **Effective** | It must actually **communicate** — a disclosure nobody understands has not been made |

> **This is the point the Standard is most concerned with.** A technically accurate disclosure in
> six-point type on page 47 satisfies none of the three requirements. The test is whether the client
> **actually received and understood** the information.

**Conflicts that must be disclosed:**

| To clients and prospective clients | To the employer |
| --- | --- |
| **Ownership of the securities** recommended, personally or by the firm | **Personal holdings** that could conflict with the firm's interest |
| The firm's **investment banking relationships** with a subject company | **Outside board memberships** |
| **Broker-dealer market-making** activities in the security | **Outside business activities** |
| **Material beneficial ownership** of the security | Any relationship that could **impair independence** |
| **Directorships** held by the member or firm | |
| **Referral arrangements** (see VI(C)) | |
| The basis of the member's **compensation**, where relevant | |

> **Board membership is the classic disclosable conflict.** A member serving on a company's board
> has both a **duty to that company** and a **duty to clients holding its shares**, and may come into
> possession of **material non-public information** (Standard II(A)). It must be disclosed to the
> employer and, where relevant, to clients — and many firms prohibit it outright.

**Disclosure of compensation arrangements** matters where the structure could bias advice — for
example, commission-based compensation that rewards recommending particular products.

### Standard VI(B) — Priority of Transactions

> **Investment transactions for clients and employers must have PRIORITY over investment
> transactions in which a Member or Candidate is the beneficial owner.**

**The ordering, which is the hierarchy from LM1 applied to trading:**

```
1. CLIENTS
2. EMPLOYER
3. The member's own (beneficial ownership) transactions
```

**"Beneficial ownership"** means the member has a **direct or indirect pecuniary interest** —
including accounts held for family members, trusts in which the member has an interest, and any
account over which the member has control and from which they benefit.

> **Family accounts are treated as client accounts when the family member is a fee-paying client.**
> A member managing their spouse's account as a normal client must treat it **exactly like any other
> client account** — including in trade allocation. **Disadvantaging** the family account to avoid
> any appearance of favouritism is **itself a breach of III(B) Fair Dealing.**
>
> This is a genuinely counter-intuitive point and it is tested.

**What is prohibited:**

- **Front-running** — trading ahead of a client order to benefit from the price impact
- **Trading on knowledge of a pending recommendation** before clients can act
- **Allocating favourable fills** to personal or firm accounts
- Personal trading that **conflicts with the client's interest**

**Recommended procedures:**

| Procedure | Detail |
| --- | --- |
| **Limited participation in equity IPOs** | Restricted or prohibited, because allocations are scarce and desirable |
| **Restrictions on private placements** | Requiring pre-approval, given the potential for favours |
| **Blackout / restricted periods** | No personal trading in a security around the time of a client transaction or a pending recommendation |
| **Reporting requirements** | **Duplicate confirmations** of personal trades sent to compliance, **disclosure of holdings** on joining and annually, and **pre-clearance** of personal trades |
| **Disclosure of policies** | Clients told, on request, about the firm's personal trading policies |

### Standard VI(C) — Referral Fees

> **Members and Candidates must disclose to their employer, clients, and prospective clients, as
> appropriate, any compensation, consideration, or benefit received from or paid to others for the
> recommendation of products or services.**

**The requirements:**

| Requirement | Detail |
| --- | --- |
| **Disclose to whom** | The **employer**, **clients**, and **prospective clients** |
| **When** | **BEFORE** entering into any formal agreement for services — so the client can evaluate the recommendation knowing the incentive, and assess the full cost |
| **What must be disclosed** | The **nature** of the consideration (cash, services, reciprocal referrals) and its **amount or value** |
| **Direction** | Fees **received** and fees **paid** — both directions must be disclosed |

**Referral fees are not prohibited.** They are a legitimate commercial arrangement. What is required
is **disclosure**, in advance, so the client can weigh the recommendation knowing the referrer has an
interest in it.

> **Non-cash consideration counts.** A **reciprocal referral arrangement** — "I send you clients, you
> send me clients" — is compensation and must be disclosed, even though no money changes hands.
> Similarly, services provided in lieu of payment.

**Recommended procedures:** the firm should maintain a **record of all referral arrangements**,
update disclosures **at least quarterly**, and require that disclosure be made in writing before any
engagement.

### How the three sub-sections fit together

| Sub-section | The remedy |
| --- | --- |
| **VI(A)** | **Disclose** the conflict — prominently, in plain language, effectively |
| **VI(B)** | **Prioritise** — clients first, then employer, then self |
| **VI(C)** | **Disclose** referral arrangements, **in advance**, in both directions |

> **Note that VI(A) and VI(C) are disclosure Standards while VI(B) is a priority Standard.** VI(B)
> cannot be cured by disclosure — you cannot front-run a client and then disclose it. The required
> action is to **order the transactions correctly**.
>
> This is the LM2 point again: identify which remedy the specific Standard requires.

## Formulas to know cold

```
VI(A) DISCLOSURE OF CONFLICTS
  Disclosure must be:  PROMINENT · in PLAIN LANGUAGE · EFFECTIVE
  (A technically accurate disclosure buried in a footnote satisfies NONE of these.)

  DISCLOSE TO CLIENTS: ownership of recommended securities · investment banking relationships
    · market-making · material beneficial ownership · DIRECTORSHIPS · referral arrangements
    · the basis of compensation where relevant
  DISCLOSE TO EMPLOYER: personal holdings · OUTSIDE BOARD MEMBERSHIPS · outside business
    activities · anything that could impair independence

VI(B) PRIORITY OF TRANSACTIONS — the hierarchy applied to trading
     1. CLIENTS   →   2. EMPLOYER   →   3. SELF (beneficial ownership)

  BENEFICIAL OWNERSHIP = a direct or indirect PECUNIARY INTEREST, including family accounts,
  trusts, and accounts the member controls and benefits from.

  FAMILY ACCOUNTS THAT ARE FEE-PAYING CLIENT ACCOUNTS MUST BE TREATED LIKE ANY OTHER CLIENT.
  DISADVANTAGING them to avoid the appearance of favouritism BREACHES III(B).

  PROHIBITED: FRONT-RUNNING · trading on a pending recommendation · allocating favourable fills
              to personal or firm accounts

  PROCEDURES: limited IPO participation · restrictions on private placements · BLACKOUT periods
              · DUPLICATE CONFIRMATIONS to compliance · holdings disclosure · PRE-CLEARANCE

VI(C) REFERRAL FEES
  Disclose to EMPLOYER, CLIENTS, and PROSPECTIVE CLIENTS
  BEFORE entering into any formal agreement for services
  The NATURE and the AMOUNT/VALUE, in BOTH directions (received AND paid)
  NON-CASH consideration counts — including RECIPROCAL REFERRAL arrangements.

  VI(A) and VI(C) are DISCLOSURE Standards. VI(B) is a PRIORITY Standard —
  it CANNOT be cured by disclosure.
```

## Exam traps

> **Trap 1 — Treating any disclosure as adequate.** It must be **prominent**, in **plain language**,
> and **effective**. A footnote in small print fails all three.

> **Trap 2 — Thinking VI(B) can be cured by disclosure.** It cannot. You cannot front-run a client
> and then disclose it. The remedy is to **order the transactions correctly**.

> **Trap 3 — Disadvantaging a family account.** If the family member is a **fee-paying client**, the
> account must be treated **exactly like any other** — including in trade allocation.
> **Disadvantaging it breaches III(B) Fair Dealing.**

> **Trap 4 — Missing that referral fees must be disclosed in BOTH directions.** Fees **paid** as well
> as fees **received**.

> **Trap 5 — Disclosing referral fees after the engagement.** Disclosure must come **BEFORE** any
> formal agreement, so the client can evaluate the recommendation and the full cost.

> **Trap 6 — Thinking only cash referral fees count.** **Reciprocal referral arrangements** and
> services in lieu of payment are consideration and must be disclosed.

> **Trap 7 — Forgetting board memberships.** A directorship creates a conflict **and** a risk of
> receiving material non-public information (II(A)). It must be disclosed to the employer.

> **Trap 8 — Thinking referral fees are prohibited.** They are **not** — they require **disclosure**.

## Self-check

*Closed book. Commit to an answer before opening the fold.*

**1.** A member manages client portfolios and also manages her sister's account, on which her sister pays the standard fee. A limited allocation of an attractive IPO becomes available. How should she allocate it?

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**Treat her sister's account EXACTLY like any other client account — include it in the pro rata allocation on the same basis as every other client.**

This is genuinely counter-intuitive, and it is precisely why it is tested.

**Why the sister's account is a client account:** the sister **pays the standard fee**. That makes her a client, entitled to the same treatment as any other client under **Standard III(B) Fair Dealing**.

**Why excluding her would be a VIOLATION:** disadvantaging the account to avoid any appearance of favouritism breaches **III(B)** — the sister is being treated **unfairly relative to other clients** for a reason that has nothing to do with her circumstances or objectives. Good intentions do not cure unfair treatment.

**When VI(B) WOULD apply:** if the member had a **beneficial interest** in the account — if she were a joint owner, a beneficiary, or otherwise had a **pecuniary interest** in it. Then it would rank behind client accounts in priority.

**The test is beneficial ownership, not family relationship.** A fee-paying family account with no beneficial interest to the member is a client account. An account in a family member's name in which the member has a financial interest is a personal account.

**What she should do in practice:**
- **Allocate pro rata** across all eligible client accounts, including her sister's, per the firm's written allocation procedure (III(B))
- **Disclose the relationship** to her employer under **VI(A)**
- Ensure the firm's compliance function is **aware and has approved** the arrangement
- Document the allocation so it can be shown to have followed the standard procedure

</details>

**2.** An adviser receives a fee from a law firm for each client she refers to it. Must she disclose this?

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**Yes — to her employer, to clients, and to prospective clients, BEFORE any formal agreement for services. Standard VI(C) Referral Fees.**

**Referral fees are not prohibited.** They are a legitimate commercial arrangement. What VI(C) requires is **disclosure**, so the client can weigh the recommendation knowing the adviser has a financial interest in it.

**What must be disclosed:**
- The **nature** of the consideration — cash, services, or a reciprocal arrangement
- The **amount or value**
- To the **employer**, to **clients**, and to **prospective clients**
- **BEFORE** the client enters into any formal agreement for the law firm's services

**Why the timing matters:** disclosure after the engagement is worthless. The client needs the information **at the point of decision** so they can (a) discount the recommendation appropriately and (b) understand the **full cost** of the arrangement.

**Two points that are tested:**

**(1) Both directions.** Fees **paid** to others for referrals must be disclosed just as fees **received** must be. An adviser paying for referrals has an incentive to recover that cost.

**(2) Non-cash consideration counts.** A **reciprocal referral arrangement** — 'I send you clients, you send me clients' — is compensation and must be disclosed, even though no money changes hands. So are services provided in lieu of payment.

**Recommended firm procedures:** maintain a record of all referral arrangements, update disclosures **at least quarterly**, and require written disclosure before any engagement.

</details>

**3.** A portfolio manager decides to buy a stock for client accounts. Before placing the client orders, he buys the stock in his personal account. What has he done?

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**Front-running — a violation of Standard VI(B) Priority of Transactions.**

The required ordering is:

```
1. CLIENTS   →   2. EMPLOYER   →   3. SELF
```

By trading first, he has:
- **Obtained a better price** than his clients, at their expense — his buying moves the price against the client orders that follow
- **Used client-generated information** (his own decision to buy for them) for **personal gain**
- **Placed his interest ahead of his clients'** — a direct breach of the hierarchy and of **Standard III(A) Loyalty, Prudence, and Care**

**The critical point: disclosure does NOT cure this.** Telling clients afterwards that he traded first does not make it permissible. **VI(B) is a PRIORITY Standard, not a disclosure Standard** — the required action is to **order the transactions correctly**, and no amount of disclosure substitutes.

This contrasts with **VI(A)** and **VI(C)**, which **are** disclosure Standards. Identifying which remedy the specific Standard requires is the LM2 skill.

**What he should have done:** place **all client orders first**, allow them to execute, and only then consider a personal trade — subject to the firm's **blackout period** policy, which typically prohibits personal trading in a security for a defined period around client transactions.

**The firm-level controls that prevent this:**
- **Blackout/restricted periods** around client trades and pending recommendations
- **Pre-clearance** of all personal trades by compliance
- **Duplicate confirmations** of personal trades sent to compliance
- **Holdings disclosure** on joining and annually thereafter
- **Limited participation in IPOs** and restrictions on private placements

</details>

**4.** An analyst sits on the board of a company she also covers. What are her obligations?

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**Disclose to her employer and to clients — and she should expect the firm to prohibit or restrict the arrangement.**

**The conflicts created are multiple and serious:**

**(1) Conflicting duties.** As a director she owes a **fiduciary duty to the company and its shareholders collectively**. As an analyst she owes a **duty to her clients**, who may want to sell the shares. These duties can point in opposite directions.

**(2) Material non-public information.** As a director she will **routinely receive** information about earnings, transactions, and strategy before it is public. Under **Standard II(A)** she then cannot trade or recommend the security — which makes covering it effectively impossible. Her research on the company would be permanently compromised.

**(3) Compensation.** Director's fees are **additional compensation** and engage **Standard IV(B)**, requiring **prior written consent** from her employer.

**(4) Impaired objectivity.** Her relationship with management and her stake in the company's success make genuinely independent analysis very difficult — **Standard I(B)**.

**Her obligations:**
- **Disclose the directorship to her employer** under **VI(A)**, and obtain **prior written consent** under **IV(B)** for the fees
- **Disclose it prominently to clients** in any communication regarding the company — VI(A) requires disclosure that is **prominent, plain, and effective**
- **Abstain** from trading or recommending on any material non-public information received as a director (II(A))

**The practical outcome:** most firms **prohibit** analysts from serving on the boards of companies they cover, precisely because the conflicts cannot be adequately managed by disclosure alone. The usual resolution is that she must **cease covering the company** or **resign the directorship**.

</details>
