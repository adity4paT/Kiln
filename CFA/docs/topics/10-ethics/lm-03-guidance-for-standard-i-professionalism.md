## Core concepts

### Standard I(A) — Knowledge of the Law

> **Members and Candidates must understand and comply with all applicable laws, rules, and
> regulations (including the Code and Standards) of any government, regulatory organisation,
> licensing agency, or professional association governing their professional activities. In the
> event of conflict, Members and Candidates must comply with the MORE STRICT law, rule, or
> regulation. Members and Candidates must not knowingly participate or assist in and must dissociate
> from any violation of such laws, rules, or regulations.**

**The conflict rule — memorise it:**

```
Where the law and the Code differ →  FOLLOW THE STRICTER
Where local law is LESS strict than the Code →  FOLLOW THE CODE
Where local law is MORE strict than the Code →  FOLLOW THE LAW
Where there is NO applicable law →  FOLLOW THE CODE
```

**The participation and dissociation obligations:**

- You must **not knowingly participate or assist** in a violation
- You must **dissociate** from ongoing violations — stop the activity, and if it continues, remove
  yourself from it
- **Inaction is participation** if you continue to work on the matter

**Recommended dissociation steps, in order:**

1. **Attend to the matter yourself** — stop your own involvement
2. **Report to a supervisor or compliance**
3. **Seek independent legal advice** if it continues
4. **Remove yourself** — resign or decline further involvement if necessary

> **Whistleblowing is NOT required by the Standards** — reporting to regulators or the public is
> encouraged where appropriate but is not a requirement. **Dissociation IS required.** This
> distinction is tested.

**Recommended procedures:** stay informed of changes in applicable law; review and comply with
compliance procedures; maintain current reference materials; seek legal advice when uncertain;
document any dissociation in writing.

### Standard I(B) — Independence and Objectivity

> **Members and Candidates must use reasonable care and judgment to achieve and maintain
> independence and objectivity in their professional activities. Members and Candidates must not
> offer, solicit, or accept any gift, benefit, compensation, or consideration that reasonably could
> be expected to compromise their own or another's independence and objectivity.**

**The pressures this Standard addresses:**

| Source | Example |
| --- | --- |
| **Investment banking relationships** | Pressure not to publish negative research on a banking client (Equity LM11) |
| **Corporate access** | Denial of management meetings to critical analysts |
| **Issuer-paid research** | The subject pays for the report |
| **Gifts and entertainment** | From clients, brokers, or issuers |
| **Travel and accommodation** | Paid trips to company sites |
| **Credit rating agencies** | The issuer-pays model (Fixed Income LM14) |
| **Buy-side pressure** | A large client pressuring an analyst to change a rating |

**The gift rules — the distinction that is tested constantly:**

| From whom | Rule |
| --- | --- |
| **From a CLIENT** | Permitted, but **must be disclosed to the employer**. A gift from a client for good performance is **additional compensation** — see also **Standard IV(B)**, which requires **prior written consent** from the employer for compensation arrangements |
| **From anyone else** (brokers, issuers, vendors) | Must be **modest** and must not reasonably be expected to compromise independence. **Token gifts** are acceptable; anything beyond that is not |

> **The reasoning behind the distinction:** a gift from a **client** creates a conflict with the
> **employer** (it may bias the member toward that client over others), so the **employer** must be
> told. A gift from a **third party** creates a conflict with **clients** (it may bias
> recommendations), so it must be refused unless token.

**Travel:** members should use **commercial transportation** and pay their own way. Accepting an
issuer's corporate jet or paid accommodation compromises the appearance of independence. If
commercial travel is genuinely unavailable — a remote mine site — the trip may be accepted, and the
arrangement should be disclosed.

**Issuer-paid research** is not prohibited, but requires: **full disclosure** of the funding
arrangement, a **flat fee** agreed **in advance** and **not contingent** on the conclusion, and
strict adherence to a **thorough, independent analytical process**.

**Recommended procedures:** a written policy on independence; **limits on gifts** (a stated monetary
threshold); restricting the ability of covered companies to influence compensation; separating
research from investment banking (information barriers); disclosing all relationships; and a
requirement that analysts' compensation not be tied to specific transactions.

### Standard I(C) — Misrepresentation

> **Members and Candidates must not knowingly make any misrepresentations relating to investment
> analysis, recommendations, actions, or other professional activities.**

**A misrepresentation is any untrue statement, or a statement that is misleading through omission.**

**What it covers:**

| Area | Prohibited |
| --- | --- |
| **Qualifications and credentials** | Overstating education, experience, or designations |
| **Services offered** | Claiming capabilities the firm does not have |
| **Performance** | Guaranteeing returns; presenting misleading results |
| **Plagiarism** | Using another's work, language, or ideas **without attribution** |
| **Omission** | Leaving out information that makes a statement misleading |

**Plagiarism is the most heavily tested element.** The rules:

- **Always attribute** the source of any material, data, charts, models, or language used
- **Applies to second-hand sources too** — citing a report that itself plagiarised is still a breach
- **Recognised statistical and factual sources** (e.g. widely available government statistics) may
  be used **without attribution**; **analysis and opinion may not**
- Using a colleague's work within the firm is acceptable **if the firm owns it** — but a member who
  leaves cannot take it and present it as their own
- **Omitting a source is plagiarism even if no words are copied** — using someone's **idea** or
  **analysis** requires attribution

> **Performance guarantees are prohibited.** A member may not guarantee a specific rate of return on
> an investment whose return is inherently uncertain. Stating a **contractually guaranteed** return
> on an instrument that genuinely carries one (a guaranteed investment certificate) is permissible
> — the prohibition is on guaranteeing what cannot be guaranteed.

**Recommended procedures:** provide clients with a written statement of the firm's capabilities and
limitations; maintain a **records file of all supporting materials** for reports; attribute
everything; and have a formal review process for marketing materials.

### Standard I(D) — Misconduct

> **Members and Candidates must not engage in any professional conduct involving dishonesty, fraud,
> or deceit or commit any act that reflects adversely on their professional reputation, integrity,
> or competence.**

**The breadth is the point.** This Standard reaches **personal** conduct where it reflects on
professional integrity — not merely conduct at work.

| Covered | Not covered |
| --- | --- |
| Fraud, theft, embezzlement | Personal bankruptcy **without** fraud |
| Lying on an expense claim | Lawful personal behaviour unrelated to professional integrity |
| Habitual drunkenness **affecting professional performance** | Civil disobedience in support of personal beliefs (generally) |
| Falsifying records | A personal disagreement with a colleague |
| A criminal conviction involving dishonesty | |

> **The test is whether the conduct reflects adversely on professional reputation, integrity, or
> competence** — not whether it occurred at work. Cheating on a tax return has nothing to do with
> the day job and everything to do with honesty, so it is covered.
>
> Conversely, **personal bankruptcy alone is not misconduct** — financial misfortune is not
> dishonesty. Bankruptcy arising from **fraudulent** behaviour is.

**Recommended procedures:** develop and adopt a firm **code of ethics**; disseminate a list of
prohibited conduct; make clear that ethical violations carry consequences; and check references
when hiring.

## Formulas to know cold

```
I(A) KNOWLEDGE OF THE LAW
  Comply with the MORE STRICT of law or the Code.
    Local law LESS strict than the Code  → FOLLOW THE CODE
    Local law MORE strict than the Code  → FOLLOW THE LAW
    NO applicable law                    → FOLLOW THE CODE
  Must NOT knowingly participate or assist in a violation; MUST DISSOCIATE.
  Dissociation steps: attend to it yourself → report to supervisor/compliance
                      → seek legal advice → REMOVE YOURSELF
  WHISTLEBLOWING IS NOT REQUIRED. DISSOCIATION IS.

I(B) INDEPENDENCE AND OBJECTIVITY
  GIFTS FROM A CLIENT     → permitted, but MUST DISCLOSE TO THE EMPLOYER
                            (and see IV(B): PRIOR WRITTEN CONSENT for compensation arrangements)
  GIFTS FROM ANYONE ELSE  → must be MODEST / token only
  Travel: use COMMERCIAL transportation and pay your own way
  Issuer-paid research: permitted with FULL DISCLOSURE, a FLAT FEE agreed IN ADVANCE,
                        NOT contingent on the conclusion

I(C) MISREPRESENTATION
  No untrue statements; no statements misleading BY OMISSION
  PLAGIARISM — always attribute. Applies to SECOND-HAND sources.
    Recognised statistical/factual sources → no attribution needed
    ANALYSIS AND OPINION → attribution ALWAYS required
    Using someone's IDEA without copying words is STILL plagiarism
  NO PERFORMANCE GUARANTEES on inherently uncertain returns

I(D) MISCONDUCT
  No dishonesty, fraud, or deceit; no act reflecting adversely on professional reputation,
  integrity, or competence — INCLUDING PERSONAL CONDUCT.
  Personal bankruptcy ALONE is NOT misconduct (unless fraudulent).
```

## Exam traps

> **Trap 1 — Confusing gifts from clients and from others.** **From a CLIENT: permitted, disclose to
> the EMPLOYER.** **From anyone else: token only.** This distinction is tested constantly.

> **Trap 2 — Thinking whistleblowing is required.** **Dissociation is required; whistleblowing is
> not.** Reporting to regulators is encouraged where appropriate, not mandated.

> **Trap 3 — Getting the strictness rule backwards.** Follow the **MORE STRICT** of law or the Code.
> If local law is **less** strict — or absent — **follow the Code**.

> **Trap 4 — Assuming plagiarism requires copying words.** Using someone's **idea, model, or
> analysis** without attribution is plagiarism even with no text copied.

> **Trap 5 — Thinking second-hand attribution is enough.** Citing a report that itself plagiarised
> is **still** a violation. Verify the original source.

> **Trap 6 — Over-applying I(D) to personal life.** The test is whether conduct **reflects adversely
> on professional reputation, integrity, or competence**. **Personal bankruptcy alone is not
> misconduct**; bankruptcy involving fraud is.

> **Trap 7 — Missing that inaction is participation.** Continuing to work on a matter you know
> involves a violation is participation under I(A).

> **Trap 8 — Accepting issuer-paid travel.** Use **commercial transportation** and pay your own way
> unless genuinely unavailable — and disclose if not.

## Self-check

*Closed book. Commit to an answer before opening the fold.*

**1.** An analyst works in a country where local securities law permits trading on material non-public information. The Code prohibits it. What must the analyst do?

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**Follow the Code — do not trade.**

**Standard I(A)** requires compliance with the **more strict** of the applicable law or the Code and Standards. Here the local law is **less strict** (it permits what the Code prohibits), so the **Code governs**.

**The full rule:**
- Local law **less** strict than the Code (or absent) → **follow the Code**
- Local law **more** strict than the Code → **follow the law**
- **No applicable law** → **follow the Code**

**Why the Code holds members to a higher standard:** the designation's value depends on it meaning the same thing everywhere. A member operating in a permissive jurisdiction who traded on inside information would be behaving legally and still bringing the profession into disrepute. Jurisdiction-shopping is precisely what the strictness rule prevents.

**The further obligation:** if the analyst's **employer or colleagues** are trading on such information, I(A) also requires that the analyst **not knowingly participate or assist**, and that they **dissociate** — stop their involvement, report internally, seek legal advice if it continues, and if necessary remove themselves from the activity.

</details>

**2.** A portfolio manager receives (a) a case of wine from a client pleased with performance, and (b) a weekend at a luxury resort from a broker seeking order flow. How should each be treated?

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**(a) From the CLIENT — permitted, but must be DISCLOSED TO THE EMPLOYER.**

A gift from a client creates a potential conflict with the **employer**: it may bias the manager toward favouring that client over others in the firm. **Standard I(B)** therefore requires disclosure to the employer so the conflict can be managed.

**Note the overlap with Standard IV(B) Additional Compensation Arrangements.** A gift from a client **for good performance** is effectively additional compensation, and IV(B) requires **prior written consent from the employer** before accepting any compensation that competes with the employer's interest. Where the gift is a bonus arrangement rather than a token, obtain written consent first.

**(b) From the BROKER — must be REFUSED.**

A weekend at a luxury resort is **not modest** and could reasonably be expected to **compromise independence and objectivity**. A broker seeking order flow is offering value in exchange for the manager directing client trades — which would breach the duty to seek **best execution** for clients under **Standard III(A)**.

**The distinction that resolves both cases:**
- A gift from a **client** creates a conflict with the **EMPLOYER** → **disclose to the employer**
- A gift from a **third party** creates a conflict with **CLIENTS** → **refuse unless token**

**Best practice:** a written firm policy with a stated monetary threshold, and disclosure of all gifts regardless of size.

</details>

**3.** An analyst uses a chart from a competitor's research report and a passage from a widely cited industry study, both without attribution. She also cites government inflation statistics without attribution. Which are violations?

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**The chart and the passage are violations of Standard I(C) Misrepresentation. The government statistics are not.**

**(1) The competitor's chart — VIOLATION.** Using another's work — including charts, tables, models, and graphics, not merely text — without attribution is **plagiarism**. This applies even though no words were copied.

**(2) The passage from the industry study — VIOLATION.** Using another's language without attribution is the clearest form of plagiarism. And note it would **still** be a violation if she had paraphrased: using someone's **analysis or idea** without attribution is plagiarism even with no text copied.

**(3) Government inflation statistics — NOT a violation.** The Standard permits using **recognised statistical and factual sources** without attribution. Widely available official statistics — CPI, GDP, unemployment — fall into this category. Best practice is still to cite them, but it is not required.

**The line: FACTS from recognised sources may be used without attribution; ANALYSIS AND OPINION may not.**

**Two further points that are tested:**
- **Second-hand attribution is insufficient.** If she cited a report that had itself plagiarised the original, she is still in violation. Verify the original source.
- **Work produced at a firm belongs to the firm.** An analyst may use a colleague's work internally, but cannot take it to a new employer and present it as their own.

</details>

**4.** A member is convicted of falsifying their personal tax return. A second member files for personal bankruptcy following a business failure. Are either in violation of Standard I(D)?

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**The tax fraud is a violation. The bankruptcy is not.**

**Standard I(D) Misconduct** prohibits conduct involving **dishonesty, fraud, or deceit**, and any act that **reflects adversely on professional reputation, integrity, or competence**.

**(1) Falsifying a tax return — VIOLATION.** It involves **dishonesty and deceit**. That it occurred in the member's **personal** life is irrelevant: the Standard explicitly reaches personal conduct where it reflects on professional integrity. A person who will falsify a tax return has demonstrated a willingness to be dishonest with money and with authorities — which is directly relevant to whether clients should trust them with theirs.

**(2) Personal bankruptcy — NOT a violation, on these facts.** Bankruptcy arising from a **business failure** is **financial misfortune, not dishonesty**. The Standard prohibits dishonesty, fraud, and deceit — it does not penalise commercial failure. Many capable and honest people have been bankrupted by circumstances.

**It WOULD become a violation if the bankruptcy involved fraud** — concealing assets, misleading creditors, or deliberately incurring debts without intent to repay. The distinction is **conduct, not outcome**.

**The general test for I(D):** does the conduct reflect adversely on **professional reputation, integrity, or competence**? Ask what it demonstrates about the person's honesty or judgement, not where it happened.

</details>

**5.** An analyst is invited by a company to tour its mining operation in a remote location. The company offers to fly the analyst there on its corporate jet, as no commercial flights serve the site. What should the analyst do?

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**The analyst may accept the flight, but should disclose the arrangement — and should be alert to the independence risk.**

**The general rule under Standard I(B):** members should use **commercial transportation and pay their own way**. Accepting an issuer's travel and accommodation creates an obligation, or at minimum the appearance of one, that could compromise objectivity.

**The exception:** where commercial transportation is **genuinely unavailable** — and a remote mine site with no scheduled service qualifies — the member may accept the issuer's transport as a practical necessity. The alternative would be that no analyst could ever inspect such an operation, which serves nobody.

**What the analyst must still do:**
- **Disclose** the arrangement in any research report arising from the visit, so readers can assess it
- **Limit** what is accepted to what is genuinely necessary — transport to the site, not a luxury hotel, entertainment, or gifts
- **Pay their own way** for anything where a commercial alternative exists (accommodation in the nearest town, meals)
- Remain **alert to the pressure**: hospitality creates reciprocity instincts, and the company knows this

**Best practice at the firm level:** a written policy on issuer-paid travel with a requirement to seek pre-approval and to document the arrangement.

</details>
