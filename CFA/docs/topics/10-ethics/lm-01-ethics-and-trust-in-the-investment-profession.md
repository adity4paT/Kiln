## Core concepts

### Start here, on day 1, and do one hour every day

Ethics is **15–20% of the exam** — the largest single topic — and it is the **designated tie-breaker**
for candidates near the minimum passing score. It is also the topic that responds **least** to
cramming, because it is tested through **scenarios** rather than recall: you are not asked to recite
Standard III(B), you are asked whether a described action violates it.

That skill is **pattern recognition built by repeated exposure to many different scenarios over
time**. One hour a day for 150 days beats a 150-hour block, by a wide margin. This is why the plan
runs Ethics in parallel with every other topic from day one.

### What ethics is

| Term | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| **Ethics** | A set of **shared beliefs about what is good, acceptable, or obligatory** behaviour |
| **Values** | Beliefs about what is **important** — honesty, fairness, respect, integrity |
| **Morality / moral principles** | Beliefs about what constitutes **good and bad conduct** |
| **Ethical conduct** | Behaviour that follows moral principles and **balances self-interest against the effect on others** |
| **Code of ethics** | A **written set of moral principles** guiding behaviour and articulating the values a group expects |
| **Standards of conduct** | **Explicit benchmarks** that make a code's principles operational and enforceable |

> **A code of ethics states the principles; standards of conduct make them testable.** "Act with
> integrity" is a principle. "Do not act on material non-public information" is a standard. The Code
> and Standards contain both, and the exam tests the standards.

### Stakeholders and the effects of unethical conduct

Unethical behaviour in investment management harms, in widening circles:

1. **The client** — direct financial loss
2. **The employer** — reputational damage, legal liability, lost business
3. **The profession** — erosion of the trust on which every practitioner depends
4. **The capital markets and society** — higher cost of capital, reduced participation, misallocated
   savings

> **The externality is the key idea.** An individual's misconduct imposes costs far beyond their own
> client relationship. Because capital markets run on **trust**, each breach raises the cost of
> capital for everyone — including the many practitioners who did nothing wrong.

### Professions and trust

**What makes an occupation a profession:**

| Characteristic | Detail |
| --- | --- |
| **Specialised knowledge and skills** | Acquired through extended training |
| **A code of ethics** | Governing members' conduct |
| **Service to society** | An obligation beyond self-interest |
| **Self-regulation** | Standards set and enforced by the profession itself |
| **Barriers to entry** | Qualification requirements |
| **Ongoing competence** | Continuing education requirements |

**How professions establish trust:**

- **Normalise practitioner conduct** — clients know what to expect from any member
- **Provide a service to society** beyond commercial self-interest
- **Are client-focused**, placing the client's interest first
- **Have high entry standards** that signal competence
- **Encourage and enforce ethical behaviour**

> **Why the investment profession needs trust more than most.** Investment management is
> characterised by:
> - **Information asymmetry** — the manager knows far more than the client about what is being done
>   with their money
> - **Delegated authority** — the client hands over control of their savings
> - **Difficulty verifying quality** — outcomes are dominated by market movements, so a client
>   cannot easily distinguish skill from luck, or diligence from negligence
> - **Products that are intangible and complex**
>
> A client buying a physical product can inspect it. A client buying investment management largely
> **cannot verify what they received**. The relationship therefore depends on trust to a degree that
> very few commercial relationships do — and that is precisely why the ethical obligations are so
> demanding.

### Ethics versus law

| | **Legal standards** | **Ethical standards** |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Source | Legislation and regulation | Shared moral principles |
| Scope | **Narrower** — specific prohibited acts | **Broader** — principles covering situations not anticipated by law |
| Nature | Minimum acceptable conduct | **Higher** aspirational conduct |
| Timing | **Reactive** — written after problems emerge | **Proactive** — applies to new situations |
| Enforcement | Courts, regulators | Professional bodies, reputation |

**The four combinations, and the two that matter:**

| | **Legal** | **Illegal** |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Ethical** | The normal case | **Whistleblowing** in a jurisdiction that prohibits it; civil disobedience |
| **Unethical** | **The important case** — legal but wrong | Clearly prohibited |

> **The cell that matters is "legal but unethical".** Much harmful conduct in finance has been
> entirely legal: excessive but disclosed fees, products designed to be confusing, exploiting a
> loophole, trading ahead of a client's order where no rule forbids it. Law is **reactive** — it is
> written after a problem has emerged — so at any moment there are harmful practices that no rule
> yet prohibits.
>
> **The Code and Standards therefore set a higher bar than the law**, and "it was legal" is never a
> sufficient answer under them. This framing recurs throughout the topic.

### Challenges to ethical behaviour

| Challenge | Detail |
| --- | --- |
| **Overestimating one's own ethics** | Almost everyone believes they are more ethical than average. This is the single biggest obstacle, because it prevents people from preparing |
| **Situational influences** | **External pressures that overwhelm principles**: bonus targets, peer behaviour, time pressure, loyalty to colleagues, fear of job loss. These are far more powerful predictors of misconduct than character |
| **Focusing on immediate consequences** | Short-term pressures crowd out long-term ones |
| **Rationalisation** | "Everyone does it"; "it's only this once"; "no one is harmed"; "I earned it" |
| **The slippery slope** | Small compromises escalate. Nobody starts with large fraud |
| **Failure to recognise the ethical dimension** | The most common failure — treating a decision as purely commercial or technical when it has an ethical component |

> **The research finding that matters most: situational influences outweigh individual character.**
> People behave unethically not primarily because they are bad people but because they are placed
> in situations where the pressure is intense, the reward is immediate, the harm is diffuse, and
> everyone around them is doing the same thing.
>
> **The practical implication:** relying on good intentions is insufficient. You need a **decision
> framework** applied **before** the pressure arrives — which is the point of the next section.

### A framework for ethical decision-making

**The four-step framework:**

| Step | What you do |
| --- | --- |
| **1. Identify** | The **facts**; the **stakeholders** and duties owed to them; the **ethical principles** at stake; and the **conflicts of interest** |
| **2. Consider** | Situational influences acting on you; **alternative actions**; and **additional guidance** — from a supervisor, compliance, an outside advisor, or the Code and Standards |
| **3. Decide and act** | |
| **4. Reflect** | Was the outcome as expected? What would you do differently? Feed it back into future decisions |

> **The most valuable step is step 2 — seeking additional guidance.** Almost every ethical failure
> involves someone deciding alone, under pressure, without consulting anyone. The act of having to
> **explain** a course of action to a colleague, a supervisor, or compliance is itself a powerful
> filter: conduct that cannot be comfortably explained usually should not be undertaken.
>
> A practical version of the same test: **would you be comfortable if this appeared on the front
> page of a newspaper**, or if your client could see exactly what you did and why?

**Applying the framework on the exam.** For any Ethics scenario, work through:

1. **Who are the stakeholders and what duties are owed to each?** (Client, employer, market, public)
2. **Which Standard is engaged?** Often more than one
3. **What does the Standard require — disclosure, abstention, prior consent, or something else?**
4. **Is the described conduct consistent with that requirement?**

> **The default hierarchy when duties conflict:** **client > employer > self**. Standard III(A)
> requires that client interests come before the employer's and the member's own. Holding this
> ordering resolves a large share of exam scenarios before any detailed analysis.

## Exam traps

> **Trap 1 — Treating legal compliance as sufficient.** The Code and Standards set a **higher** bar
> than the law. **"It was legal" is never a complete answer** to an Ethics question.

> **Trap 2 — Underestimating situational influences.** Research consistently shows **situation
> outweighs character**. Misconduct arises from pressure, incentives, and peer behaviour far more
> than from bad people.

> **Trap 3 — Confusing a code of ethics with standards of conduct.** The **code states principles**;
> the **standards are the testable benchmarks** that make them enforceable.

> **Trap 4 — Missing the widening circle of harm.** Misconduct harms the client, the employer, the
> **profession**, and the **capital markets**. The externality is the point.

> **Trap 5 — Overlooking step 2 of the framework.** **Seeking additional guidance** is the step that
> most reliably prevents failures, and the one most often skipped under pressure.

> **Trap 6 — Failing to recognise that a decision has an ethical dimension.** This is the most
> common failure mode of all — treating a decision as purely commercial when duties are engaged.

> **Trap 7 — Getting the duty hierarchy wrong.** **Client first, then employer, then self.** This
> ordering resolves a large share of exam scenarios.

## Self-check

*Closed book. Commit to an answer before opening the fold.*

**1.** Why does the investment profession require higher trust than most commercial relationships?

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Because of a combination of features that makes the client structurally unable to protect themselves:

**(1) Information asymmetry.** The manager knows vastly more than the client about what is being done with their money, what it costs, and what the risks are. The client cannot close that gap by effort.

**(2) Delegated authority.** The client hands over **control of their savings** — often their entire financial security — to someone else's discretion.

**(3) Difficulty verifying quality.** Outcomes are dominated by **market movements**, so a good year may reflect luck and a bad year may reflect nothing the manager did wrong. The client cannot distinguish skill from luck, or diligence from negligence, from the result alone — and often not for many years.

**(4) Intangible, complex products.** There is nothing to inspect.

**The contrast that makes the point:** a client buying a physical product can examine it, compare it, and return it if defective. A client buying investment management **largely cannot verify what they received**, even after the fact.

**So the relationship depends on trust to a degree very few commercial relationships do** — which is exactly why the ethical obligations are so demanding, why fiduciary duty applies, and why the profession self-regulates. The trust is not a nicety; it is the only thing standing in for verification.

</details>

**2.** A practice is entirely legal in the relevant jurisdiction but harms clients. What does the Code require?

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**The Code requires more than the law.** Legality is not a defence.

**Why the Code sets a higher bar:**
- **Law is reactive.** Legislation is written **after** a problem has emerged and caused harm. At any moment there are harmful practices no rule yet prohibits.
- **Law is narrow.** It prohibits specific enumerated acts; the Code articulates **principles** that apply to situations no rule anticipated.
- **Law is a minimum.** It defines the floor of acceptable conduct, not the standard a professional should meet.

**The 'legal but unethical' cell is where most real professional judgement lives.** Examples: fees that are disclosed but excessive; products deliberately designed to be difficult to compare; exploiting a loophole in a client agreement; allocating the best executions to the largest accounts where no rule requires otherwise.

**What the member must do:** apply the **Code and Standards**, not merely the local law. Where the Code is **stricter** than the applicable law, **follow the Code** — this is explicitly required by **Standard I(A) Knowledge of the Law**, which the next modules cover in detail.

**The practical test:** if the conduct could not be comfortably explained to the client, or would be damaging if reported publicly, its legality does not resolve the question.

</details>

**3.** Name the four steps of the ethical decision-making framework and explain which is most valuable.

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**(1) Identify** — the relevant **facts**; the **stakeholders** and the duties owed to each; the **ethical principles** at stake; and any **conflicts of interest**.

**(2) Consider** — the **situational influences** acting on you (bonus pressure, deadlines, what colleagues are doing); the **alternative actions** available; and **additional guidance** — from a supervisor, compliance, an outside advisor, or the Code and Standards themselves.

**(3) Decide and act.**

**(4) Reflect** — did the outcome match expectations? What would you do differently? Feed the answer into future decisions.

**The most valuable step is (2), specifically seeking additional guidance.**

**Why:** almost every documented ethical failure involves someone deciding **alone**, **under pressure**, **without consulting anyone**. Isolation is the common factor.

The act of having to **articulate** a course of action to a colleague or compliance officer is itself a powerful filter. Conduct that cannot be comfortably explained usually should not be undertaken — and the requirement to explain it forces the ethical dimension into view, which addresses the most common failure mode of all (not recognising that a decision has one).

**A practical version of the same test:** would you be comfortable if this appeared on the front page of a newspaper, or if the client could see exactly what you did and why?

</details>

**4.** Why are situational influences considered a greater threat to ethical behaviour than individual character?

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Because the **empirical evidence consistently shows** that ordinary people behave unethically when placed in situations of intense pressure, immediate reward, diffuse harm, and peer conformity — regardless of how they would describe their own character beforehand.

**The situational factors that matter most:**
- **Compensation and targets** — a bonus contingent on a number creates pressure to produce that number
- **Peer behaviour** — 'everyone here does it' is among the strongest predictors of individual misconduct
- **Time pressure** — decisions made in haste skip the reflection that would surface the ethical dimension
- **Loyalty to colleagues and the firm** — misplaced loyalty rationalises a great deal
- **Authority** — instructions from a superior
- **Fear** — of job loss, of being the only objector

**Why this matters more than character:** almost everyone **overestimates their own ethics** — the large majority of people rate themselves above average. That belief is itself dangerous, because it means people do **not prepare**. They assume that when the moment comes they will simply do the right thing, and so they never think about it in advance.

**The practical implication, which is the point of the whole module:** good intentions are insufficient. What works is:
- **Recognising in advance** which situations create pressure
- Applying a **decision framework** — deliberately, before the pressure arrives
- **Seeking guidance** rather than deciding alone
- **Firm-level controls**: compliance systems, supervision, and a culture where raising concerns is safe

This is why the Standards impose obligations on **supervisors** (Standard IV(C)) and on **firms**, not only on individuals — the profession's answer to a situational problem is structural, not exhortation.

</details>
