## Core concepts

### The principal–agent problem

A **principal** hires an **agent** to act on their behalf. Conflict arises whenever:

1. The agent's **interests differ** from the principal's, **and**
2. There is **information asymmetry** — the agent knows more than the principal and their actions
   cannot be perfectly observed.

Both conditions are necessary. Perfectly aligned interests make monitoring unnecessary; perfect
observability makes misalignment harmless.

**In a corporation:**

| Principal | Agent | Conflict |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Shareholders** | **Management** | Empire-building, excessive perks, risk aversion (protecting their job over maximising value), earnings management, resisting value-creating takeovers |
| **Shareholders** | **Board** | Board capture — directors too close to management, or insufficiently expert to challenge |
| **Creditors** | **Shareholders/management** | Excessive leverage, large dividends, **asset substitution** (LM2) |
| **Controlling shareholders** | **Minority shareholders** | Related-party transactions, tunnelling value out, dual-class shares entrenching control |

> The last row is easy to overlook. In markets with concentrated ownership — much of Asia,
> continental Europe, and family-controlled businesses everywhere — the dominant conflict is **not**
> shareholders versus managers but **controlling versus minority shareholders**. The controller
> *is* management, so the classic Anglo-American agency problem does not apply; the risk is value
> being extracted from minorities through related-party dealings.

### Mechanisms of corporate governance

**Internal mechanisms:**

| Mechanism | How it works |
| --- | --- |
| **Board of directors** | Oversight of management; hiring and firing the CEO |
| **Board independence** | A majority of **independent** (non-executive, unaffiliated) directors |
| **Separating chair and CEO** | Prevents the CEO from chairing the body that oversees them |
| **Board committees** | **Audit** (financial reporting and external auditor), **remuneration/compensation**, **nomination/governance**, **risk** |
| **Staggered vs. annual elections** | **Annual** election of all directors is more shareholder-friendly; staggered boards entrench |
| **Compensation design** | Linking pay to long-term performance; deferral, clawbacks, share ownership requirements |
| **Internal audit and controls** | Detect and deter misreporting |
| **Codes of ethics and conduct** | Set expectations and provide a reporting channel |

**External mechanisms:**

| Mechanism | How it works |
| --- | --- |
| **Shareholder voting** | Electing directors; say-on-pay; approving major transactions |
| **Shareholder activism** | Public pressure, proxy contests, board seats |
| **The market for corporate control** | The threat of a hostile takeover disciplines underperforming management |
| **Regulation and listing rules** | Mandatory disclosure, independence requirements |
| **Auditors** | External assurance (with the limitations in FSA LM10) |
| **Credit rating agencies and analysts** | External scrutiny of the numbers and the strategy |
| **Debt covenants** | Creditor constraint on behaviour |

### Shareholder rights and anti-takeover provisions

Mechanisms that **strengthen** shareholders: cumulative voting (helps minorities elect a director),
annual election of all directors, majority voting for directors, proxy access, say-on-pay,
one-share-one-vote.

Mechanisms that **entrench management** and weaken shareholders:

- **Dual-class share structures** — founders hold shares with 10 or 20 votes each, retaining control
  with a small economic stake. The most consequential of these in practice.
- **Staggered (classified) boards** — only a third of directors are elected each year, so gaining
  control takes two or three annual meetings.
- **Poison pills** — allow existing shareholders to buy discounted shares if an acquirer crosses a
  threshold, massively diluting them.
- **Golden parachutes** — large payouts to executives on a change of control.
- **Supermajority requirements** for mergers or charter changes.

> These are not automatically bad. A founder-controlled company can take a genuinely longer view,
> free of quarterly pressure. But they remove the **market for corporate control** as a disciplining
> force, so the governance burden falls entirely on the board — and the board is appointed by the
> controller. Assess them as a risk factor, not a verdict.

### Risks of poor governance

| Risk | Manifestation |
| --- | --- |
| **Weak control** | Fraud, misappropriation, unchecked related-party transactions |
| **Accounting and reporting failure** | Earnings management, restatements (FSA LM10) |
| **Poor capital allocation** | Value-destroying acquisitions, empire-building, overinvestment |
| **Excessive risk-taking** | Compensation rewarding short-term results without downside |
| **Legal, regulatory, and reputational** | Fines, litigation, loss of licence, customer flight |
| **Higher cost of capital** | Investors and lenders demand a governance risk premium |
| **Default risk** | Where poor governance permits excessive leverage |

### Benefits of effective governance

- **Operational efficiency** — better capital allocation and decision-making
- **Lower cost of debt and equity** — a narrower risk premium
- **Improved financial performance** and fewer negative surprises
- **Reduced legal and reputational risk**
- **Better reporting quality**, which makes the company analysable
- **Sustainable long-term value creation** for all stakeholders

> The evidence base is mixed on whether good governance *causes* outperformance, but it is
> consistent on the tail: **poor governance predicts blow-ups.** For an analyst, governance is
> primarily a tool for avoiding catastrophic losses, not for identifying winners. That framing is
> the right one to carry into the exam and into practice.

### What to actually look at

A practical governance checklist, drawn from the proxy statement and the annual report:

- Board size, independence percentage, and whether the chair and CEO are separate
- Directors' other commitments (over-boarded directors cannot monitor properly) and tenure
- Audit committee composition — is there genuine financial expertise?
- **Compensation structure**: what metric is management paid on, over what horizon, with what
  clawback? Pay for EPS growth invites buybacks; pay for ROIC invites capital discipline.
- Share class structure and the gap between economic and voting ownership
- **Related-party transactions** — the clearest signal of value leakage
- Auditor tenure, fees, and the ratio of non-audit to audit fees
- History of restatements, auditor changes, and CFO turnover

## Formulas to know cold

No formulas. Two frameworks to memorise:

```
PRINCIPAL-AGENT PROBLEM requires BOTH:
    (1) divergent interests   AND   (2) information asymmetry
    Remove either one and the conflict disappears.

THE FOUR AGENCY CONFLICTS IN A CORPORATION:
    Shareholders ↔ Management        (classic; dispersed ownership)
    Shareholders ↔ Board             (board capture)
    Creditors    ↔ Shareholders      (leverage, dividends, asset substitution)
    Controlling  ↔ Minority          (dominant in concentrated-ownership markets)
       shareholders   shareholders

GOVERNANCE MECHANISMS:
    INTERNAL — board, independence, committees, compensation design, internal audit
    EXTERNAL — voting, activism, market for corporate control, regulation, auditors,
               rating agencies, covenants
```

## Exam traps

> **Trap 1 — Assuming the shareholder–manager conflict is universal.** In concentrated-ownership
> markets the dominant conflict is **controlling versus minority shareholders**, and the remedies
> are entirely different.

> **Trap 2 — Treating anti-takeover provisions as unambiguously bad.** They remove takeover
> discipline, which is a risk — but they can also permit a genuinely long-term orientation. Assess,
> do not assume.

> **Trap 3 — Equating board independence with effectiveness.** An independent board that lacks
> relevant expertise, or whose members sit on eight other boards, cannot monitor effectively.

> **Trap 4 — Ignoring compensation metrics.** *What* management is paid on determines behaviour. Pay
> tied to EPS growth encourages buybacks and acquisitions; pay tied to ROIC encourages capital
> discipline. Read the proxy.

> **Trap 5 — Overlooking related-party transactions.** They are the clearest available signal of
> value leaking to insiders, and they are disclosed.

> **Trap 6 — Believing governance predicts outperformance.** The reliable finding is that poor
> governance predicts **blow-ups**. Governance analysis is downside protection.

## Self-check

*Closed book. Commit to an answer before opening the fold.*

**1.** State the two conditions required for a principal-agent problem, and explain why both are necessary.

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**(1) Divergent interests** between principal and agent, **and (2) information asymmetry** — the agent knows more than the principal, and their actions cannot be perfectly observed.

Both are necessary. If interests were **perfectly aligned**, the agent would do exactly what the principal wants regardless of monitoring — no conflict. If actions were **perfectly observable**, the principal could simply contract on behaviour and enforce it — misalignment would be harmless.

The practical implication: governance mechanisms work by attacking one condition or the other. **Compensation design** attacks divergent interests (aligning the agent's payoff with the principal's). **Disclosure, audit, and board oversight** attack information asymmetry.

</details>

**2.** In a family-controlled company where the founder holds 60% of the votes and serves as CEO, what is the principal governance risk?

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**Not** the classic shareholder–manager conflict. The controller *is* the manager, so their interests as owner and as executive are aligned by construction.

The risk is **controlling shareholder versus minority shareholders** — value being extracted from minorities:
- **Related-party transactions** at non-market terms (selling to or buying from entities the family also owns)
- **Tunnelling** — moving assets or opportunities to entities where the family's stake is larger
- Excessive compensation to family members
- Refusing value-maximising transactions that would dilute control
- Minorities cannot vote the controller out, and the **market for corporate control is dead** — no hostile bid can succeed

**What to examine:** related-party transaction disclosures, the independence of directors (are they genuinely independent of the family?), minority protection provisions in the jurisdiction's company law, and whether the economic stake matches the voting stake.

</details>

**3.** A CEO's bonus is based entirely on one-year EPS growth. What behaviours does this incentivise?

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

- **Share buybacks** — reduce the share count, raise EPS, with no operating improvement whatsoever
- **Debt-funded acquisitions** that are EPS-accretive but value-destroying (any acquisition funded at a cost below the target's earnings yield is accretive, regardless of whether it creates value)
- **Cutting R&D, capex, and marketing** — immediate EPS benefit, long-term damage
- **Earnings management** — the levers in FSA LM10: estimate changes, provision releases, revenue timing
- **Avoiding value-creating investments** with payback beyond one year

**Better design:** multi-year measurement, **ROIC or economic profit** rather than EPS (which cannot be gamed by buybacks or leverage), relative performance against peers, significant deferral, **clawback** provisions, and minimum shareholding requirements so the executive holds equity through the consequences.

</details>

**4.** Name three internal and three external governance mechanisms, and explain what the 'market for corporate control' disciplines.

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**Internal:** an independent board with a separate chair; board committees (audit, remuneration, nomination); compensation design with deferral and clawbacks. Also internal audit and control systems.

**External:** shareholder voting and activism; regulation and listing standards; external auditors and rating agencies. Also debt covenants.

**The market for corporate control** is the threat of a **hostile takeover**. If management runs a company poorly, its share price falls below what the assets are worth under better management — creating an opportunity for an acquirer to buy it, replace management, and capture the difference.

The **threat alone** disciplines behaviour, even if no bid materialises. That is why anti-takeover provisions — dual-class shares, staggered boards, poison pills — are a governance concern: they remove this discipline entirely and place the whole burden on a board the incumbent controls.

</details>

**5.** Why is governance analysis better framed as downside protection than as a source of outperformance?

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

The empirical evidence that good governance **causes** superior returns is mixed and contested — well-governed companies are often already recognised and priced accordingly, so the governance quality is in the price.

What the evidence is consistent about is the **tail**: poor governance is strongly associated with fraud, restatements, value-destroying acquisitions, unchecked risk-taking, and outright failure. These are large, sudden, permanent losses — the kind that no amount of diversification elsewhere recovers.

So the payoff to governance analysis is **asymmetric**. It rarely identifies a winner; it regularly identifies a company you should not own. For an analyst, that is the correct use: a screening and avoidance tool, applied consistently, whose value shows up as losses not taken rather than as gains made.

</details>
