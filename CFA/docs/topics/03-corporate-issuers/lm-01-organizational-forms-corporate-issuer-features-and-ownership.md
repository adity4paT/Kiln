## Core concepts

### Organisational forms

Three dimensions distinguish them: **liability**, **access to capital**, and **taxation**.

| Form | Owners | Liability | Access to capital | Taxation |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Sole proprietorship** | One | **Unlimited personal** | Very limited — the owner's own funds and borrowing | Owner's personal income |
| **General partnership** | Two or more | **Unlimited, and joint and several** | Limited to the partners | Partners' personal income |
| **Limited partnership** | General + limited partners | **GP unlimited; LPs limited** to their investment | Broader — LPs can be passive investors | Partners' personal income |
| **Corporation (limited company)** | Shareholders | **Limited** to the amount invested | **Broadest** — public equity and debt markets | Corporate level; **double taxation** in many jurisdictions |

**The three defining features of a corporation:**

1. **Legal identity separate from its owners.** It can contract, sue, be sued, and own property in
   its own name.
2. **Owner–manager separation.** Shareholders own; a board and management run it. This separation is
   what creates the principal–agent problem (LM3).
3. **Limited liability.** A shareholder can lose their investment and no more.

> **Why limited liability matters economically:** without it, no rational person would buy a small
> stake in a large enterprise they cannot control — the downside would be unbounded. Limited
> liability is what makes the diffuse, liquid ownership of public equity possible at all.

**Double taxation and its remedies.** Corporate profits are taxed at the company level, then
dividends are taxed again in the shareholder's hands. Jurisdictions mitigate this in three ways:

- **Classical system** — full double taxation (the baseline).
- **Imputation system** — the shareholder receives a credit for the corporate tax already paid, so
  the effective total is the shareholder's own rate.
- **Split-rate system** — distributed profits are taxed at a lower corporate rate than retained
  profits.

Some jurisdictions also permit **pass-through** corporate forms (such as the US S-corporation or
various LLC structures) that combine limited liability with partnership-style single taxation.

### Public vs. private corporate issuers

| | **Public** | **Private** |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Shares trade | On an exchange or over the counter | Not publicly traded |
| Ownership | Dispersed, often thousands of holders | Concentrated — founders, families, PE funds |
| **Disclosure** | Extensive and mandatory (LM1 of FSA) | Minimal, often only what lenders require |
| **Access to capital** | Broad and deep | Limited — private placements, bank debt, PE |
| **Liquidity for owners** | High | **Low** — exit requires a sale or an IPO |
| **Share price** | Observable, continuous | Unobservable; valued periodically at best |
| **Regulatory burden** | Heavy | Light |
| **Pressure** | Quarterly earnings, analyst scrutiny, activists | Long-term, but lender covenants bite |

**Routes from private to public:**

- **IPO (initial public offering)** — new shares sold to the public via underwriters. Raises capital
  and creates a market.
- **Direct listing** — existing shares listed without issuing new ones and typically without
  underwriters. No new capital raised; cheaper but no price support.
- **Acquisition** — bought by an already-public company.
- **SPAC (special purpose acquisition company)** — merger with a listed shell that already holds cash.

**And from public to private:** a **leveraged buyout (LBO)** or management buyout, typically
financed largely with debt and sponsored by a private equity firm. The company deregisters, sheds
the disclosure burden, and operates under a highly levered balance sheet.

> **For the analyst:** the choice is not a hierarchy. Public markets give access and liquidity at the
> cost of disclosure and short-term pressure. Private ownership gives operational freedom at the
> cost of illiquidity and a narrower capital base. Which is better depends entirely on the company's
> capital needs and its owners' horizon.

## Formulas to know cold

No formulas. Memorise the comparison tables:

```
ORGANISATIONAL FORMS — three dimensions:  LIABILITY · CAPITAL ACCESS · TAXATION
    Sole proprietorship  → unlimited liability, minimal capital, personal tax
    General partnership  → unlimited, joint and several
    Limited partnership  → GP unlimited, LPs limited
    Corporation          → LIMITED liability, broadest capital, double taxation

CORPORATION — three defining features:
    1. Separate legal identity
    2. Owner–manager separation  → creates the principal-agent problem
    3. Limited liability         → makes diffuse public ownership possible

DOUBLE TAXATION REMEDIES:  classical (none) · imputation (credit) · split-rate (lower on distributions)
```

## Exam traps

> **Trap 1 — Limited partnership liability.** The **general partner has unlimited** liability;
> **limited partners** are liable only up to their investment — and lose limited status if they
> participate in management. This structure is the basis of every private equity and hedge fund
> vehicle (Alternative Investments LM1).

> **Trap 2 — Assuming public is always better.** Public listing brings disclosure cost, regulatory
> burden, and short-term earnings pressure. Many profitable companies stay private deliberately.

> **Trap 3 — Double taxation is universal.** It is not. Imputation and split-rate systems mitigate
> it, and some corporate forms pass income through entirely.

> **Trap 4 — Direct listing raises capital.** It does **not**. Existing shares are listed; no new
> shares are issued and no new money comes in.

> **Trap 5 — Partnership taxation.** Partnerships are **not** taxed as entities; income passes
> through to the partners' personal returns.

## Self-check

*Closed book. Commit to an answer before opening the fold.*

**1.** Compare the liability of a general partner and a limited partner, and explain why the distinction matters for fund structures.

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

A **general partner has unlimited liability** for the partnership's obligations — personal assets are exposed, and liability is **joint and several** (any one partner can be pursued for the whole amount).

A **limited partner's liability is capped at their investment**, but only while they remain passive. Participating in management can cost them that protection.

Why it matters: this is the structure of essentially every private equity, venture capital, and hedge fund. The **fund manager is the GP** (unlimited liability, but typically a thin shell entity), and the **investors are LPs** (capped liability, no management role). It lets passive capital take equity risk without unbounded exposure, which is what makes the asset class possible.

</details>

**2.** Why does limited liability make dispersed public ownership possible?

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Without limited liability, a shareholder's downside would be **unbounded** — they could lose far more than they invested if the company incurred large obligations.

No rational investor would take an unbounded, unlimited downside on a small stake in an enterprise they cannot observe closely or control. The only people who would hold equity would be those with enough ownership to monitor management directly.

**Limited liability caps the downside at the amount invested**, which makes a small passive stake a sensible proposition. That is what permits ownership to be split among thousands of holders, traded freely, and priced continuously — i.e. what makes public equity markets exist.

</details>

**3.** A founder is deciding between staying private and pursuing an IPO. Lay out the trade-off.

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**In favour of going public:**
- Access to **broad, deep capital** for growth, at lower cost than private sources
- **Liquidity** for the founder and early investors — an exit route that does not require selling the whole company
- An **observable share price**, usable as acquisition currency and for employee equity compensation
- Profile and credibility with customers and counterparties

**In favour of staying private:**
- No mandatory **disclosure** — competitors do not see your margins, customers, or strategy
- No **quarterly earnings pressure**, activist investors, or analyst management
- Far lower **regulatory and compliance cost**
- The founder keeps **control**

The deciding factor is usually capital need. A company that can fund its growth from cash flow and bank debt gains little from listing; one that needs large equity infusions, or whose owners need liquidity, gains a great deal.

</details>

**4.** What is the key difference between an IPO and a direct listing?

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

An **IPO issues new shares**, sold to investors through underwriters. It **raises capital** for the company, the underwriters provide price discovery and often price support, and existing shareholders are typically subject to a lock-up period.

A **direct listing** simply lists **existing** shares on an exchange. **No new shares are issued and no capital is raised.** There are typically no underwriters, so the process is cheaper and the opening price is set purely by supply and demand — with no stabilisation.

Direct listings suit companies that are already well capitalised but whose existing shareholders want liquidity. They cannot serve a company that needs money.

</details>
