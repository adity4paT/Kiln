## Core concepts

### What a business model is

A business model describes **how a company creates, delivers, and captures value**. It is the
mechanism connecting a customer need to a profit.

**The key features — the questions a business model must answer:**

| Feature | The question |
| --- | --- |
| **Who** | Who is the customer? What segment, and what do they need? |
| **What** | What is the offering, and what is the **value proposition**? Why this rather than the alternative? |
| **Where** | Through what **channel** does it reach the customer — direct, retail, wholesale, digital, platform? |
| **How much** | What is the **pricing** model? |
| **How** | What is the **value chain** — which activities are performed in-house, which outsourced? |
| **Why profitable** | Where does the margin come from, and what protects it? |

**Pricing approaches** worth recognising:

| Approach | Basis |
| --- | --- |
| **Value-based** | What the customer is willing to pay, given the benefit received |
| **Cost-based** | Cost plus a markup |
| **Price discrimination** | Different prices to different segments (tiered, dynamic, auction, bundling) |
| **Penetration** | Price low to gain share, monetise later — common where network effects exist |
| **Premium / skimming** | Price high initially, targeting less price-sensitive customers first |
| **Freemium** | Free base tier, paid upgrades |
| **Razor-and-blades** | Cheap durable, profitable consumables |
| **Subscription** | Recurring fee for ongoing access |

### Types of business model

| Model | How it works | Examples |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Traditional retail / wholesale** | Buy, mark up, sell | Department stores, distributors |
| **Manufacturer** | Produce and sell goods | Industrial and consumer goods |
| **Service** | Sell labour and expertise | Consulting, legal, healthcare |
| **Subscription** | Recurring revenue for ongoing access | Software-as-a-service, media, gyms |
| **Franchise** | Licence the brand and operating system to operators | Fast food, hotels |
| **Licensing** | Grant rights to intellectual property for a royalty | Pharmaceuticals, brands, technology |
| **Freemium** | Free base, paid premium tier | Consumer apps, developer tools |
| **Razor-and-blades** | Sell the platform cheaply, profit on the consumables | Printers and ink, consoles and games |
| **Platform / marketplace** | Connect two or more user groups and take a fee | Marketplaces, payment networks, app stores |
| **Aggregator** | Bundle many suppliers under one brand and control the customer relationship | Travel booking, streaming |
| **Crowdsourcing** | Users generate the value | User-review and user-content businesses |

### Network effects and platforms

A **network effect** exists when the value of a product **increases with the number of users**.

- **Direct (same-side)** — more users make it better for other users of the same type (a messaging
  service).
- **Indirect (cross-side)** — more users on one side make it more valuable to the other side (more
  buyers attract more sellers, which attracts more buyers).

> **Why this matters for valuation:** network effects produce **increasing returns to scale** and
> can lead to winner-take-most outcomes and very durable competitive positions. They justify
> penetration pricing and long periods of deliberate loss-making to acquire users — which in turn
> makes conventional near-term earnings multiples useless for such companies.
>
> The analytical burden is to distinguish a **genuine** network effect (value truly rises with
> users, and switching is costly) from a business that is simply growing and losing money. The test:
> does the *n*+1th user make the product better for the existing *n*, or merely add revenue?

### Business model risk and the analyst's use of it

The business model determines **which risks matter and which metrics to track**:

| Model | Key metrics | Key risks |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Subscription** | Churn rate, ARPU, customer acquisition cost, net revenue retention, lifetime value | Churn accelerating; CAC rising above LTV |
| **Platform** | Take rate, gross merchandise value, active users on each side | Disintermediation; regulatory intervention; one side defecting |
| **Retail** | Same-store sales, sales per square metre, inventory turns | Footfall decline; channel shift; inventory obsolescence |
| **Manufacturer** | Capacity utilisation, gross margin, order backlog | Operating leverage in a downturn; input cost pass-through |
| **Franchise** | Unit growth, franchisee profitability, royalty rate | Franchisee distress; brand damage from one operator |
| **Licensing** | Royalty rate, patent life, renewal rates | Expiry; litigation; obsolescence |

> The connection back to FSA LM12: the business model tells you **what to forecast**. You do not
> forecast a subscription company's revenue with a growth rate — you forecast subscribers, churn,
> and ARPU, and multiply. Getting the model right is what makes the forecast structurally sound
> rather than a trend extrapolation.

## Formulas to know cold

No formulas, but know the metrics that go with each model:

```
SUBSCRIPTION
  Churn rate = customers lost / customers at start of period
  ARPU = revenue / average users
  Lifetime value (LTV) ≈ ARPU × gross margin % / churn rate
  Rule of thumb: LTV / CAC > 3 is generally considered healthy
  Net revenue retention = revenue from existing cohort this year / last year
                          (> 100% means expansion outweighs churn)

PLATFORM
  Take rate = platform revenue / gross merchandise value

RETAIL
  Same-store (like-for-like) sales growth — strips out new openings
  Sales per square metre
```

## Exam traps

> **Trap 1 — Confusing the business model with the strategy.** The **model** is *how value is
> created, delivered, and captured*. The **strategy** is *how the company competes* within that
> model. A question describing a pricing mechanism is asking about the model.

> **Trap 2 — Assuming network effects exist wherever there are many users.** A genuine network effect
> requires that the *n*+1th user makes the product **better for the existing n**. Scale alone is not
> a network effect.

> **Trap 3 — Applying the same metrics across models.** Same-store sales is meaningless for a SaaS
> company; churn is meaningless for a manufacturer. The model dictates the metrics.

> **Trap 4 — Reading penetration pricing as failure.** Deliberate loss-making to build a network can
> be rational — *if* the network effect is real and the eventual monetisation is credible. Both
> conditions must be established, not assumed.

> **Trap 5 — Forecasting a subscription business with a revenue growth rate.** Forecast **subscribers
> × ARPU**, with churn modelled explicitly. A single growth rate hides everything that matters.

## Self-check

*Closed book. Commit to an answer before opening the fold.*

**1.** What six questions must a business model answer?

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**Who** — which customer segment, and what need?
**What** — the offering and the value proposition; why this rather than the alternative?
**Where** — the channel through which it reaches the customer.
**How much** — the pricing model.
**How** — the value chain: which activities are in-house and which are outsourced.
**Why profitable** — where the margin comes from and what protects it.

The last one is the one candidates skip and the one that matters most analytically. A model that answers the first five but not the sixth describes an activity, not a business.

</details>

**2.** Distinguish direct from indirect network effects with an example of each.

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**Direct (same-side) network effects:** more users of the *same type* make the product more valuable to each other. A messaging service is the canonical case — it is worthless with one user and valuable when everyone you know is on it.

**Indirect (cross-side) network effects:** more users on **one side** make the platform more valuable to users on the **other side**. A marketplace: more buyers attract more sellers, which attracts more buyers. A payment network: more merchants accepting a card make it more valuable to cardholders, and vice versa.

**Why the distinction matters:** indirect effects create a **chicken-and-egg launch problem** — neither side wants to join before the other. Platforms solve it by subsidising one side (often heavily) to attract the other, which is why platform businesses frequently run large deliberate losses early. Understanding which side is subsidised, and whether it can ever be monetised, is central to valuing them.

</details>

**3.** Why might a company with a genuine network effect rationally lose money for years?

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Because network effects produce **increasing returns to scale** and often **winner-take-most** outcomes. If value rises with user count and switching costs accumulate, then market share captured early is durable, and the eventual position is far more profitable than an early, smaller, profitable one.

So spending to acquire users — penetration pricing, subsidising one side of a platform, free tiers — is **investment**, not operating loss, and conventional near-term earnings multiples misprice it.

**The two conditions that must both hold**, and which the analyst must verify rather than assume:
1. The network effect is **real** — the *n*+1th user genuinely improves the product for the existing *n*, and switching away is costly.
2. The eventual **monetisation is credible** — there is a plausible path to charging, at a rate the users will bear, once the position is established.

Many loss-making growth companies satisfy neither and are simply buying revenue that disappears when the subsidy stops. The distinction between the two cases is the whole analysis.

</details>

**4.** A SaaS company reports 40% revenue growth. What do you need to know before assessing it?

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Decompose the 40% into its drivers:

- **New customers vs. expansion within existing customers.** Growth from existing customers (net revenue retention above 100%) is far higher quality — it is cheap and it indicates the product is working.
- **Churn rate**, and whether it is rising. Gross growth of 40% with 25% churn is a very different business from 40% with 5% churn.
- **ARPU trend** — is growth coming from more customers, or from raising prices on the same ones?
- **Customer acquisition cost and the LTV/CAC ratio.** If CAC is rising faster than LTV, growth is being bought at a loss and does not scale.
- **Contract length and billing terms** — is revenue recognised over time while cash is collected upfront (a favourable working capital position), and how much is in deferred revenue?

**The headline growth rate alone is uninterpretable.** A subscription business is a cohort machine: forecast subscribers and ARPU with churn modelled explicitly, never a single revenue growth rate.

</details>
