## Core concepts

### Debt and equity — the asymmetry that explains everything

| | **Debtholders (lenders)** | **Shareholders** |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Claim** | **Fixed** — principal and contractual interest | **Residual** — whatever is left |
| **Priority in liquidation** | **Senior** | **Last** |
| **Upside** | **Capped** at the promised payments | **Unlimited** |
| **Downside** | Loss of principal (but they get paid first) | Total loss of investment |
| **Control rights** | None normally; **covenants** restrict behaviour; control on default | **Vote** on directors and major matters |
| **Preference on risk** | **Low risk** — upside is capped, so extra risk is all downside | **Higher risk can be good** — it raises the value of the residual option |
| **Horizon** | Until maturity | Indefinite |

> **The central insight:** debt and equity want **different amounts of risk**, and the reason is
> structural, not attitudinal. A lender's payoff is capped, so additional business risk offers them
> no upside and all downside. A shareholder holds what is effectively a **call option on the firm's
> assets** with a strike at the face value of debt — and option value **rises with volatility**.
>
> This is why an equity-aligned management may take projects a lender would refuse, and why
> **covenants exist**: they are the lender's substitute for control rights.

**The conflict sharpens as leverage rises.** In a company close to distress, shareholders have
little left to lose and can rationally prefer a high-risk gamble — if it works they recover
everything, if it fails the lenders bear the loss. This is **asset substitution** (or risk shifting),
and it is the reason distressed-company covenants are so restrictive.

### Stakeholder groups and their interests

| Stakeholder | Primary interest | Where it conflicts |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Shareholders** | Share price appreciation, dividends | With lenders over risk; with employees over cost |
| **Creditors / lenders** | Being repaid; preservation of collateral | With shareholders over leverage, dividends, and risk |
| **Board of directors** | Oversight; representing shareholders | May be captured by management |
| **Management** | Compensation, job security, prestige, empire-building | With shareholders — the core agency problem |
| **Employees** | Pay, job security, working conditions, development | With shareholders over cost reduction |
| **Customers** | Product quality, safety, price, service, continuity | With shareholders over pricing and cost-cutting |
| **Suppliers** | Being paid on time; a continuing relationship | With the company over payment terms (working capital, LM4) |
| **Governments / regulators** | Tax receipts, compliance, employment, stability | With the company over tax planning and regulatory cost |
| **Communities** | Employment, environmental impact, local investment | With shareholders over externalities |

The important intellectual point: **stakeholder interests are not automatically aligned**, and
corporate governance (LM3) is the set of mechanisms for managing the conflicts rather than
pretending they do not exist.

### ESG considerations

ESG factors are increasingly treated as **financially material** rather than as an ethical overlay —
that framing matters for the exam.

| | Examples |
| --- | --- |
| **Environmental** | Carbon emissions and transition risk, energy and water use, waste and pollution, biodiversity, physical climate risk to assets |
| **Social** | Labour standards and safety, diversity, supply chain conditions, product safety, data privacy, community relations |
| **Governance** | Board independence and composition, executive compensation alignment, shareholder rights, audit quality, related-party transactions, ownership concentration |

**Why an analyst cares, financially:**

- **Regulatory risk** — carbon pricing, emissions standards, and disclosure requirements change cost
  structures and can strand assets.
- **Litigation and liability** — environmental damage and product safety failures produce large,
  lumpy, unbudgeted cash outflows.
- **Reputational risk** — supply chain scandals hit revenue and hiring.
- **Cost of capital** — issuers with weak ESG profiles can face a narrower investor base and higher
  funding costs.
- **Operational quality** — strong governance is empirically associated with better reporting
  quality (FSA LM10) and fewer negative surprises.

**Approaches to ESG investing** worth recognising:

| Approach | Method |
| --- | --- |
| **Negative screening** | Exclude sectors or issuers (tobacco, weapons, thermal coal) |
| **Positive screening / best-in-class** | Select the leaders within each sector |
| **ESG integration** | Incorporate ESG factors directly into financial analysis and valuation |
| **Thematic investing** | Target a theme (clean energy, water) |
| **Engagement / active ownership** | Use voting and dialogue to change corporate behaviour |
| **Impact investing** | Seek measurable social/environmental outcomes alongside return |

> **The practical difficulty the exam acknowledges:** ESG data is **unstandardised, largely
> unaudited, self-reported, and inconsistent between rating providers.** Two reputable agencies
> routinely assign opposite ESG ratings to the same company. Treat ESG scores as an input requiring
> scrutiny, not as a measurement.

## Formulas to know cold

No formulas. The structural point to internalise:

```
DEBTHOLDER payoff:   capped at promised payments  → PREFERS LOW RISK
SHAREHOLDER payoff:  residual, unlimited upside   → a CALL OPTION on firm assets
                                                    struck at the face value of debt
                                                  → option value RISES with volatility
                                                  → can prefer MORE risk

⇒ The debt–equity conflict is STRUCTURAL, not a difference in temperament.
⇒ COVENANTS are the lender's substitute for control rights.
⇒ The conflict intensifies as leverage rises (asset substitution / risk shifting).
```

---

## Exam traps

> **Trap 1 — Assuming all investors want less risk.** Shareholders hold a **residual claim with
> unlimited upside**, which behaves like a call option. More volatility can make them **better off**.
> Debtholders, with capped upside, never benefit from more risk.

> **Trap 2 — Thinking debtholders have no influence.** They have **covenants**, and on breach they
> can accelerate the debt or seize control. That is real power, exercised at exactly the moment it
> matters.

> **Trap 3 — Treating ESG as purely ethical.** The curriculum frames ESG factors as **financially
> material risk factors**. Regulatory, litigation, and reputational exposures are cash flow items.

> **Trap 4 — Trusting ESG ratings as measurements.** They are unstandardised, self-reported, and
> inconsistent across providers — providers frequently disagree on the same company.

> **Trap 5 — Assuming stakeholder interests align.** They routinely conflict. Governance exists to
> manage those conflicts, not to assert they are absent.

## Self-check

*Closed book. Commit to an answer before opening the fold.*

**1.** Why might shareholders prefer a riskier project than lenders would, even when both hold claims on the same company?

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Because their **payoff structures differ fundamentally**.

A **lender's** return is **capped** at the promised interest and principal. More business risk offers them no additional upside but raises the probability of default — pure downside.

**Shareholders** hold the **residual** claim, which behaves like a **call option on the firm's assets** with a strike price equal to the face value of debt. Option value **increases with volatility**: if the risky project succeeds, shareholders capture all the upside; if it fails, their loss is limited to their (already sunk) investment and lenders absorb the rest.

The asymmetry sharpens as leverage rises. Near distress, shareholders have little left to lose and can rationally prefer a long-shot gamble — **asset substitution**. Covenants exist precisely to constrain this.

</details>

**2.** List four stakeholder groups and give one way each conflicts with shareholders.

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**Management** — prefers job security, larger empires, and higher compensation; may avoid value-creating but personally risky decisions, or pursue value-destroying acquisitions for scale. The core agency conflict.

**Employees** — want higher pay, job security, and better conditions, all of which reduce margins in the short run.

**Creditors** — want lower leverage, restricted dividends, and less risk-taking; shareholders want the opposite when leverage is high.

**Customers** — want lower prices and higher quality; shareholder value pressure pushes toward higher prices and cost reduction.

Also: **suppliers** (want prompt payment, while working capital management pushes for stretched terms), and **communities/regulators** (want externalities internalised, which is a cost).

</details>

**3.** Why are ESG factors considered financially material rather than merely ethical?

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Because each maps to a cash flow or a discount rate:

- **Regulatory risk** — carbon pricing and emissions standards change the cost base and can strand assets outright.
- **Litigation and liability** — environmental damage and product safety failures produce large, unbudgeted, lumpy cash outflows.
- **Reputational risk** — a supply chain scandal reduces revenue and raises hiring costs.
- **Cost of capital** — issuers with poor ESG profiles face a narrower investor base and, in many markets, wider spreads.
- **Governance quality** — empirically associated with better reporting quality and fewer negative surprises, which is directly a risk-premium question.

An analyst who ignores these is not being neutral about ethics; they are omitting identifiable risk factors from the forecast.

</details>

**4.** Two reputable ESG rating agencies assign opposite ratings to the same company. What does this tell you?

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

That ESG ratings are **not measurements** in the way a debt/EBITDA ratio is.

The divergence arises from: different **materiality frameworks** (which factors matter for this industry), different **weightings** of E, S and G, different **data sources** (self-reported disclosure versus controversies versus estimates), different treatment of **missing data** (some impute, some penalise), and different **normalisation** (absolute versus sector-relative).

Practical response: do not use a composite score as an input. **Decompose it** — identify the two or three factors that are financially material for *this* industry, assess those directly from primary disclosure, and treat the agency ratings as a screening tool and a prompt for investigation rather than as an answer.

</details>
