## Core concepts

### Why industry analysis comes before company analysis

A company's profitability is bounded by the economics of the industry it operates in. An excellent
operator in a structurally terrible industry — airlines for most of their history — earns less than
a mediocre one in a good industry. Industry analysis tells you **the range of outcomes available**;
company analysis tells you where in that range this company sits.

### The process

| Step | What you do |
| --- | --- |
| 1 | **Define the industry** — which companies actually compete with each other |
| 2 | **Size it** — total revenue, units, and addressable market |
| 3 | **Assess growth** — historical rates, drivers, and the lifecycle stage |
| 4 | **Assess profitability** — margins and returns on capital across the industry and over time |
| 5 | **Analyse structure** — **Porter's Five Forces** |
| 6 | **Assess external influences** — **PESTLE** |
| 7 | **Map market shares** — concentration, and whether shares are stable or shifting |
| 8 | **Identify the key success factors** — what actually determines who wins |

### Industry classification methods

| Method | Basis | Examples |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Products and services** | What the company sells | GICS, ICB, SIC — the standard commercial schemes |
| **Business cycle sensitivity** | **Cyclical** vs. **defensive (non-cyclical)** | Autos vs. utilities |
| **Statistical similarity** | Clustering on historical return correlation | Quantitative groupings |

**Commercial classification schemes:** **GICS** (Global Industry Classification Standard, by S&P and
MSCI) and **ICB** (Industry Classification Benchmark) are the two dominant systems. Both are
hierarchical — sector → industry group → industry → sub-industry — and assign each company to a
**single** category based on its principal revenue source.

**Cyclical vs. defensive:**

| | **Cyclical** | **Defensive (non-cyclical)** |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Demand | Varies strongly with the economy | Relatively stable |
| Examples | Autos, construction, luxury goods, semiconductors, capital goods | Utilities, food and staples, healthcare, tobacco |
| Typical cost structure | High **operating leverage** | Lower operating leverage |
| Earnings volatility | High | Low |

**Limitations of any classification:**

- **Conglomerates** fit nowhere and are assigned to whichever segment is largest
- A company can be assigned to one category while **competing** with companies in another
- **Convergence** — a technology company selling financial services, a retailer running a cloud
  business; classification lags
- Geographic and regulatory differences within a single "global" industry

> **The practical point:** standard classifications are the **starting point** for a peer group
> (LM7), never the finished product. Refine on the actual drivers of return.

### Sizing, growth, profitability, and share

**Size:** total industry revenue, unit volumes, and the total addressable market. Build it from
industry association data, government statistics, aggregated company reports, and trade sources —
and cross-check bottom-up against top-down.

**Growth:** historical CAGR, the underlying drivers (population, income, penetration, replacement
cycle, regulation), and the **lifecycle stage**:

| Stage | Growth | Profitability | Competition |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Embryonic** | Slow, from a small base | **Negative** — high investment, low volume | Few competitors |
| **Growth** | **Rapid** | Improving as scale arrives | Few direct competitors; new entrants arriving |
| **Shakeout** | Slowing | **Falling** — overcapacity meets slowing demand | Intense; consolidation and exits |
| **Mature** | At or near GDP | Stable, and often the highest | Stable; entry deterred |
| **Decline** | **Negative** | Falling | Consolidation, exit, or price war |

**Profitability:** industry-wide margins and **ROIC relative to WACC** over time. An industry where
ROIC has persistently exceeded WACC has structural protection; one where it has not is competing its
returns away.

**Market share:** concentration measures — the share of the top 4 or 8 companies (**concentration
ratio**), and the **Herfindahl–Hirschman Index** (the sum of squared market shares). And, more
informatively than the level, **whether shares are stable or shifting**: stable shares indicate a
settled competitive equilibrium; shifting shares indicate a live battle and usually margin pressure.

### Porter's Five Forces

The structural analysis. Each force constrains profitability.

| Force | High when | Effect |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Threat of new entrants** | Low barriers: little capital needed, no scale economies, easy distribution, no regulation or patents | Caps long-run prices and margins |
| **Threat of substitutes** | Close alternatives exist, low switching costs | Caps pricing power |
| **Bargaining power of buyers** | Few, large, price-sensitive buyers; low switching costs; undifferentiated product | Pressures **prices** |
| **Bargaining power of suppliers** | Few suppliers; differentiated or essential inputs; high switching costs | Raises **input costs** |
| **Rivalry among existing competitors** | Many similar-sized competitors; slow growth; high fixed costs; high exit barriers; undifferentiated product | Price competition |

> **High exit barriers are underrated.** An industry where capacity cannot leave — because assets
> are specialised, closure costs are high, or governments resist job losses — suffers chronic
> overcapacity and persistent price competition. Steel and airlines are the textbook cases.

**The output of a five forces analysis** should be a statement about **sustainable margin**, not a
list of five paragraphs. If four forces are strong, assume margin reversion to the industry mean; if
the company sits behind a genuine barrier, sustained abnormal returns are defensible.

### PESTLE

The external environment, beyond industry structure.

| | What it covers |
| --- | --- |
| **P**olitical | Government stability, policy direction, trade relations, state ownership |
| **E**conomic | Growth, inflation, interest rates, exchange rates, employment |
| **S**ocial | Demographics, lifestyle, attitudes, education, consumer preferences |
| **T**echnological | Innovation, automation, R&D intensity, disruption risk |
| **L**egal | Regulation, competition law, employment law, IP protection, litigation |
| **E**nvironmental | Climate risk, emissions regulation, resource scarcity, physical asset exposure |

Variants exist (PEST, STEEP, PESTLEG). The framework's value is as a **checklist against
blind spots** — it ensures you do not model a tobacco company without considering litigation and
regulation, or a shipping company without considering emissions rules.

### Key success factors

The final output: **what actually determines who wins in this industry?** Scale? Brand? Distribution?
Cost position? Technology? Regulatory relationships? Network effects?

Identifying these is what makes company analysis (LM10) tractable — you assess the company against
the two or three factors that decide outcomes, rather than against a generic checklist.

## Formulas to know cold

```
CONCENTRATION MEASURES
  Concentration ratio CRn = Σ market shares of the top n firms
    (e.g. CR4 = combined share of the four largest)

  Herfindahl-Hirschman Index  HHI = Σ (market share as a %)²   over ALL firms
    Ranges from ~0 (perfectly competitive) to 10,000 (monopoly)
    Below 1,500  = unconcentrated
    1,500–2,500  = moderately concentrated
    Above 2,500  = highly concentrated
    HHI is superior to CRn because it reflects the SIZE DISTRIBUTION, not just the top n

INDUSTRY LIFECYCLE
  Embryonic → Growth → Shakeout → Mature → Decline

PORTER'S FIVE FORCES
  New entrants · Substitutes · Buyer power · Supplier power · Rivalry

PESTLE
  Political · Economic · Social · Technological · Legal · Environmental
```

## Exam traps

> **Trap 1 — Treating industry classification as a finished peer group.** It is the starting point.
> Refine on growth, profitability, risk, and business model (LM7).

> **Trap 2 — Confusing cyclical with low-quality.** Cyclicality is about **demand sensitivity to the
> economy**, not about business quality. A high-quality cyclical exists.

> **Trap 3 — Reading concentration ratios without the size distribution.** CR4 treats four equal
> firms the same as one dominant firm plus three small ones. **HHI captures the difference** and is
> the better measure.

> **Trap 4 — Producing five paragraphs instead of a conclusion.** A five forces analysis must end in
> a statement about **sustainable margin**, not a description.

> **Trap 5 — Ignoring exit barriers.** High exit barriers cause chronic overcapacity and persistent
> price competition even when demand recovers.

> **Trap 6 — Assuming a mature industry is unattractive.** Mature industries often have the
> **highest and most stable** profitability, because entry is deterred and competitive positions are
> settled.

> **Trap 7 — Applying one industry's lifecycle stage globally.** An industry can be mature in
> developed markets and in the growth phase in emerging ones.

## Self-check

*Closed book. Commit to an answer before opening the fold.*

**1.** An industry has four firms with market shares of 40%, 30%, 20%, and 10%. Compute CR4 and HHI. Then compute both for an industry with ten firms each holding 10%.

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**Industry A:**
CR4 = 40 + 30 + 20 + 10 = **100%**
HHI = 40² + 30² + 20² + 10² = 1,600 + 900 + 400 + 100 = **3,000** → highly concentrated

**Industry B (ten firms at 10% each):**
CR4 = 10 × 4 = **40%**
HHI = 10 × (10²) = 10 × 100 = **1,000** → unconcentrated

The HHI captures what the concentration ratio cannot: industry A is dominated by one large firm with a 40% share and has real pricing power; industry B is a fragmented scramble. Note that HHI **squares** the shares, so it weights large firms disproportionately — which is exactly the intent.

</details>

**2.** Apply Porter's Five Forces to the airline industry and state the conclusion about sustainable margin.

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**Threat of new entrants: moderate-to-high.** Aircraft can be leased rather than bought, so capital intensity is lower than it appears. Slots and gates at major airports are the real barrier, and they are limited.

**Substitutes: moderate.** Rail on short routes; video conferencing for business travel.

**Buyer power: high.** Price-comparison sites make fares perfectly transparent and the product largely undifferentiated for leisure travellers. Switching cost is essentially zero.

**Supplier power: high.** Two aircraft manufacturers, a small number of engine makers, unionised labour, monopoly airports, and fuel priced on a global market the airline cannot influence.

**Rivalry: very high.** High fixed costs create enormous pressure to fill seats at any price above marginal cost, a perishable product (an empty seat is worthless once the door closes), and **high exit barriers** — specialised assets, political resistance to job losses, and bankruptcy protection that keeps capacity in the market.

**Conclusion:** four of five forces are strongly negative. Assume **margins revert to a low industry mean** and that abnormal returns are not sustainable. Any forecast of sustained margin expansion needs a specific structural reason — a genuine slot monopoly, a protected route network, or a cost position no competitor can match.

</details>

**3.** Why might an industry in the 'shakeout' stage be the most dangerous for investors?

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Because **capacity was built for growth that has stopped arriving**.

During the growth phase, high returns attract entrants and incumbents invest heavily in capacity. When demand growth slows, that capacity does not disappear — it competes. Overcapacity meets decelerating demand, and the result is **price competition, collapsing margins, and consolidation**.

The danger for investors is specifically about **timing and narrative**: the industry still carries a growth reputation and often still trades on growth multiples, while its economics have already turned. Consensus forecasts extrapolate the growth phase into a period that will not deliver it.

**What to watch for:** capacity additions outpacing demand growth, pricing starting to weaken, inventory building (FSA LM6), and the first competitor exits or distress. The shakeout ends when enough capacity has left — but high exit barriers can extend that for years.

</details>

**4.** What is the limitation of GICS-style classification for a company like a large technology conglomerate?

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

GICS assigns each company to a **single** category based on its **principal revenue source**, which breaks down for genuinely diversified businesses.

A large technology conglomerate might run a cloud infrastructure business (competing with data-centre operators), a consumer devices business (competing with hardware manufacturers), an advertising business (competing with media companies), and a payments business (competing with financial services firms). These have **completely different** economics, growth rates, margins, capital intensity, and competitive dynamics.

Assigning the whole company to 'Information Technology' produces a peer group that is wrong for every one of its segments.

**What to do instead:** build a **sum-of-the-parts** model (LM8) — value each segment against its own relevant peer group and its own economics, then aggregate and apply any conglomerate discount. The single classification is a screening label, not an analytical framework.

</details>
