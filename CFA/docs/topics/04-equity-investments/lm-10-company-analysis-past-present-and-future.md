## Core concepts

### Competitive strategy

Porter's **generic strategies** — a company must choose, and the danger is choosing neither.

| Strategy | Source of advantage | Requires |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Cost leadership** | Being the **lowest-cost producer** in the industry | Scale, process efficiency, favourable input access, tight cost control |
| **Differentiation** | An offering customers value enough to **pay a premium** for | Brand, quality, service, innovation, design |
| **Focus** | Serving a **narrow segment** exceptionally well, via either cost or differentiation | Deep segment knowledge; resisting the pull to broaden |

> **"Stuck in the middle"** is Porter's term for a company pursuing neither convincingly: it has
> neither the lowest cost nor a premium position, so it competes on price without the cost base to
> survive doing so. It is a common and usually terminal position.

**Competitive advantage and its durability.** The question that matters for valuation is not whether
a company earns high returns today, but **whether it can keep earning them**. Sources of durable
advantage — "moats":

| Source | Why it lasts |
| --- | --- |
| **Cost advantage** | Scale economies, proprietary process, location, or input access competitors cannot replicate |
| **Intangible assets** | Brands, patents, regulatory licences |
| **Switching costs** | Retraining, data migration, integration into a customer's workflow |
| **Network effects** | Value rises with users (Corporate Issuers LM7) |
| **Efficient scale** | A market large enough for only one or two players to be profitable |

**The test:** if ROIC has persistently exceeded WACC for many years and competitors have not
competed it away, something is protecting it. **Name the mechanism.** If you cannot, assume
reversion — because in a competitive market, abnormal returns attract entry and decay.

### Revenue drivers

Decompose revenue rather than forecasting a growth rate:

```
Revenue = Volume × Price
```

and then, depending on the business model (Corporate Issuers LM7):

- **Units × average selling price** — manufacturers, retailers
- **Subscribers × ARPU**, with churn — subscription businesses
- **Transactions × take rate** — platforms and marketplaces
- **Market size × market share** — top-down approach
- **Stores × sales per store**, split into like-for-like and new openings — retail

**Questions to answer about revenue:**

- How much of growth is **volume** and how much is **price**? In an inflationary period the
  distinction is decisive — only volume growth is real.
- Is growth **organic or acquired**? Acquired growth requires capital and carries integration risk.
- **Customer concentration** — what share of revenue comes from the largest customers, and what
  happens if one leaves?
- **Contracted versus transactional** revenue — how visible is next year?
- **Geographic and segment mix** — which parts are growing, and at what margin?

### Operating profitability and pricing power

**The cost structure question** (FSA LM12): what proportion of costs is **fixed** versus **variable**?
This determines **operating leverage** — the sensitivity of profit to revenue — and therefore how
the company behaves in both a boom and a downturn.

**Pricing power** is the single most informative attribute. A company with pricing power can pass
through input cost inflation and preserve margins; one without it absorbs the cost.

**How to assess it, from the statements:**

| Evidence | What it shows |
| --- | --- |
| **Gross margin stability through an input cost cycle** | Direct evidence of pass-through ability |
| **Price increases without volume loss** | The strongest evidence available |
| **Gross margin versus peers**, sustained | Relative position |
| **Customer concentration** | Concentrated buyers erode pricing power (Porter) |
| **Product differentiation and switching costs** | Structural basis for pricing power |

> The connection to LM9: pricing power **is** the company-level expression of the five forces. Weak
> buyer power and weak substitutes give it; concentrated buyers and close substitutes remove it.

**Margin analysis** (FSA LM11): read gross, operating, and net margins **together** to locate where
any change is happening. Gross flat but operating falling means the problem is in operating
expenses, not pricing.

### Working capital, capital investment, and capital structure

**Working capital** (Corporate Issuers LM4): the cash conversion cycle, its trend, and how it
compares to peers. A lengthening CCC consumes cash and is an early operational warning. A negative
CCC is a structural funding advantage.

**Capital investments:**

| Question | Why it matters |
| --- | --- |
| **Maintenance vs. growth capex** | Maintenance is non-discretionary; only growth capex is a choice. Free cash flow before growth capex is a better measure of what the business generates |
| **Capex as a % of sales vs. peers** | Capital intensity; and whether the company is under-investing |
| **Asset age** (FSA LM7) | An ageing base predicts a capex surge, which current FCF does not reflect |
| **Returns on incremental capital** | The real test — is new investment earning above WACC? |
| **Acquisition track record** | Serial acquirers destroy value more often than not; check historical ROIC after each deal |

**Capital structure** (Corporate Issuers LM6): leverage relative to peers and to the business's
cyclicality, the **debt maturity schedule** (refinancing risk), covenant headroom, and whether the
structure suits the asset base. A company with high operating leverage should carry low financial
leverage.

### Putting it together

The synthesis that makes a company analysis useful:

```
Industry economics (LM9)  →  the range of achievable returns
Competitive position      →  where in that range this company sits
Durability of advantage   →  how long it stays there
                          ↓
                   The forecast (LM8)
                          ↓
                   The valuation (LM6, LM7)
```

> **The single question a company analysis must answer:** *what does this company do that its
> competitors cannot easily copy, and how long will that last?* Everything else is supporting
> evidence. If the answer is "nothing", the forecast should show margin reversion to the industry
> mean — and most of the time, that is the correct answer.

## Formulas to know cold

```
REVENUE DECOMPOSITION
  Revenue = Volume × Price
  Growth decomposition: (1 + volume growth)(1 + price growth) − 1
  By model: units × ASP · subscribers × ARPU (with churn) · transactions × take rate
            · market size × share · stores × sales per store (like-for-like + new)

OPERATING LEVERAGE
  DOL = %Δ operating income / %Δ units = Q(P − V) / [Q(P − V) − F]

THE VALUE-CREATION TEST
  ROIC > WACC  →  creating value;  growth then ADDS value
  ROIC < WACC  →  destroying value; growth DESTROYS value faster

  ROIC = EBIT(1 − t) / Average invested capital

CAPITAL INVESTMENT
  Maintenance capex ≈ depreciation, roughly, for a steady-state business
  Growth capex = Total capex − Maintenance capex
  Asset age ≈ Accumulated depreciation / Annual depreciation expense
```

## Exam traps

> **Trap 1 — Assuming high current returns will persist.** In competitive markets abnormal returns
> **decay**. Sustained high ROIC requires a **named mechanism**. If you cannot name it, forecast
> reversion.

> **Trap 2 — Reading revenue growth without decomposing it.** Price growth in an inflationary period
> is not real growth. Separate volume from price.

> **Trap 3 — Treating all capex as discretionary.** **Maintenance capex is not.** A company reporting
> strong free cash flow while under-investing is borrowing from the future.

> **Trap 4 — Confusing growth with value creation.** Growth at a ROIC **below** WACC destroys value
> **faster** the more the company invests.

> **Trap 5 — Missing customer concentration.** A single customer at 40% of revenue is a risk that no
> margin analysis reveals. It also destroys pricing power.

> **Trap 6 — Ignoring the split between organic and acquired growth.** Acquired growth consumes
> capital and carries integration risk; check ROIC after each deal.

> **Trap 7 — Misreading "stuck in the middle" as a middle-of-the-road but viable position.** It is a
> failure state: neither lowest cost nor able to charge a premium.

## Self-check

*Closed book. Commit to an answer before opening the fold.*

**1.** A company has earned ROIC of 22% against a WACC of 9% for twelve consecutive years. What must you establish before forecasting that it continues?

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**Name the mechanism.** Thirteen years of a 13-point spread over the cost of capital means something is preventing competitors from competing it away. Identify what:

- **Cost advantage** — scale, proprietary process, input access, location
- **Intangible assets** — a brand customers pay a premium for, patents, regulatory licences
- **Switching costs** — the product is embedded in the customer's operations
- **Network effects** — value rises with users
- **Efficient scale** — the market supports only one or two profitable players

Then ask about **durability**: is the mechanism weakening? Are patents expiring, is the brand ageing, is technology eroding the switching cost, is a regulator opening the market?

**If you cannot name a mechanism, forecast reversion.** In a competitive market abnormal returns decay, and the base rate strongly favours decay. 'It has been high for twelve years' is a description, not an explanation, and extrapolating it is the most common error in company analysis.

</details>

**2.** Revenue grew 14%. Volume grew 2%, prices rose 11%, and an acquisition contributed the rest. Assess the quality of this growth.

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Decompose: organic = (1.02 × 1.11) − 1 = **13.2%**, so the acquisition contributed roughly **0.8 percentage points**.

**Quality assessment: weak.**

- Only **2% is volume** — real growth in units sold. The business is barely selling more.
- **11% is price**, which in an inflationary period is likely just passing through input costs, not capturing value. The question is whether **gross margin held** — if input costs rose more than 11%, the company is losing ground despite the headline.
- The acquisition contribution is small but **consumed capital**; check the return on that capital.

**What to check next:** gross margin trend (did the 11% cover input inflation?), whether the price increases cost any volume (did competitors take share?), and what happens to the headline growth rate if inflation moderates — the price component collapses and revenue growth falls toward 2–3%.

</details>

**3.** How do you assess pricing power from the financial statements alone?

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**(1) Gross margin stability through an input cost cycle.** The single best test. If input costs rose 15% and gross margin held, the company passed the cost through — it has pricing power. If margin compressed, it absorbed the cost.

**(2) Price increases without volume loss.** Where disclosed (retailers often split like-for-like growth into price and volume), a company raising prices while holding or growing units has genuine power. Rising price with falling volume is the opposite.

**(3) Gross margin relative to peers, sustained.** A persistent margin premium over direct competitors indicates a differentiated position rather than a cost advantage that would show at the operating line.

**(4) Customer concentration** from the segment note. Concentrated buyers erode pricing power structurally (Porter's buyer power).

**(5) The direction of receivables and inventory.** A company forced to offer extended terms or build inventory to move product is buying revenue, which is the behaviour of a business without pricing power.

Cross-check all of this against the **five forces** conclusion from LM9 — pricing power is the company-level expression of industry structure.

</details>

**4.** A company reports strong free cash flow, but accumulated depreciation is 85% of gross PP&E. What do you conclude?

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

The asset base is **near the end of its depreciable life** — average age is roughly 85% of average depreciable life (FSA LM7).

That means a **replacement capex cycle is imminent**, and the current free cash flow does not reflect it. The company has been harvesting an ageing asset base: depreciation is high (supporting reported cash flow via the add-back) while capex has been low.

**What to do:**
- Do **not** extrapolate current FCF. Normalise capex to at least maintenance level — for a steady-state business, maintenance capex is roughly equal to depreciation, so compare actual capex to depreciation over several years.
- Ask whether the under-investment has already damaged operations — rising maintenance costs, falling utilisation, quality problems.
- Check the capex guidance and any disclosed contractual commitments to acquire PP&E.

A DCF built on current free cash flow will overstate value substantially. This is one of the clearest cases where FSA disclosure analysis changes a valuation.

</details>
