## Core concepts

### Economic rights versus voting rights

The central idea: **economic ownership and control need not be proportional.**

| Right | What it is |
| --- | --- |
| **Economic** | Claim on dividends, on residual assets in liquidation, and on capital appreciation |
| **Voting (control)** | The right to elect directors and approve major corporate actions |

Structures that separate the two:

- **Dual (or multiple) class shares.** Class B shares carry 10 or 20 votes each while Class A carries
  one. A founder holding 12% of the economics can control 60% of the votes.
- **Non-voting shares.** Economic rights only.
- **Golden shares.** A special share, usually held by a government, carrying veto rights over
  specified decisions regardless of economic stake — common in privatised utilities and defence.
- **Pyramid structures and cross-holdings.** Control amplified through chains of holding companies.

> **The analytical consequence:** where votes and economics diverge, the **market for corporate
> control is disabled** (Corporate Issuers LM3) and minority shareholders have no remedy through
> voting. The governance burden falls entirely on a board the controller appoints. Assess it as a
> risk factor and check for related-party transactions, but note that some of the best long-run
> compounders have founder control — it is a risk, not a verdict.

### Voting mechanisms

| Mechanism | How it works | Who it favours |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Statutory voting** | One share, one vote, **per director position** | The **majority** holder — they elect every director |
| **Cumulative voting** | Total votes = shares × positions; the holder may **concentrate** them all on one candidate | **Minorities** — they can elect at least one director |
| **Majority voting** for directors | A director must receive more votes *for* than *withheld* | Shareholders |
| **Plurality voting** | The highest vote-getter wins, even unopposed with one vote | Incumbents |

**Cumulative voting is the one to understand mechanically.** With 1,000 shares and 5 board seats,
statutory voting gives you 1,000 votes for each of the 5 positions. Cumulative voting gives you
5,000 votes that you may cast **all on one candidate** — enough to elect a director even against a
much larger holder who must spread their votes.

### The voting process

Most shareholders do not attend meetings. They vote by **proxy** — authorising someone to vote on
their behalf.

| Participant | Role |
| --- | --- |
| **Management** | Prepares the proxy statement, recommends how to vote, and campaigns for its slate |
| **Board of directors** | Nominates directors; sets the agenda for shareholder approval |
| **Asset owners** | The ultimate beneficial owners — pension funds, endowments, insurers, individuals. They set voting policy but usually delegate execution |
| **Asset managers** | Vote the shares they hold on behalf of asset owners. Have a **fiduciary duty** to vote in clients' interests |
| **Proxy advisers** | Independent firms (ISS, Glass Lewis) that research resolutions and issue **voting recommendations** to institutions |
| **Custodians / transfer agents** | Administer the mechanics of vote collection |

**Matters typically voted on:** election of directors, appointment of the auditor, executive
compensation (**say-on-pay**), mergers and major asset sales, amendments to the articles, share
issuance authority, and **shareholder-sponsored resolutions**.

### Proxy advisers — influence and criticism

Proxy advisers matter because institutions hold thousands of positions and cannot research every
resolution. Their recommendations move large blocks of votes.

**The criticisms, which the exam expects you to know:**

- **Concentration** — two firms dominate the market globally, giving enormous influence
- **One-size-fits-all** — standardised policies may not fit a specific company's circumstances
- **Conflicts of interest** — some advisers also sell consulting services to the companies they rate
- **Insufficient resources** — thousands of meetings in a short proxy season
- **Robo-voting** — some institutions follow recommendations automatically without independent
  analysis, which would be a **failure of fiduciary duty** if the manager is voting client shares
  without exercising judgement

> The connection to Ethics is direct: **Standard III(A) Loyalty, Prudence, and Care** requires an
> asset manager to vote proxies in the client's best interest with due diligence. Outsourcing the
> decision entirely to a third party, without oversight, does not discharge that duty.

### Jurisdictional differences

| Dimension | Varies by |
| --- | --- |
| **Board structure** | **One-tier** (a single board, Anglo-American) vs. **two-tier** (a separate supervisory board and management board, common in Germany and the Netherlands) |
| **Employee representation** | **Co-determination** — employee directors on the supervisory board (Germany) |
| **Ownership concentration** | Dispersed (US, UK) vs. concentrated family or state ownership (much of Asia, continental Europe, Latin America) |
| **Minority protection** | Strength of legal remedies, mandatory tender offer rules, related-party transaction approval requirements |
| **Dual-class acceptance** | Permitted and common in some markets, restricted or prohibited in others |
| **Shareholder rights** | Right to call meetings, propose resolutions, access the proxy |

> The practical point: **governance quality cannot be assessed against a single global standard.**
> A two-tier board with employee representation is not worse governance than a one-tier board — it
> is a different allocation of control reflecting a different view of whose interests the company
> serves. What matters is whether the mechanisms in place actually constrain the specific conflict
> that dominates in that ownership structure.

## Formulas to know cold

```
CUMULATIVE VOTING
  Total votes available = Shares held × Number of director positions to be filled
  All may be cast for a single candidate.

  Minimum shares to guarantee electing one director (Droop-style formula):
      Shares needed = [Total shares outstanding / (Directors to elect + 1)] + 1

  Statutory voting: shares × 1 vote, per position, cast separately → the majority elects ALL
```

---

## Exam traps

> **Trap 1 — Statutory vs cumulative voting, and who benefits.** **Statutory favours the majority**
> (they win every seat). **Cumulative favours minorities** (concentration can secure a seat).

> **Trap 2 — Assuming one share equals one vote.** Dual-class structures, non-voting shares, and
> golden shares all break the link between economics and control.

> **Trap 3 — Treating founder control as automatically bad governance.** It removes takeover
> discipline and minority remedies, which is a real risk — but it can also permit a genuinely
> long-term orientation. Assess the related-party transactions, not the structure alone.

> **Trap 4 — Thinking asset managers can delegate the voting decision.** They have a **fiduciary
> duty** to vote in clients' interests. Following a proxy adviser without oversight (robo-voting)
> does not discharge it — a direct Ethics Standard III(A) issue.

> **Trap 5 — Judging all markets against a one-tier Anglo-American standard.** Two-tier boards and
> employee co-determination are different allocations of control, not deficiencies.

## Self-check

*Closed book. Commit to an answer before opening the fold.*

**1.** A company has 5 board seats and 100,000 shares outstanding. Under cumulative voting, how many shares are needed to guarantee electing one director?

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Shares needed = [100,000 / (5 + 1)] + 1 = 16,666.67 → **16,667 shares** (16.67%).

With cumulative voting, those 16,667 shares yield 16,667 × 5 = 83,335 votes, all castable for one candidate. No arrangement of the remaining 83,333 shares' 416,665 votes can produce five candidates each beating 83,335 — the majority would have to spread its votes across five seats, averaging 83,333 per candidate.

Under **statutory** voting, the same 16.67% holder elects **nobody** — the 83.33% majority wins all five seats. That contrast is the whole point of cumulative voting.

</details>

**2.** A founder holds 12% of the economics but 62% of the votes through a dual-class structure. What are the implications for a minority investor?

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**What is lost:**
- **No takeover discipline.** No hostile bid can succeed, so the market for corporate control cannot correct underperformance.
- **No voting remedy.** Minorities cannot replace directors, block a transaction, or force a strategic change.
- **Related-party transaction risk.** The controller can transact with entities they own more of, extracting value — and can approve those transactions themselves.
- **Misaligned incentives at the margin.** The founder bears only 12% of the economic cost of a value-destroying decision but retains 100% of the private benefits of control.

**What may be gained:** freedom from quarterly earnings pressure and the ability to invest on a genuinely long horizon. Several of the best long-run compounders have had founder control.

**What to check:** related-party transaction disclosures, the independence and tenure of directors, any sunset provision on the dual-class structure, and the founder's track record of capital allocation. The structure is a risk factor to price, not a disqualification.

</details>

**3.** Why is 'robo-voting' a problem for an asset manager?

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Because an asset manager voting shares held for clients has a **fiduciary duty** — Ethics **Standard III(A) Loyalty, Prudence, and Care** — to act in those clients' best interests with reasonable diligence.

Automatically implementing a proxy adviser's recommendation without independent review **delegates the judgement** but not the duty. If the recommendation is wrong for a particular holding — and one-size-fits-all policies frequently are — the manager has failed to exercise care on the client's behalf.

The problem compounds because **two firms dominate** the proxy advisory market, so robo-voting concentrates effective control over corporate governance in a handful of unaccountable private firms, and because some advisers have **conflicts of interest**, selling consulting to the same companies they rate.

What discharges the duty: using adviser research as an **input**, having a documented voting policy, reviewing significant or contested votes independently, and being able to explain any vote to a client.

</details>

**4.** Contrast a one-tier and a two-tier board structure.

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**One-tier** (Anglo-American): a **single board** containing both executive and non-executive directors. The same body sets strategy and oversees management, which makes the **independence** of the non-executives and the **separation of chair and CEO** critical safeguards.

**Two-tier** (Germany, Netherlands, and others): **two separate bodies**. A **management board** runs the company day to day; a **supervisory board**, on which no executive sits, appoints, monitors, and can dismiss the management board. In Germany, **co-determination** places employee representatives on the supervisory board — typically half in large companies.

**The difference in what is prioritised:** the one-tier model emphasises shareholder primacy and board agility; the two-tier model enforces a structural separation between running and overseeing, and formally admits employees as a stakeholder with representation.

Neither is superior in the abstract. The right question is whether the structure in place constrains the conflict that actually dominates in that company's ownership pattern — manager–shareholder conflict in a dispersed-ownership firm, controller–minority conflict in a concentrated one.

</details>
