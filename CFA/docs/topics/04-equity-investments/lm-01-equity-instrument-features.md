## Core concepts

### What equity is

A share represents an **ownership claim** on a company. Three attributes define it:

1. **Residual claim** — shareholders are paid last, after every creditor and preferred holder.
2. **Limited liability** — the maximum loss is the amount invested.
3. **Potentially perpetual** — shares have no maturity date.

### Types of equity instrument

| Instrument | Key features |
| --- | --- |
| **Common (ordinary) shares** | Residual claim, voting rights, **discretionary** dividends, unlimited upside |
| **Preference (preferred) shares** | **Fixed** dividend, priority over common in dividends and liquidation, usually **non-voting** |
| **Private equity** | Ownership in a non-listed company; illiquid, long horizon |
| **Depository receipts** | A claim on foreign shares, trading in a local market and currency |

**Common share variants:**

- **Callable common shares** — the issuer may repurchase them at a set price. Benefits the **issuer**
  (it can buy back cheaply if the share price rises), so investors require compensation.
- **Putable common shares** — the holder may sell them back at a set price. Benefits the **investor**
  (a price floor), so they tend to trade at a premium and reduce the issuer's cost of capital.

**Preference share variants** — these combinations are directly examinable:

| Type | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| **Cumulative** | Missed dividends **accrue** and must be paid in full before any common dividend |
| **Non-cumulative** | Missed dividends are simply lost |
| **Participating** | Receives the fixed dividend **plus** a share of profits above a threshold; often also extra in liquidation |
| **Non-participating** | Fixed dividend only |
| **Convertible** | May be converted into a fixed number of common shares at the holder's option |
| **Callable / putable** | Redeemable at the issuer's / holder's option |

> **Preference shares sit between debt and equity.** Like debt: a fixed payment, priority over
> common, usually no vote. Like equity: the dividend is not a legal obligation (failing to pay it is
> not default), it is paid from after-tax income and is **not tax-deductible for the issuer**, and it
> ranks behind all debt in liquidation.

### Depository receipts

A depository bank holds the foreign shares and issues receipts that trade locally.

| Type | Detail |
| --- | --- |
| **Sponsored** | The **foreign company participates**; holders have voting rights; greater disclosure |
| **Unsponsored** | The depository bank acts alone; the **bank retains the voting rights** |
| **ADR** (American Depository Receipt) | Trades in the US, in USD |
| **GDR** (Global Depository Receipt) | Trades outside the issuer's home market, often in London or Luxembourg |
| **GRS** (Global Registered Share) | The same share trading on multiple exchanges in multiple currencies |

ADR levels: **Level I** (over-the-counter, minimal disclosure), **Level II** (exchange-listed,
fuller disclosure, no new capital), **Level III** (exchange-listed **and raises new capital**),
plus **Rule 144A** private placements to qualified institutional buyers.

> **Trap:** only **sponsored** depository receipts carry voting rights to the holder. With an
> unsponsored receipt, the depository bank votes.

### Public vs. private equity

| | **Public** | **Private** |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Liquidity** | High — continuous trading | **Very low** — exit requires a sale or IPO |
| **Price** | Observable and continuous | Unobservable; periodic appraisal at best |
| **Disclosure** | Extensive and mandatory | Minimal, negotiated |
| **Investor base** | Anyone | Institutions and accredited investors |
| **Horizon** | Any | **Long** — typically 5–10 years |
| **Influence** | Diffuse; one vote per share | **Concentrated** — board seats, control |
| **Regulation** | Heavy | Light |
| **Valuation** | Market price | Model-based, with a **liquidity discount** |

**Forms of private equity investment:** venture capital (early stage), leveraged buyouts (mature
companies, debt-financed), and private investment in public equity (**PIPE** — a discounted private
placement by a listed company, usually because it needs capital quickly). Full treatment in
Alternative Investments LM3.

## Formulas to know cold

No formulas. Memorise the preference-share combinations and the depository receipt types:

```
PREFERENCE SHARES — the four dimensions (any combination is possible):
    Cumulative / Non-cumulative        — do missed dividends accrue?
    Participating / Non-participating  — share in profits above the fixed dividend?
    Convertible / Non-convertible      — convert into common?
    Callable / Putable / Neither       — who holds the redemption right?

CALLABLE benefits the ISSUER  → investor requires compensation → lower price
PUTABLE  benefits the HOLDER  → premium price → lower cost of capital for the issuer

DEPOSITORY RECEIPTS
    Sponsored   → company participates, HOLDER gets voting rights
    Unsponsored → bank acts alone,      BANK retains voting rights
    ADR Level I (OTC) / II (listed) / III (listed + RAISES CAPITAL) / Rule 144A
```

## Exam traps

> **Trap 1 — Callable vs putable, and who benefits.** **Callable benefits the issuer**; **putable
> benefits the holder**. The direction of the price effect follows from that.

> **Trap 2 — Cumulative vs participating.** **Cumulative** = missed dividends accrue. **Participating**
> = shares in profits above the fixed dividend. Different dimensions entirely; a share can be both.

> **Trap 3 — Treating preference dividends as tax-deductible.** They are **not**. Interest is; preferred
> dividends are paid from after-tax income. This is why preferred is more expensive than debt for the
> issuer (Corporate Issuers LM6).

> **Trap 4 — Missing a preferred dividend is default.** It is **not**. The dividend is not a legal
> obligation, which is precisely why preferred is equity and not debt.

> **Trap 5 — Voting rights on depository receipts.** Only **sponsored** receipts pass voting rights
> to the holder.

> **Trap 6 — Assuming all ADR levels raise capital.** Only **Level III** does.

## Self-check

*Closed book. Commit to an answer before opening the fold.*

**1.** A company misses its preferred dividend for two years, then wants to pay a common dividend. What must it do if the preferred is cumulative? What if it is non-cumulative?

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**Cumulative:** all arrears — both missed years **plus** the current year's preferred dividend — must be paid **in full** before any common dividend can be declared. The unpaid amounts accrued as a claim.

**Non-cumulative:** the missed dividends are simply **lost**. The company need only pay the current period's preferred dividend before paying common.

This is why cumulative preference shares are worth more, and why the cumulative feature is standard in most issues — without it, an issuer could suspend the preferred dividend indefinitely and resume common dividends at will.

</details>

**2.** Contrast callable and putable common shares in terms of who benefits and the effect on price.

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**Callable** — the **issuer** has the right to repurchase at a set price. If the share price rises above the call price, the issuer can buy back cheaply, capping the investor's upside. The option belongs to the issuer, so investors demand compensation: callable shares trade at a **discount** and the issuer's cost of capital is **higher**.

**Putable** — the **holder** has the right to sell back at a set price. This is a **price floor**, limiting the investor's downside. The option belongs to the investor, so they pay for it: putable shares trade at a **premium** and the issuer's cost of capital is **lower**.

The general principle, which recurs throughout Fixed Income: **whoever holds the option pays for it**.

</details>

**3.** Why do preference shares sit between debt and equity, and why is that costly for the issuer?

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**Debt-like features:** a **fixed** dividend payment, **priority** over common shares in both dividends and liquidation, and usually **no voting rights**.

**Equity-like features:** the dividend is **not a legal obligation** (missing it is not default and does not trigger bankruptcy), the shares have **no maturity**, and they rank **behind all debt** in liquidation.

**Why it is costly for the issuer:** preferred dividends are paid from **after-tax income** and are **not tax-deductible**, unlike interest. So the issuer gets the fixed-payment obligation of debt with none of the tax shield, while investors demand a return above debt because they rank behind it. Preferred is therefore typically the **most expensive** form of capital short of common equity, which is why it is used selectively — often in regulated industries where it counts toward capital requirements, or in negotiated private financings.

</details>

**4.** An investor buys an unsponsored GDR. What do they get, and what do they not get?

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**They get:** economic exposure to the foreign shares — price movements and (after the depository's fees) dividends — in a locally traded, locally settled security, avoiding the operational complexity of trading directly in the foreign market.

**They do not get:** **voting rights**. In an unsponsored programme the depository bank holds the underlying shares and retains the votes. They also get **less disclosure** than a sponsored programme, since the foreign company has not agreed to participate and has no obligation to the receipt holders.

If voting matters — for an activist position, or for any governance-sensitive holding — a **sponsored** receipt or direct ownership of the underlying shares is required.

</details>
