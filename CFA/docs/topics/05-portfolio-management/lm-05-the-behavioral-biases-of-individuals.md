## Core concepts

### The two families

| | **Cognitive errors** | **Emotional biases** |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Source | Faulty **reasoning** — statistical, information-processing, or memory errors | **Feelings, impulses, intuition** |
| Can they be corrected? | **Yes, to a large extent** — through education, data, and process | **Only partly** — they are usually **accommodated** rather than corrected |
| Adviser response | Educate, provide information, impose a structured process | **Moderate and adapt** the plan around them |

> **The distinction is the point of the first LOS.** Cognitive errors respond to evidence. Emotional
> biases do not — telling a loss-averse client that their fear is irrational does not remove it. The
> practical consequence is different treatment: **correct cognitive errors, accommodate emotional
> ones.**

### Cognitive errors

**Belief perseverance** — clinging to a prior view.

| Bias | Description | Consequence |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Conservatism** | Under-reacting to new information; updating too slowly | Holding a stale thesis after the facts have changed |
| **Confirmation** | Seeking and over-weighting evidence that supports the existing view | Ignoring disconfirming data; echo chambers |
| **Representativeness** | Judging by resemblance to a stereotype; over-extrapolating small samples | Chasing a fund with three good years; **base rate neglect** (QM LM6) |
| **Illusion of control** | Believing you influence outcomes you do not | Over-trading; excessive model detail; concentrated positions |
| **Hindsight** | Believing past events were predictable | Overconfidence in forecasting; unfair performance evaluation |

**Information processing** — errors in how information is handled.

| Bias | Description | Consequence |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Anchoring and adjustment** | Fixating on an initial value and adjusting insufficiently | Anchoring on a purchase price or an analyst's prior target |
| **Mental accounting** | Treating money differently by source or label | Ignoring portfolio-level risk; spending a "windfall" recklessly |
| **Framing** | Answering differently depending on how a choice is presented | Risk-seeking when framed as losses, risk-averse when framed as gains |
| **Availability** | Over-weighting what comes easily to mind — recent or vivid events | Over-insuring against dramatic risks; home bias; recency-driven allocation |

### Emotional biases

| Bias | Description | Consequence |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Loss aversion** | Losses hurt roughly **twice as much** as equivalent gains feel good | **Holding losers too long and selling winners too early** (the disposition effect); myopic loss aversion leading to excessive conservatism |
| **Overconfidence** | Over-estimating one's own knowledge and prediction accuracy | Under-diversification; **excessive trading**; ranges far too narrow |
| **Self-control** | Failing to act in one's own long-term interest | Under-saving; spending capital rather than income |
| **Status quo** | Doing nothing by default | Failing to rebalance; leaving an inherited portfolio untouched for decades |
| **Endowment** | Valuing what you own above what you would pay for it | Refusing to sell inherited or employer stock |
| **Regret aversion** | Avoiding decisions for fear of being wrong | Herding; excessive conservatism; paralysis |

> **The two most costly in practice are overconfidence and loss aversion.** Overconfidence produces
> under-diversification and turnover that consumes returns in costs. Loss aversion produces the
> **disposition effect** — selling winners to realise a gain and holding losers to avoid realising a
> loss, which is also tax-inefficient in most jurisdictions, since it defers gains realisation
> exactly backwards.

### Market consequences

Behavioural biases aggregate into observable market phenomena that standard theory does not explain:

| Phenomenon | Behavioural explanation |
| --- | --- |
| **Momentum** | Under-reaction (conservatism) followed by over-reaction (herding, representativeness) |
| **Bubbles and crashes** | Overconfidence, herding, regret aversion, availability |
| **Excess volatility** | Prices move far more than fundamentals justify |
| **Value premium** | Representativeness — extrapolating poor recent results onto cheap stocks too far |
| **Home bias** | Familiarity and availability — investors hold far more domestic equity than diversification implies |
| **Equity premium puzzle** | **Myopic loss aversion** — investors check portfolios too often and demand an implausibly high premium to bear short-term volatility |
| **Disposition effect** | Loss aversion at the level of individual positions |
| **Calendar and size anomalies** | Partly behavioural, partly measurement and liquidity artefacts |

> **Behavioural finance does not claim markets are always wrong.** It claims that the assumption of
> perfectly rational agents is descriptively false, and that the resulting deviations are
> **systematic** — predictable in direction — rather than random. Systematic deviations can in
> principle be exploited, but limits to arbitrage (costs, risk, capital constraints, and the fact
> that mispricing can worsen before it corrects) mean they often persist.

### Managing biases in practice

| Bias family | Approach |
| --- | --- |
| **Cognitive** | **Correct.** Provide data, use a structured process, keep a decision journal, require an explicit falsification condition, use base rates |
| **Emotional** | **Accommodate.** Adapt the plan so the client can live with it; automate decisions (rebalancing rules, automatic contributions); reduce the frequency of portfolio reporting |

**The general remedies that work on both:** a **written IPS** (LM4) agreed in calm conditions, an
**automated rebalancing policy** that removes discretion, **pre-commitment** to what would change
your mind, and **reviewing decisions rather than outcomes** — a good decision can have a bad outcome
and vice versa, and evaluating on outcomes alone reinforces hindsight bias.

> **Reducing reporting frequency is a genuine, evidence-backed intervention.** Myopic loss aversion
> is driven by how often an investor evaluates the portfolio: the more frequently you look, the more
> losses you see, and the more conservative you become. A long-horizon investor who reviews annually
> rather than daily will hold a more appropriate allocation.

## Formulas to know cold

No formulas. Two classifications to memorise cold:

```
COGNITIVE ERRORS — faulty reasoning; CAN be corrected with education and process
  Belief perseverance:    conservatism · confirmation · representativeness
                          · illusion of control · hindsight
  Information processing: anchoring · mental accounting · framing · availability

EMOTIONAL BIASES — feelings and impulses; usually ACCOMMODATED, not corrected
  loss aversion · overconfidence · self-control · status quo · endowment · regret aversion

THE ADVISER RULE
  Cognitive  →  CORRECT     (data, structured process, base rates)
  Emotional  →  ACCOMMODATE (adapt the plan, automate, reduce reporting frequency)
```

## Exam traps

> **Trap 1 — Misclassifying a bias.** The test is the **source**: faulty **reasoning** (cognitive) or
> **feeling** (emotional). Overconfidence is **emotional** despite sounding like a reasoning error;
> representativeness is **cognitive** despite feeling intuitive.

> **Trap 2 — Assuming all biases can be educated away.** **Emotional biases generally cannot.** The
> correct response is to **accommodate** them in the plan.

> **Trap 3 — Confusing anchoring with conservatism.** **Anchoring** is fixating on a specific
> **number** and adjusting insufficiently. **Conservatism** is under-reacting to **new information**
> generally.

> **Trap 4 — Confusing representativeness with availability.** **Representativeness** judges by
> resemblance to a stereotype or a small sample. **Availability** over-weights what is easy to
> recall — recent or vivid.

> **Trap 5 — Getting the disposition effect backwards.** Loss aversion makes investors **hold losers
> too long and sell winners too early**.

> **Trap 6 — Thinking behavioural finance says markets are always wrong.** It says deviations are
> **systematic**, not that they are always exploitable — limits to arbitrage keep many in place.

> **Trap 7 — Evaluating decisions by outcomes.** A good decision can produce a bad outcome. Judging
> on outcomes alone reinforces hindsight bias.

## Self-check

*Closed book. Commit to an answer before opening the fold.*

**1.** An investor refuses to sell a stock that has fallen 40%, saying 'I'll sell when it gets back to what I paid.' Identify the biases and say how an adviser should respond.

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**Anchoring** (cognitive) — fixating on the purchase price, which is irrelevant to the stock's future prospects. The market does not know or care what they paid.

**Loss aversion** (emotional) — refusing to **realise** the loss, because an unrealised loss feels different from a realised one even though the economic position is identical. This is the **disposition effect**.

Possibly also **regret aversion** (emotional) — selling would confirm the mistake.

**Response — treat the two differently:**

For the **anchoring** (cognitive, correctable): ask the question that reframes it — *'if you held cash today instead of this stock, would you buy it at the current price?'* If the answer is no, the position should be sold; the purchase price is a sunk cost (Corporate Issuers LM5). Provide current analysis of the company's prospects.

For the **loss aversion** (emotional, accommodate): point out the tax benefit of realising the loss where applicable, propose a partial sale rather than a full one to lower the emotional barrier, and consider switching into a similar exposure so the decision feels like a reallocation rather than a defeat.

</details>

**2.** Distinguish overconfidence from illusion of control, and say which family each belongs to.

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**Overconfidence** is **emotional**. It is over-estimating one's own **knowledge and predictive accuracy** — believing forecasts are more precise than they are, and setting confidence intervals far too narrow. Consequences: **under-diversification** (why hold 40 stocks when you know which 5 are best?) and **excessive trading**, which consumes returns in transaction costs.

**Illusion of control** is **cognitive**. It is believing you can **influence outcomes you cannot** — that more research, more model detail, or more frequent monitoring improves results in areas governed by chance. Consequences: over-trading, excessively detailed models, and concentrated positions in 'well understood' companies.

**Why the classification matters:** illusion of control is a reasoning error and responds to **evidence** — showing that a 40-tab model does not forecast better than a 5-tab one, or that a trading record underperforms a buy-and-hold benchmark. Overconfidence is emotional and largely does not — the standard intervention is a **decision journal** that makes the person's own track record unavoidable over time.

</details>

**3.** How does myopic loss aversion help explain the equity premium puzzle, and what does that imply for practice?

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**The puzzle:** the historical equity risk premium over bonds is far larger than standard utility theory can justify — it would require implausibly extreme risk aversion.

**Myopic loss aversion** combines two elements:
- **Loss aversion** — losses hurt roughly twice as much as equivalent gains feel good
- **Myopia** — investors evaluate their portfolios **frequently**, over short horizons

Over a **one-day** horizon, equities show a loss roughly 46% of the time. Over a **one-year** horizon, roughly 25% of the time. Over **twenty years**, historically almost never. An investor who checks daily therefore experiences constant, painful losses — and demands a very large premium to keep holding. An investor who checks once a decade experiences almost none.

**The equity premium is therefore large because investors evaluate too often**, not because equities are objectively that risky over long horizons.

**The practical implication, which is genuinely actionable:** **reduce reporting and review frequency** for long-horizon investors. An annual review produces a more appropriate allocation than a daily app notification, for the same person with the same true risk capacity. This is one of the few behavioural findings with a clean, cheap intervention.

</details>

**4.** Why should an adviser correct cognitive errors but accommodate emotional biases?

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Because they have **different sources and different responsiveness to intervention**.

**Cognitive errors** arise from faulty **reasoning** — statistical mistakes, information-processing shortcuts, memory distortions. They are, in principle, **correctable**: showing someone the base rate, providing the data they overlooked, or imposing a structured process genuinely changes the decision. Correcting them improves outcomes at no cost to the client's comfort.

**Emotional biases** arise from **feelings**, and feelings do not yield to argument. Telling a loss-averse client that their fear is statistically unwarranted does not make them less afraid. Attempting to override an emotional bias produces a plan the client **will not follow** — they abandon it at the worst possible moment, which is far more damaging than the suboptimal-but-held alternative.

**So the adviser's job differs by family:**
- **Cognitive** → educate, provide evidence, impose process
- **Emotional** → adapt the plan so the client can live with it, automate decisions to remove them from the moment of stress, and reduce the frequency of exposure to short-term fluctuations

**The exception:** if an emotional bias is severe enough to threaten the client's financial goals — chronic under-saving from self-control bias, or an allocation so conservative it guarantees failure — moderation must be attempted, with education about the consequence. But the plan must still be one they will actually keep.

</details>
