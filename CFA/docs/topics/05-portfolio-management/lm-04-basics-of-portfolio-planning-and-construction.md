## Core concepts

### Why a written IPS

| Reason | What it prevents |
| --- | --- |
| **Discipline** | Panic selling in a drawdown; chasing performance at a top |
| **Clarity of objectives** | Vague mandates and drifting strategy |
| **Accountability** | Disputes about what was agreed |
| **Continuity** | Loss of understanding when a manager or adviser changes |
| **Benchmark specification** | Performance measured against a standard agreed **in advance** |
| **Regulatory and fiduciary** | Evidence that suitability was assessed (**Ethics Standard III(C)**) |

> The most valuable function is **behavioural**. An IPS is a commitment device written in calm
> conditions to constrain decisions made in stressed ones. Its real test is whether it prevents the
> client from selling everything after a 30% decline.

### Major components of an IPS

1. **A brief client description**
2. **Purpose and scope**
3. **Duties and responsibilities** — of the client, the manager, and the custodian
4. **Procedures** — for updating the IPS and responding to unexpected events
5. **Investment objectives** — return and risk
6. **Investment constraints** — liquidity, time horizon, tax, legal/regulatory, unique circumstances
7. **Investment guidelines** — permitted assets, leverage, derivatives
8. **Evaluation and review** — benchmark, frequency, reporting
9. **Appendices** — the strategic asset allocation and the rebalancing policy

> **Memorise the five constraints** with the mnemonic **L-T-T-L-U**: **L**iquidity, **T**ime horizon,
> **T**axes, **L**egal and regulatory, **U**nique circumstances.

### Risk objectives — willingness versus ability

This distinction is the single most examinable idea in the module.

| | **Ability (capacity) to take risk** | **Willingness to take risk** |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Based on | **Objective** financial circumstances | **Psychological** attitude |
| Determined by | Wealth relative to needs, time horizon, income stability, liquidity requirements, the importance of the goal | Personality, experience, stated preferences, behaviour in past drawdowns |
| Assessed from | Financial analysis | Interview, questionnaire, observed behaviour |

**The resolution rule:**

| Situation | What to do |
| --- | --- |
| **Ability low, willingness high** | **Ability governs.** Take the lower. Educate the client on why their circumstances cannot support the risk they want |
| **Ability high, willingness low** | **Willingness governs in practice.** Take the lower — but educate, because the client may be leaving needed return on the table. Never override the client |
| **Both low** | Conservative |
| **Both high** | Higher risk tolerance is appropriate |

> **The governing principle: take the LOWER of the two.** An adviser must never impose risk a
> client's circumstances cannot bear, and must never impose risk a client will abandon at the worst
> moment. Education is legitimate; overriding is not.
>
> The ethical dimension is direct: **Standard III(C) Suitability** requires that recommendations be
> consistent with the client's financial situation, constraints, and objectives.

### Return objectives

| Type | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| **Required return** | What the client **needs** to meet their goals |
| **Desired return** | What the client **wants** |
| **Absolute** | A stated level — "6% a year", or "inflation + 4%" |
| **Relative** | Versus a benchmark — "the index + 150bp" |

**Return objectives must be stated after fees, after tax, and in real terms where appropriate** —
and, critically, they must be **consistent with the risk objective**. A client wanting 10% a year
with a maximum drawdown of 5% has stated an infeasible combination, and identifying that is part of
the planning process.

### The five constraints

**Liquidity.** Anticipated cash needs — spending, known obligations, emergency reserves. High
liquidity needs rule out private equity, direct real estate, and lock-up vehicles regardless of
their return characteristics.

**Time horizon.** Longer horizons permit more risk, because there is time to recover from
drawdowns and illiquidity can be tolerated. Horizons can be **multi-stage** (working years, then
retirement) and each stage may need a different allocation.

**Taxes.** For taxable investors: the treatment of income versus capital gains, tax-deferred
accounts, loss harvesting, and **asset location** — placing tax-inefficient assets in sheltered
accounts and tax-efficient ones in taxable accounts.

**Legal and regulatory.** Trust law, prudent investor rules, pension funding regulation, insurance
capital requirements, restrictions on insiders' holdings.

**Unique circumstances.** Anything else: concentrated holdings in an employer's stock, ESG
restrictions, illiquid legacy assets, a family business, a special-needs dependant.

### Asset classes and allocation

**Asset classes should be specified so that they are:**

- **Homogeneous** internally — assets within a class behave similarly
- **Mutually exclusive** — no asset falls into two classes
- **Diversifying** — low correlation with other classes
- **Together comprehensive** — spanning the investable universe
- **Individually large enough** to absorb a meaningful allocation without moving prices

**Strategic asset allocation (SAA)** is the long-term target, derived from the IPS. It is the
single most consequential decision in portfolio management: the allocation between broad asset
classes explains the great majority of the **variation in returns over time** for a typical
diversified portfolio.

**Tactical asset allocation** is short-term deviation from the SAA to exploit perceived mispricing,
within bands set by the IPS.

**Rebalancing** returns the portfolio to target weights. The policy specifies either **calendar**
rebalancing (quarterly, annually) or **threshold** rebalancing (when a weight drifts beyond a band).
Rebalancing is inherently contrarian — it sells what has risen and buys what has fallen — which is
another way the IPS enforces discipline against behavioural bias.

**Risk budgeting** allocates the portfolio's total risk tolerance across asset classes and
strategies, rather than allocating capital alone.

### ESG integration in portfolio planning

ESG preferences belong in the IPS under **unique circumstances** or in the investment guidelines,
and they must be **specific** — "avoid controversial weapons" is implementable; "invest
responsibly" is not.

Approaches (Corporate Issuers LM2): negative screening, positive/best-in-class screening, ESG
integration into valuation, thematic investing, engagement and active ownership, and impact
investing.

**Practical considerations for the IPS:**

- **Be specific** about exclusions and thresholds, or the mandate is unmanageable
- **Acknowledge the tracking error** that exclusions create against a standard benchmark, and agree
  an appropriate benchmark up front
- **Specify the data source**, given that ESG ratings diverge widely across providers
- **Distinguish** a values-based restriction (the client's ethics, accepted as a constraint) from a
  financially motivated integration (ESG as a risk factor, part of the return objective) — they have
  different implications for benchmarking and for fiduciary duty

```
  ABILITY (capacity)  = objective: wealth vs needs, horizon, income stability, goal importance

IPS COMPONENTS
  Client description · Purpose · Duties & responsibilities · Procedures
  · OBJECTIVES (return, risk) · CONSTRAINTS (L-T-T-L-U) · Guidelines
  · Evaluation & review · Appendices (strategic asset allocation, rebalancing policy)

ASSET CLASS SPECIFICATION — classes should be
  homogeneous · mutually exclusive · diversifying · comprehensive · investable at scale
```

## Exam traps

> **Trap 1 — Resolving a willingness/ability conflict the wrong way.** **Take the LOWER.** Educate
> the client, but never impose risk beyond either limit.

> **Trap 2 — Confusing the two.** **Ability is objective** (financial circumstances). **Willingness
> is psychological** (attitude). A wealthy client with a long horizon has high *ability* regardless
> of how nervous they are.

> **Trap 3 — Return objectives stated before fees and tax.** They must be **net** and, where
> relevant, **real**.

> **Trap 4 — Accepting an infeasible objective pair.** "10% a year with a 5% maximum drawdown" is
> not a mandate, it is a contradiction. Identifying that is part of planning.

> **Trap 5 — Forgetting a constraint.** **L-T-T-L-U.** Unique circumstances is the one most often
> omitted, and it is where concentrated stock positions and ESG restrictions live.

> **Trap 6 — Treating rebalancing as optional.** It enforces the strategic allocation and is
> inherently contrarian. Without it, the portfolio drifts toward whatever has risen most.

> **Trap 7 — Vague ESG language in an IPS.** "Invest responsibly" is unimplementable. Specify
> exclusions, thresholds, the data source, and the benchmark.

## Self-check

*Closed book. Commit to an answer before opening the fold.*

**1.** A 32-year-old with a stable professional income, no dependants, 400,000 saved and no immediate needs says they cannot tolerate seeing their portfolio fall by more than 5%. How do you resolve this?

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**Ability to take risk: high.** Long horizon (30+ years to retirement), stable income, no dependants, no near-term liquidity needs, and substantial human capital remaining. Financially, they can bear significant volatility.

**Willingness to take risk: low.** A 5% maximum tolerance is very conservative and would essentially rule out equities.

**Resolution: take the LOWER — the willingness constraint governs in practice.** But this is not where the conversation ends.

**What to do:**
1. **Educate.** Explain the trade-off concretely: a portfolio constrained to 5% volatility will likely not keep pace with inflation over 30 years, and the real risk to a 32-year-old is not a drawdown — it is **arriving at retirement with insufficient assets**.
2. **Explore the source.** Is this low willingness based on a bad experience, on unfamiliarity, or on a genuine settled preference? Newly-investing clients often overstate their sensitivity before experiencing a market cycle.
3. **Start conservative and revisit.** Set an allocation the client will actually hold, and review annually.

**What not to do:** override them. A portfolio the client abandons in the first drawdown is worse than a conservative one they hold — and imposing unsuitable risk breaches **Standard III(C) Suitability**.

</details>

**2.** Name the five investment constraints and give an example of each for a retired couple living off their portfolio.

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**Liquidity** — they need regular withdrawals to fund living expenses, plus a reserve for unexpected medical costs. This rules out lock-up vehicles and argues for a cash buffer covering 1–2 years of spending.

**Time horizon** — potentially 25–30 years, and **multi-stage**: an active early retirement with higher discretionary spending, then a later phase with higher healthcare costs. Long enough to justify meaningful equity exposure despite being 'retired'.

**Taxes** — withdrawals may be taxed differently depending on the account; **asset location** matters (income-producing assets in tax-sheltered accounts), and the order of drawdown across account types affects the after-tax outcome.

**Legal and regulatory** — any trust arrangements, estate planning structures, required minimum distributions from retirement accounts in some jurisdictions.

**Unique circumstances** — a concentrated holding in a former employer's stock, a property they intend to leave to children, a dependant with special needs, or specific charitable intentions.

</details>

**3.** Why is the strategic asset allocation described as the single most consequential decision in portfolio management?

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Because the allocation across broad asset classes explains the great majority of the **variation in a diversified portfolio's returns over time**. Equity, bond, and cash exposures have very different return and risk characteristics, and the mix determines the portfolio's fundamental behaviour far more than which particular securities are held within each class.

Security selection and tactical deviations operate at the margin around that decision.

**Two important qualifications**, because this claim is often overstated:
- The classic studies measure the share of **variance over time** explained by policy allocation for a typical diversified fund — not the share of *return level*, and not a claim that selection never matters.
- For a concentrated or highly active portfolio, selection matters a great deal more.

**The practical point that survives:** the SAA is the decision made in the planning stage, derived from the IPS, and it should be set deliberately and changed rarely. Most investor harm comes from abandoning the allocation in a drawdown, not from picking the wrong securities within it — which is why the rebalancing policy belongs in the IPS.

</details>

**4.** What does an IPS need to say about ESG for the mandate to be implementable?

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**Specifics, not intentions.**

- **Which exclusions, at what threshold.** 'No tobacco' needs a revenue threshold — does a supermarket selling cigarettes count? 'No fossil fuels' needs a definition covering producers, refiners, distributors, and utilities.
- **Which approach** — negative screening, best-in-class, integration into valuation, thematic, engagement, or impact. These produce very different portfolios.
- **Which data source**, given that ESG ratings **diverge widely across providers** (Corporate Issuers LM2). Two agencies can rate the same company at opposite ends.
- **The benchmark.** Exclusions create **tracking error** against a standard index. Agree an appropriate custom or screened benchmark up front, or the manager will be measured against something the mandate prevents them from matching.
- **Whether the basis is values or finance.** A **values-based** restriction is a client constraint, accepted and worked within. A **financially motivated** integration treats ESG as a risk factor and belongs in the return objective. The distinction matters for benchmarking, for reporting, and for the fiduciary framing.

'Invest responsibly' is not a mandate — it is an aspiration the manager cannot implement or be measured against.

</details>
