## Core concepts

### The portfolio approach

Evaluate an investment by **what it contributes to the portfolio**, not by its standalone
characteristics. A volatile asset with low correlation to the rest of the portfolio can **reduce**
total portfolio risk — and is therefore attractive despite looking dangerous on its own.

This follows directly from LM1: portfolio risk depends on **covariances**, not just on individual
variances. A security is judged by its marginal contribution to portfolio risk and return.

**Diversification ratio** — a simple measure of the benefit achieved:

```
Diversification ratio = σ of the portfolio / σ of an equally weighted single-asset average
```

### The portfolio management process

| Step | What happens | Output |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **1. Planning** | Understand the client: objectives, constraints, circumstances | The **investment policy statement (IPS)** |
| **2. Execution** | Asset allocation, security analysis, portfolio construction | The portfolio |
| **3. Feedback** | Monitor, rebalance, measure and attribute performance | Reports, and revisions to the IPS |

The IPS (LM4) is the governing document. Everything in execution must be traceable to it, and the
feedback stage loops back to revise it when circumstances change.

### Types of investor

| Investor | Horizon | Risk tolerance | Liquidity need | Distinctive feature |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Individual** | Varies with life stage | Varies widely | Varies | **Taxable**; behavioural biases (LM5); goal-based |
| **Defined benefit pension** | Long | Depends on funded status and workforce maturity | Low, and **predictable** | Liability-driven; the **employer bears the risk** |
| **Defined contribution plan** | Long (the member's) | Set by the individual member | Low until retirement | The **employee bears the risk**; needs a menu of options |
| **Endowment / foundation** | **Perpetual** | **High** | Low but steady (a spending rule) | Tax-exempt; must preserve **real** value in perpetuity |
| **Bank** | Short | Low | **High** | Manages a spread between assets and liabilities; heavily regulated |
| **Insurance — life** | Long | Low | Low, but actuarially predictable | Long-dated, predictable liabilities |
| **Insurance — property & casualty** | **Short** | Low | **High** — claims are lumpy and unpredictable | Must hold liquid assets against catastrophe risk |
| **Sovereign wealth fund** | Very long | Varies by mandate | Low | Objectives range from stabilisation to savings to development |

> **The two questions that determine everything:** *what is the time horizon* and *what are the
> liabilities?* An endowment with a perpetual horizon and no fixed liabilities can hold illiquid,
> volatile assets. A property and casualty insurer facing unpredictable claims cannot, whatever its
> nominal risk appetite.

### Defined benefit versus defined contribution

| | **Defined benefit (DB)** | **Defined contribution (DC)** |
| --- | --- | --- |
| The promise | A specified **benefit** — typically a formula on final salary and service | A specified **contribution** |
| **Who bears investment risk** | **The employer** | **The employee** |
| Who bears longevity risk | The employer | The employee |
| Employer's balance sheet | Carries the **funded status** (FSA LM8) | Carries only unpaid contributions |
| Investment decisions | Made by trustees/sponsor for the whole plan | Made by the **individual member** from a menu |
| Portability | Poor | **Good** |
| Trend | Declining in most developed markets | Growing |

**Funded status** = fair value of plan assets − present value of the defined benefit obligation
(FSA LM8). It drives the DB plan's risk tolerance: a well-funded plan can de-risk; an underfunded
one faces pressure to take more risk to close the gap — which is precisely when it can least afford
to.

### The asset management industry

**Two axes** define a manager:

| | |
| --- | --- |
| **Traditional vs. alternative** | Long-only equities and bonds vs. hedge funds, private equity, real assets |
| **Active vs. passive** | Seeking to beat a benchmark vs. tracking one |

**Structural trends worth knowing:**

- **Fee compression** and the shift from active to passive — driven by the factor-attribution
  evidence (Equity LM12) that much apparent skill is replicable exposure
- **Growth of ETFs**
- **Consolidation** — scale economics in distribution and compliance
- **Robo-advice** and technology-driven distribution
- **ESG integration** (Corporate Issuers LM2)
- **Regulatory burden** rising, particularly on disclosure and suitability

### Pooled investment products

| Vehicle | Pricing | Liquidity | Distinctive features |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Open-end mutual fund** | **NAV**, calculated at the end of the day | Redeem with the fund at NAV | Share count changes with flows; the fund must hold cash for redemptions |
| **Closed-end fund** | **Market price**, which can differ from NAV | Trade on an exchange | Fixed share count; can trade at a **discount or premium** to NAV; no redemption pressure, so it can hold illiquid assets |
| **ETF** | Market price, kept near NAV by **creation/redemption** arbitrage | Intraday on an exchange | Low cost, tax-efficient, transparent; mostly index-tracking |
| **Separately managed account** | Direct ownership of the securities | Depends on the holdings | Customisable; tax management at the individual lot level; high minimums |
| **Hedge fund** | NAV, periodic | **Restricted** — lock-ups, gates, notice periods | Performance fees, leverage, short selling; limited to qualified investors |
| **Private equity fund** | Appraisal-based | **Very low** — 10-year life, capital calls | Committed capital drawn over time; J-curve |

**Mutual fund types:** money market, bond, equity (index or active), balanced, and target-date
(which automatically de-risk as a retirement date approaches).

> **Why closed-end funds can trade away from NAV:** the share count is fixed, so there is no
> redemption mechanism to force convergence. An ETF's **creation/redemption** process lets
> authorised participants arbitrage any gap, which is why ETFs track NAV closely and closed-end
> funds routinely do not. This is a favourite exam distinction.

## Formulas to know cold

```
Diversification ratio = σ(portfolio) / σ(equally weighted average of the single assets)

Funded status = Fair value of plan assets − PV of the defined benefit obligation
  Positive → overfunded (net pension asset);  Negative → underfunded (net pension liability)

PORTFOLIO MANAGEMENT PROCESS
  1. PLANNING   → the Investment Policy Statement
  2. EXECUTION  → asset allocation, security selection, construction
  3. FEEDBACK   → monitor, rebalance, measure, attribute → revise the IPS

DB vs DC — the one fact that decides most questions:
  DEFINED BENEFIT      → the EMPLOYER bears investment and longevity risk
  DEFINED CONTRIBUTION → the EMPLOYEE bears them
```

## Exam traps

> **Trap 1 — Who bears the risk.** **DB → employer. DC → employee.** Asked directly and often.

> **Trap 2 — Judging a security in isolation.** The portfolio approach evaluates the **marginal
> contribution to portfolio risk and return**. A volatile, low-correlation asset can reduce total
> portfolio risk.

> **Trap 3 — Confusing P&C and life insurers.** **Property and casualty** has **short, unpredictable**
> liabilities and needs **high liquidity**. **Life** has long, actuarially predictable liabilities and
> can hold longer, less liquid assets.

> **Trap 4 — Assuming endowments are conservative.** A perpetual horizon and no fixed liabilities
> give endowments **high** risk tolerance and the ability to hold illiquid assets.

> **Trap 5 — Closed-end funds and NAV.** They can trade at a **discount or premium** because the
> share count is fixed. ETFs stay near NAV because of the **creation/redemption** mechanism.

> **Trap 6 — Thinking an underfunded DB plan should take more risk.** It faces pressure to, which is
> exactly the problem — its ability to bear risk has fallen while the temptation has risen.

## Self-check

*Closed book. Commit to an answer before opening the fold.*

**1.** A property and casualty insurer and a university endowment both have large portfolios. Contrast their constraints.

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**P&C insurer:** liabilities are **short-dated, lumpy, and unpredictable** — a hurricane season can produce enormous claims with no warning. It therefore needs **high liquidity**, a **short duration** asset portfolio, and **low risk tolerance**. It is also **taxable** and heavily regulated on the capital it must hold.

**Endowment:** the horizon is **perpetual** and there are no fixed liabilities — only a spending rule, typically 4–5% of a rolling average of assets, which is **smooth and predictable**. It is **tax-exempt**. This gives it **high risk tolerance** and, crucially, the ability to hold **illiquid** assets (private equity, real assets, timber) and to harvest the illiquidity premium.

**The decisive difference is not attitude to risk but the liability structure.** The insurer cannot hold illiquid assets at any risk appetite, because claims arrive on a schedule it does not control. The endowment can, because nobody can demand its capital back.

</details>

**2.** Explain why an underfunded defined benefit plan faces a difficult choice.

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**Funded status = plan assets − the benefit obligation.** An underfunded plan has a shortfall the sponsor must eventually make good, either through contributions or through investment returns.

**The temptation:** take more investment risk, hoping higher returns close the gap without cash contributions from the sponsor.

**Why that is dangerous:** the plan's **ability** to bear risk has **fallen** precisely because it is underfunded. There is no cushion — a further decline in asset values deepens the deficit, increases required contributions at a time the sponsor may be least able to afford them (deficits usually widen in downturns, when the sponsor's business is also weak), and can trigger regulatory funding requirements.

**The mismatch:** willingness to take risk has risen; **ability** has fallen. Good practice resolves it in favour of ability — de-risk and fund the gap with contributions — but the incentive runs the other way, which is why funding regulation exists.

The analyst's angle (FSA LM8): a large net underfunding is economically debt-like and many analysts add it to leverage.

</details>

**3.** Why can a closed-end fund trade at a 15% discount to NAV while an ETF tracking the same assets trades within 0.1%?

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Because of the **arbitrage mechanism**, or its absence.

A **closed-end fund** has a **fixed share count**. There is no way to redeem shares with the fund at NAV, so if sentiment turns against it, sellers can only sell to other investors in the market — and the price can fall well below the value of the underlying assets with nothing to force convergence. (Discounts persisting for years are common; the literature on why is extensive and unresolved.)

An **ETF** has a **creation/redemption** mechanism. Authorised participants can exchange a basket of the underlying securities for new ETF shares, or ETF shares for the underlying basket, in large blocks. If the ETF trades **below** NAV, an AP buys ETF shares, redeems them for the more valuable basket, and pockets the difference — buying pressure that closes the gap. If it trades **above** NAV, the reverse.

That continuous arbitrage is what keeps ETF prices tethered to NAV. Its absence is what frees closed-end fund prices to drift — which is also why closed-end funds can safely hold **illiquid** assets that an ETF could not.

</details>

**4.** List the three steps of the portfolio management process and name the output of each.

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**1. Planning.** Understand the client — return and risk objectives, and the constraints of liquidity, time horizon, tax, legal and regulatory factors, and unique circumstances. **Output: the investment policy statement (IPS).**

**2. Execution.** Set the strategic asset allocation consistent with the IPS, conduct security analysis, and construct the portfolio. **Output: the portfolio itself.**

**3. Feedback.** Monitor the portfolio and the client's circumstances, rebalance to target weights, and measure and attribute performance against the benchmark. **Output: performance reports, rebalancing trades, and revisions to the IPS** when circumstances change.

The process is a **loop**, not a line: the feedback stage feeds back into planning. An IPS written once and never revisited has failed at step 3.

</details>
