## Core concepts

### What risk management is — and is not

> **Risk management is the process of defining the level of risk desired, identifying the level of
> risk currently borne, and taking actions to bring the two into alignment.**

Three things it is **not**:

- It is **not risk minimisation.** An organisation that takes no risk earns no return. The goal is
  to take the **right** risks, in the right size, deliberately.
- It is **not risk elimination.** Some risks must be borne — they are the source of the return.
- It is **not prediction.** It is preparation for a range of outcomes, including ones you did not
  forecast.

The purpose is to **maximise value subject to the risk the organisation is prepared to bear**.

### The risk management framework

| Element | What it covers |
| --- | --- |
| **Risk governance** | The **senior-level** structure: who decides, who oversees, who reports |
| **Risk identification and measurement** | Recognising and quantifying exposures |
| **Risk infrastructure** | The people, systems, data, and models that support the process |
| **Policies and processes** | Day-to-day rules translating governance into action |
| **Risk monitoring, mitigation, and management** | Ongoing oversight and corrective action |
| **Communication** | Reporting to the board, management, regulators, and investors |
| **Strategic risk analysis and integration** | Embedding risk consideration into strategy, not bolting it on |

### Risk governance

**Risk governance is the senior management function of directing risk management activities toward
the organisation's overall goals and values.**

**Elements of effective risk governance:**

- A **clear statement of risk tolerance**, set at board level
- An **enterprise-wide (ERM) view** rather than a set of siloed departmental views
- **Independence** of the risk function from the business units that generate the risk
- **Appropriate reporting lines** — a chief risk officer with direct board access
- **Clear accountability** for each risk
- Regular **review** of the risk appetite as circumstances change

> **Why the enterprise view matters:** risks that look small in isolation can be **correlated** or
> **concentrated** across an organisation. Three business units each with a modest exposure to the
> same counterparty, or to the same interest rate move, together constitute a large one. Siloed risk
> management is structurally blind to exactly the exposures that cause failures.

### Risk tolerance

**Risk tolerance determines which risks to take and how much of each.** It is set by senior
management and the board, and it answers three questions:

1. **What risks is the organisation in business to take?** (Its source of return)
2. **Which risks should it minimise, transfer, or avoid?** (Not its competence, and not compensated)
3. **How much total risk can it bear** before its viability or its objectives are threatened?

Determinants: the ability to bear losses, the expertise available, the regulatory environment, and
stakeholder expectations. Note the parallel with the individual investor's **ability versus
willingness** (LM4) — the same logic applies at organisational scale.

### Risk budgeting

**Risk budgeting allocates the organisation's total risk tolerance across activities, units, or
asset classes** — allocating **risk** rather than only capital.

| Method | Allocation basis |
| --- | --- |
| **By standard deviation / volatility** | Contribution to total portfolio volatility |
| **By beta or factor exposure** | Contribution to systematic risk |
| **By Value at Risk** | Contribution to potential loss at a confidence level |
| **By scenario loss** | Loss under specified stress scenarios |

> **Why it is superior to capital budgeting alone:** two units given the same capital can generate
> completely different amounts of risk. Allocating capital without regard to risk means the
> organisation does not actually control its aggregate exposure. Risk budgeting makes the risk
> allocation explicit and forces units to justify their risk consumption by the return it generates.

### Sources of risk

**Financial risks** — arising from the financial markets:

| Risk | Definition |
| --- | --- |
| **Market risk** | Loss from movements in prices, rates, exchange rates, or volatility |
| **Credit risk** | Loss from a counterparty failing to pay |
| **Liquidity risk** | The risk of being unable to transact at a fair price when needed — a **transaction cost** risk, distinct from funding liquidity |

**Non-financial risks:**

| Risk | Definition |
| --- | --- |
| **Operational** | Failures of people, processes, systems, or external events — including fraud and cyber |
| **Solvency** | Running out of cash to meet obligations |
| **Regulatory / legal / compliance** | Changes in rules, litigation, enforcement |
| **Model risk** | The model is wrong, or is used outside its valid domain |
| **Tail risk** | Extreme events occurring more often than a normal distribution implies (QM LM5 — leptokurtosis) |
| **ESG risk** | Environmental, social, and governance exposures (Corporate Issuers LM2) |
| **Political / settlement / accounting** | Various |

**How risks interact — the crucial point:**

> Risks are **not additive and not independent.** A market decline (market risk) triggers margin
> calls, forcing asset sales into a falling market (liquidity risk), which pressures solvency
> (solvency risk), while counterparties weaken simultaneously (credit risk). Correlations that
> looked comfortable in normal conditions converge toward 1 (QM LM6 — conditional correlations).
>
> **Risks compound in stress.** A framework that measures each risk in isolation systematically
> understates the total, and does so most in exactly the states where it matters.

### Measuring and modifying risk

**Measurement methods:**

| Method | Measures |
| --- | --- |
| **Standard deviation** | Total volatility |
| **Beta** | Systematic risk (equities) |
| **Duration and convexity** | Interest rate sensitivity (Fixed Income LM11–LM13) |
| **Delta, gamma, vega** | Option sensitivities (Derivatives) |
| **Value at Risk (VaR)** | The loss not expected to be exceeded at a stated confidence level over a stated horizon |
| **Conditional VaR (expected shortfall)** | The **expected loss given that VaR is exceeded** — captures tail severity |
| **Stress testing and scenario analysis** | Loss under specified adverse conditions |

> **The standard criticism of VaR:** it tells you a threshold but **says nothing about how bad
> things get beyond it.** A 99% one-day VaR of 10m means losses should exceed 10m on about one day
> in a hundred — it does not say whether that loss is 11m or 500m. **Conditional VaR** answers that
> question and is increasingly preferred for exactly this reason.

**Methods for modifying risk:**

| Method | How it works | Cost |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Avoid** | Do not take the exposure | Forgone return |
| **Prevent / mitigate** | Controls, procedures, diversification | Direct cost, and diversification limits |
| **Transfer** | **Insurance**; surety bonds — pay a premium to move the risk to a third party | The premium; counterparty risk |
| **Shift** | **Derivatives** — change the distribution of outcomes (forwards, futures, swaps, options) | Basis risk, cost, and counterparty risk |
| **Accept / self-insure** | Bear it, with capital held against it | Capital cost |

**Choosing among them** — the factors the LOS asks for:

- **Cost versus benefit** — the cheapest hedge is often not to take the exposure
- Whether the risk is one the organisation is **in business to take** (accept it) or incidental
  (transfer or hedge it)
- **Availability and liquidity** of the hedging instrument
- **Basis risk** — an imperfect hedge can leave substantial residual exposure
- **Counterparty risk** created by the hedge itself
- **Accounting and regulatory** treatment of the hedge
- The organisation's **expertise** in managing the instrument used

## Exam traps

> **Trap 1 — Equating risk management with risk minimisation.** It is **alignment** of risk taken
> with risk desired. An organisation taking no risk earns no return.

> **Trap 2 — Confusing transfer with shift.** **Transfer** moves the risk to a third party via
> **insurance**. **Shift** changes the **distribution** of outcomes via **derivatives**. Different
> mechanisms, different residual risks.

> **Trap 3 — Treating VaR as a worst case.** VaR is a **threshold**, not a maximum. It says nothing
> about losses beyond it. **Conditional VaR / expected shortfall** is what answers that.

> **Trap 4 — Adding risks as if independent.** Risks **interact and compound** in stress, and
> conditional correlations rise toward 1 exactly when it matters.

> **Trap 5 — Siloed risk measurement.** Small exposures in several units can aggregate into a large
> concentrated one. **Enterprise-wide** measurement is the point of ERM.

> **Trap 6 — Risk budgeting as capital budgeting.** Risk budgeting allocates **risk**, not capital.
> Equal capital can produce very unequal risk.

> **Trap 7 — Forgetting a hedge creates new risks.** Derivatives introduce **basis risk** and
> **counterparty risk**; insurance introduces counterparty risk too.

## Self-check

*Closed book. Commit to an answer before opening the fold.*

**1.** Why is risk management not the same as risk minimisation?

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Because **risk is the source of return**. An organisation that eliminates all risk also eliminates its ability to earn anything above the risk-free rate — and for most businesses, that means it has no reason to exist.

Risk management is about **alignment**: determining the level and type of risk the organisation *wants* to bear (its risk tolerance, set by governance), measuring the risk it *currently* bears, and acting to close the gap. The gap can run in either direction — an organisation taking **too little** risk relative to its tolerance is failing at risk management just as much as one taking too much.

The practical framing: identify which risks you are **in business to take** — those are your source of return, and you should take them deliberately and in size — and which are **incidental**, to be hedged, transferred, or avoided because you are not compensated for them.

</details>

**2.** An investment bank's equity desk, credit desk, and FX desk each report modest exposures. Why might the firm still be at serious risk?

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Because the exposures may be **correlated or concentrated** in ways no individual desk can see.

**Concentration:** all three desks may have material exposure to the **same counterparty**. Individually each position looks small; aggregated, the firm has a large single-name exposure that would be unacceptable if anyone had measured it.

**Correlation:** all three may be exposed to the same underlying driver — a rate rise, a credit spread widening, a flight to quality. In normal conditions these desks' P&Ls look uncorrelated; in stress, **conditional correlations converge toward 1** (QM LM6) and the losses arrive together.

**Compounding:** market losses trigger margin calls → forced selling into falling markets (liquidity risk) → pressure on funding (solvency risk) → while counterparties weaken simultaneously (credit risk). Each risk **amplifies** the others.

**The remedy is enterprise risk management** — an aggregated, firm-wide view with an independent risk function reporting directly to the board. Siloed measurement is structurally blind to precisely the exposures that cause failures, which is the lesson of essentially every large financial institution collapse.

</details>

**3.** A fund reports a one-day 99% VaR of 8m. What does this mean, and what does it fail to tell you?

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**What it means:** on about **1 day in 100**, the fund's loss is expected to **exceed 8m**. Equivalently, there is a 99% probability that the daily loss will not exceed 8m.

**What it fails to tell you — the critical omission:** it says **nothing about how large the loss is when the threshold is breached**. The 1-in-100 loss could be 8.5m or 400m; VaR is silent on both cases and treats them identically.

**Additional limitations:**
- It depends heavily on the **estimation method** — historical simulation, parametric (normal), or Monte Carlo can give very different numbers on the same portfolio (QM LM9)
- A **parametric VaR assuming normality understates the tail**, because real returns are **leptokurtic** (QM LM5)
- It is backward-looking if estimated from historical data that excludes a crisis
- It can be **gamed** — a position with a small probability of a catastrophic loss (selling far out-of-the-money options) shows a low VaR

**The remedy:** report **conditional VaR (expected shortfall)** — the expected loss *given* that VaR is exceeded — alongside VaR, and supplement both with **stress testing** against specified scenarios rather than statistical estimates.

</details>

**4.** Distinguish transferring risk from shifting risk, and give an example of each.

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**Transferring risk** moves the exposure to a **third party**, typically through **insurance** or a surety bond. The organisation pays a premium and the counterparty assumes the loss. Example: a company buys property insurance against fire damage — if the building burns, the insurer pays. The risk has left the organisation's balance sheet.

**Shifting risk** changes the **distribution of outcomes** using **derivatives**, without transferring the underlying exposure to anyone in the insurance sense. Example: an airline buys jet fuel futures to lock in a price — it still consumes the fuel and still bears the operational exposure, but the *financial* outcome distribution has been altered. Or it buys call options on fuel, which caps the downside while retaining the benefit if prices fall.

**The practical differences:**
- **Insurance** typically covers a discrete, insurable event and pays on proof of loss; **derivatives** track a market variable continuously
- Derivatives introduce **basis risk** — the hedge instrument may not move exactly with the exposure (hedging airline fuel with crude oil futures leaves refining-margin exposure)
- Both introduce **counterparty risk**, though exchange-traded derivatives mitigate it through central clearing
- Options **shift** the distribution asymmetrically (cap the loss, keep the gain) at the cost of a premium; forwards and futures **remove** the variation in both directions at no upfront cost

</details>
